## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Engineering leadership is mostly the work of managing complexity that code alone does not absorb. On the people side, each step of a management career changes the work itself, from building to coaching to designing organizations, and people fail when they keep doing the job they were promoted out of. On the systems side, every team making its own technology choices produces integration code that no one owns, and that code slows the whole organization over time. The remedy in both cases is deliberate design of the role, or of the platform, around the people who have to live with it.
- **[T:working-line]** Standardization via authority isn't enough. (*Platform Engineering*, with Ian Nowland, 2024, chapter 1.)
- **[F:management-career-path]** The manager's path (popularized): Engineering management as a sequence of distinct roles (mentor, tech lead, people manager, team manager, manager of multiple teams, manager of managers, senior leadership), each with different work and different ways to fail. Codifies a career structure the industry already had, and gives each step a job description.
- **[F:tech-lead-as-role]** Tech lead as a role (extended, with patrick-kua): The tech lead is a set of responsibilities a senior engineer takes on, separate from a rung on the ladder. Extends Patrick Kua's earlier *Talking with Tech Leads*, which treats the tech lead role as distinct from the developer role.
- **[F:manager-as-debugger]** The manager as debugger (original): Teams are complex black boxes interacting with other black boxes. When a team's output is not what anyone expected, a good manager does what a good debugger does: keeps asking why, several layers down, until the box is open.
- **[F:over-general-swamp]** The over-general swamp (co-authored, with ian-nowland): What an organization becomes when every application team picks its own cloud and open source building blocks and writes its own integration code: applications get easier to build and harder to maintain, and the whole organization slows down as it grows.
- **[F:glue]** Glue (co-authored, with ian-nowland): The book's term for integration code, one-off automation, configuration, and management tooling that holds unintegrated building blocks together. Glue gets systems working and makes every later change harder.
- **[F:shadow-platforms]** Shadow platforms (co-authored, with ian-nowland): Duplicate platforms that application teams build for themselves. They raise complexity for the company while lowering it locally, so the goal is to stay aware of them rather than try to stop every one.
- **[F:centralizing-migration-costs]** Centralizing the cost of migrations (co-authored, with ian-nowland): One of the book's four ways platform engineering clears the swamp: the platform team absorbs the cost of upgrades and migrations that would otherwise land separately on every application team.
- **[F:platform-as-product]** Platform as a product (distributed, with camille-fournier, ian-nowland): An internal platform as a curated product that teams choose to adopt, built around its users and defined as much by what it leaves out. The platform definition comes from Evan Bottcher (2018); the product framing runs through the wider platform community, and the book develops it.
- **[F:boring-plans]** Boring plans (extended, with dan-mckinley; derived from choose-boring-technology): Plans that favor predictability and clarity over novelty. Extends Dan McKinley's "Choose Boring Technology" from technology choices to planning.
- **[F:public-engineering-ladder]** The public engineering ladder (popularized): Publishing a company's engineering ladder, with separate management and technical tracks, as a tool for transparency and fairer promotion. Fournier describes her 2015 Rent the Runway ladder as one of the first public ladders to draw that distinction and spell out what each level requires.
- **[L:1]** The advice assumes a large, well-funded organization (structural): Most of Fournier's career ran through Goldman Sachs, Two Sigma, JPMorgan Chase, and CoreWeave, with Rent the Runway as the main exception. Both books assume room for dedicated platform teams, product managers for internal tools, and multiple layers of management. A forty-person company can borrow the vocabulary, but much of the prescription needs headcount it does not have.
- **[L:2]** Management as a ladder has a strong counterposition (structural): *The Manager's Path* follows the management career upward, one role at a time. Charity Majors argues that the best technical leaders move back and forth between engineering and management, and that treating management as a ladder to climb pushes people away from the hands-on work that keeps them good at it. She does not name Fournier, but the two models give different advice to an engineer deciding what to do next.
- **[L:3]** Broad coverage, shallow on each topic (local): Reviewers of *The Manager's Path* often describe it as a strong overview that goes deep on no single topic. The book maps the terrain of each role well; readers facing a hard problem inside one role, such as a difficult reorg or a failing senior hire, will need other sources.
- **[L:4]** The platform vocabulary is new and partly borrowed (local): *Platform Engineering* adopts Evan Bottcher's definition of a platform and works in a field already shaped by *Team Topologies*. Its own terms (glue, the over-general swamp, shadow platforms) appear in a 2024 book and have not yet shown they travel beyond its readers, and glue widens the older programming term "glue code." Treat them as useful names under test.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** The management map holds because the underlying observation is right: each step changes the work, and people struggle when they keep doing the job they left. That observation survives the pendulum argument, which is about movement between roles, and the scale problem, which is about how much structure a company can afford. The platform book earns its place for a different reason. It gives leaders words for costs they already pay and rarely name, which is the first step toward budgeting for them.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:fred-brooks]** antecedent, secondary → Fred Brooks (has a page): *Platform Engineering* uses "accidental complexity," a term from Brooks's 1986 paper, in a chapter 13 heading, "Managing the Accidental Complexity of Human Coordination," and gives it its own meaning: complexity that a platform moves somewhere else instead of removing. The published excerpt does not name Brooks or draw his distinction between essential and accidental complexity.
- **[N:fred-brooks:thesis]** Fred Brooks's thesis: Software is fundamentally a communication problem dressed up as an engineering problem. The cost of building large systems is mostly the cost of coordinating the people building them; that cost grows superlinearly with team size and is bounded below by communication topology, not by individual programmer productivity. Therefore: adding people to a late project makes it later (Brooks's Law). Therefore: conceptual integrity is the single most important property of a system and must be defended by keeping the design in a small number of heads. Therefore: tools and methodologies that target *accidental* complexity (syntax, memory, type errors) can yield big productivity gains; tools and methodologies that promise an order-of-magnitude cut in *essential* complexity (the problem domain itself) are the silver bullets, and Brooks argued that no single development promised one within a decade.
- **[E:out:evan-bottcher]** antecedent, primary → Evan Bottcher: The platform book adopts Bottcher's 2018 definition of a platform, first published on martinfowler.com: self-service APIs, tools, services, knowledge, and support arranged as a compelling internal product.
- **[E:out:patrick-kua]** antecedent, secondary → Patrick Kua: *Talking with Tech Leads* (2014) described the tech lead role as distinct from the developer role, a few years before *The Manager's Path* gave the tech lead its own chapter.
- **[E:out:nicole-forsgren]** parallel, secondary → Nicole Forsgren (has a page): Both work on how engineering organizations perform. Forsgren measures the outcomes of delivery systems; Fournier describes how to organize the teams and platforms that produce them.
- **[N:nicole-forsgren:thesis]** Nicole Forsgren's thesis: Engineering performance is empirically tractable, and the instruments to prove it are real. The intuition that good software organizations differ structurally from bad ones, in kind rather than merely in degree, turns out to be testable, and the testing keeps confirming the claim. Throughput and stability do not trade off in well-run systems; they correlate. Elite performers cluster; the gap between elite and low is not normally distributed. The measurement substrate is operational: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore service. The decomposition is multi-dimensional: satisfaction, performance, activity, communication, efficiency become the SPACE; feedback loops, cognitive load, flow state become DevEx. Each layer is a sharper instrument for the same underlying argument: engineering work is a system, the system has lawful properties, the properties can be measured, and the measurement changes how the work gets organized. The cost of skipping the empirical work is that every measurement argument stays a measurement *argument* instead of a measurement program.
- **[E:out:matthew-skelton-manuel-pais]** parallel, primary → Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais: *Team Topologies* (2019) approaches platforms from team design, with platform teams and cognitive load as core terms. Fournier and Nowland approach the same territory from platform product and operations.
- **[E:out:charity-majors]** parallel, secondary → Charity Majors (has a page): The engineer/manager pendulum, a counterposition to the management ladder. Majors argues for moving back and forth between hands-on and management work instead of climbing.
- **[N:charity-majors:thesis]** Charity Majors's thesis: Complex systems fail in ways their builders did not predict, so the useful question about telemetry is whether it can answer a question formed after the failure began, whatever it collects along the way. Pre-aggregated metrics cannot, because the decision about what to count was made before the question existed. Her remedy is to record one wide, structured event per unit of work, keep its high-cardinality fields intact, and derive dashboards, traces and alerts from that single record at read time. The same instinct drives her other positions: staging environments never match production, so the useful testing happens in production behind guardrails, and management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering, so good engineers should be able to move between the two.
- **[E:out:will-larson]** parallel, primary → Will Larson (has a page): The other track above senior engineer. *The Manager's Path* follows the management sequence one role at a time; Larson catalogued the individual-contributor roles beside it, and spends most of his writing on what someone in them does without a reporting line to act through. Where he goes furthest from this shelf is on authority as a loan from a sponsor, and on what happens to a policy that runs on exceptions.
- **[N:will-larson:thesis]** Will Larson's thesis: Larson's subject is the written machinery of an engineering organization, and the recurring move across the corpus is to name a way one of those pieces decays and then prescribe the repair. A policy that spends significant time granting exceptions has exception debt, and working the exceptions one at a time is what sustains it; the repair is to batch a month of requests and merge the escalations into the next revision. An engineering strategy usually already exists and has simply never been written down, so writing it down solves half the problem (the structure he writes into is Richard Rumelt's diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action, which he credits by name). A team sits in one of four states and the state decides what it needs, in what he calls a loose framework. The staff engineer archetypes came the other way around, out of interviews with people already in the roles rather than from a mechanism. Holding all of it up is his account of authority: the power that comes with a title is loaned by a sponsor, can be retracted, and lasts only while its holder stays aligned with whoever bestowed it (the conferral-and-revocation structure is French and Raven's legitimate power, 1959; the loan framing and the maintenance condition it places on the holder are his).
- **[E:out:uri-bar-joseph]** critiques, secondary → Uri Bar-Joseph: A direct public response (2018) to Fournier's "I hate manager READMEs," arguing that the fault lies in how READMEs get used, and that the document itself can still be worth writing.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:fred-brooks]** For Brooks, software is a communication problem before it is an engineering problem, and the cost of coordinating the people building a system grows faster than the team. Brooks's Law follows directly: adding people to a late project makes it later. No Silver Bullet sharpens the claim by separating essential complexity, which lives in the problem, from accidental complexity, which the tools add. Tooling can remove accidental complexity, sometimes dramatically, while no single development promises an order-of-magnitude gain within a decade. The prediction dates from 1986 and still stands, which is why the vocabulary carries weight in any honest conversation about engineering.
- **[R:nicole-forsgren]** The part of Forsgren that travels furthest is a finding: in well-run delivery systems, throughput and stability rise together. That finding removes the most common argument for slowing change down, the belief that each additional deploy spends safety. DORA's stability measures, change failure rate and time to restore, make the safety question observable: how often a change degrades service, and how fast the team recovers when one does. Because DORA is itself the doctrine here, the four keys belong to the delivery system they were built to measure. Turning them into targets for teams invites the misuse the frame's own limits describe, where people manage to the number until it stops describing the system.
- **[R:charity-majors]** Majors judges telemetry by one test: can an engineer ask a question of a live system that nobody thought to ask while designing it. Pre-aggregated metrics fail that test, because the choice of what to count was made before the question existed. Her answer is one wide structured record per unit of work, with its high-cardinality fields kept whole, so views can be derived from it at read time instead of being fixed at write time. The fields she insists on keeping are the ones that identify a single request: user id, build id, feature flag. The pattern came from Scuba at Facebook, and the company she co-founded sells it. The same instinct runs through her other positions, that staging cannot reproduce production and that management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering.
- **[R:will-larson]** The organization Larson writes about is the one on paper: the ladder, the policy, the strategy. The essays work one decay at a time: name how a written artifact fails, then prescribe the repair. A policy that spends its time granting exceptions carries exception debt, and the repair is to batch a month of requests and rewrite the policy around what the escalations show. Most companies already have an engineering strategy and never wrote it down, so by his account the writing solves half the problem, inside Rumelt's kernel of diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action. The staff engineer archetypes came the other way, out of interviews with people who already held the title, which is what the taxonomy describes and what bounds it. Underneath sits his account of authority: the power a title carries is bestowed by a sponsor, can be retracted, and holds only while its holder stays aligned with whoever bestowed it.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Fournier treats engineering leadership as a series of distinct jobs, and *The Manager's Path* maps them one role at a time, from mentoring to running an organization. Its central advice is that each step changes the work itself, so people struggle when they keep doing the job they were promoted out of. *Platform Engineering*, written with Ian Nowland, turns the same attention to internal systems. Its core diagnosis is the over-general swamp: every team picks its own building blocks and writes its own glue, so applications get easier to build and harder to maintain. The book's answer is a platform curated like a product that teams choose to use, because standards imposed by authority do not hold.

> Security organizations often standardize by authority: a policy names the approved tool, and every team is told to comply. The platform book's mechanism holds in security too: each team writes its own glue to meet the policy, and the security team inherits the swamp. The remedies assume a funded platform team many organizations lack, and the vocabulary is untested in security work. Where that team exists, the question is who pays: when it absorbs the cost of a migration, such as moving every service to a new secrets manager, adoption depends less on each team's spare capacity. An application team that builds its own scanner shows where the supported path fell short, and the book advises staying aware of such shadow platforms, which in security means knowing what each one can reach. A curated security platform earns adoption when it makes the secure path cheaper than the alternative, the same test the book applies to any platform.
