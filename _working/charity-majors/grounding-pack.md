## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Complex systems fail in ways their builders did not predict, so the useful question about telemetry is whether it can answer a question formed after the failure began, whatever it collects along the way. Pre-aggregated metrics cannot, because the decision about what to count was made before the question existed. Her remedy is to record one wide, structured event per unit of work, keep its high-cardinality fields intact, and derive dashboards, traces and alerts from that single record at read time. The same instinct drives her other positions: staging environments never match production, so the useful testing happens in production behind guardrails, and management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering, so good engineers should be able to move between the two.
- **[T:working-line]** Can you understand what is happening inside the system, can you understand any internal state the system may get itself into, simply by asking questions from the outside? ("Observability is a Many-Splendored Definition," charity.wtf, 2020.)
- **[F:observability-as-unknown-unknowns]** Observability as unknown-unknowns (extended, with rudolf-kalman): A system is observable when an engineer can ask any question of it, including one nobody predicted, without shipping new instrumentation first. Her framing keeps the emphasis on questions that were never anticipated, which is where monitoring dashboards run out. The word itself comes from control theory, where Kálmán defined observability as how well internal state can be inferred from external outputs.
- **[F:wide-structured-events]** Arbitrarily wide structured events (extended, with christine-yen): One structured record per unit of work, carrying as many fields as the context requires, kept whole instead of being reduced to counters. The pattern comes from Facebook's Scuba, which Majors used at Parse and credits by name; Honeycomb rebuilt it as a product.
- **[F:high-cardinality]** High cardinality as the point (extended): The fields worth keeping are the ones with many distinct values: user id, build id, feature flag, request id. Those are the fields that identify which request broke, and the ones metrics tooling drops first because they are expensive to index. Cardinality is an older database term, and telemetry stores were already using it for fields to avoid indexing before Honeycomb was founded.
- **[F:unified-storage-versus-pillars]** Unified storage versus the multiple pillars (original): Storing telemetry once and deriving every view from it at read time, against the practice of keeping metrics, logs and traces in separate tools. She first published this as "observability 1.0 versus 2.0." In March 2025 she wrote that she would "mostly avoid" the version labels after vendors objected, and now prefers "the multiple pillars model" and "the unified or consolidated storage model." The pillars framing she argues against is Bourgon's and Sridharan's.
- **[F:test-in-production]** Test in production (popularized, with seth-eliot): Staging environments cannot reproduce production, so the honest response is to build the guardrails that make testing against real traffic safe: feature flags, progressive deployment and instrumentation. The practice and the case for it predate her. In 2009 Seth Eliot was arguing on his Microsoft blog for Testing in Production (TiP), because the environment a service runs in "is impossible to model in the lab," and in 2011 he called it "a good thing," with examples from Facebook, Amazon, Google and Microsoft. Her 2018 talk rests on the same point about staging: trying to mirror it to production is, in her words, "a fool's errand."
- **[F:engineer-manager-pendulum]** The engineer/manager pendulum (original): Management is a change of profession rather than a promotion, and the strongest technical leaders swing between the two roles instead of leaving one behind. In her words, the best frontline engineering managers are never more than two or three years from hands-on work.
- **[L:1]** The prescription is also the product (structural): Majors is CTO and co-founder of Honeycomb, which sells the unified storage architecture her writing recommends, and much of that writing is published on the company's blog. A reader should weigh the argument knowing that the person making it profits from its adoption, including any adoption the extension below argues for. She is direct about the commercial context, including about vendors who adopted her vocabulary without changing their products.
- **[L:2]** Cost and sampling undercut the single record (structural): Laban Eilers's 2025 response grants the appeal and argues the economics break it: the cost gap between metrics and wide events is large enough that teams must sample, sampling makes the record incomplete for forensic work, and logs come back as the fallback. He adds that most infrastructure and third-party services emit only logs and metrics, so the single source of truth is unreachable in practice for much of a real estate.
- **[L:3]** The version labels did not survive contact (local): She published the argument as "observability 1.0 versus 2.0." In March 2025 she wrote that she would "mostly avoid" those labels, because vendors "got really bristly about having '1.0' retroactively applied," which "reads like a subtle diss." Her current phrasing is "the multiple pillars model" and "the unified or consolidated storage model." A page or a practitioner citing the version numbers is citing a framing their author has partly abandoned.
- **[L:4]** It answers what happened, not whether it was allowed (structural): Wide events with high-cardinality fields are built to reconstruct what a system did, when, and to whom. Nothing in the model establishes whether an action was authorized, and her corpus does not address write-path integrity, tamper evidence or retention, which is what a security team needs from a record it may have to defend. Treat her work as evidence for investigation rather than assurance.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** The test at the center survives every limit above, because it is a question about tooling rather than a claim about a product: can this system answer a question formed after the trouble started? Teams that cannot answer yes are choosing, usually without noticing, to be limited to the questions someone predicted during design. Her cardinality point holds for the same reason. The fields that identify which request failed and which customer felt it are the first to be dropped for cost, and they are the ones an investigation needs.

- **[N:camille-fournier:thesis]** Camille Fournier's thesis: Engineering leadership is mostly the work of managing complexity that code alone does not absorb. On the people side, each step of a management career changes the work itself, from building to coaching to designing organizations, and people fail when they keep doing the job they were promoted out of. - **[E:out:john-allspaw]** parallel, primary → John Allspaw (has a page): Two layers of the same incident. Allspaw works on how engineers make sense of a failure in progress; Majors works on whether the telemetry lets them ask the questions that sensemaking requires.
- **[N:john-allspaw:thesis]** John Allspaw's thesis: An incident is not a defect to be eliminated; it is a sample drawn from your system's actual operating envelope, telling you things about how the work is done that no design document, runbook, or simulation can show you. The way to honor that signal is to set up the conditions where engineers will tell you honestly what happened, what they thought was happening, what they almost did, and what they would do differently. That requires removing blame as a default, recognizing the gap between *work-as-imagined* and *work-as-done*, and treating the human side of the system as the primary source of expertise about how the system actually behaves.
- **[E:out:nicole-forsgren]** parallel, primary → Nicole Forsgren (has a page): Both treat instrumentation as the basis of an argument, from opposite ends. Forsgren measures delivery outcomes across organizations through research instruments; Majors instruments a single running system so that any question about it can be asked later.
- **[N:nicole-forsgren:thesis]** Nicole Forsgren's thesis: Engineering performance is empirically tractable, and the instruments to prove it are real. The intuition that good software organizations differ structurally from bad ones, in kind rather than merely in degree, turns out to be testable, and the testing keeps confirming the claim. Throughput and stability do not trade off in well-run systems; they correlate. Elite performers cluster; the gap between elite and low is not normally distributed. The measurement substrate is operational: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore service. The decomposition is multi-dimensional: satisfaction, performance, activity, communication, efficiency become the SPACE; feedback loops, cognitive load, flow state become DevEx. Each layer is a sharper instrument for the same underlying argument: engineering work is a system, the system has lawful properties, the properties can be measured, and the measurement changes how the work gets organized. The cost of skipping the empirical work is that every measurement argument stays a measurement *argument* instead of a measurement program.
- **[E:out:cindy-sridharan]** parallel, primary → Cindy Sridharan: *Distributed Systems Observability* (2018) presented metrics, logs and traces as the three pillars, following Peter Bourgon's 2017 framing. That is the model Majors argues against, which makes Sridharan the position on the other side of her central claim rather than a critic of it.
- **[E:out:rudolf-kalman]** antecedent, secondary → Rudolf E. Kálmán: Defined observability for control theory in 1960 as how well a system's internal state can be inferred from its outputs. Majors carries the term into software telemetry; the word is his.
- **[E:out:laban-eilers]** critiques, secondary → Laban Eilers: "Are we ready for Observability 2.0?" (2025) accepts the goal and disputes the economics: the cost gap forces sampling, sampling breaks forensic completeness, and most infrastructure emits only logs and metrics.
- **[E:out:david-woods]** parallel, secondary → David Woods (has a page): Both write about systems that surprise their operators. Woods names the reserves that absorb surprise; Majors asks whether the instruments can describe a surprise once it arrives.
- **[N:david-woods:thesis]** David Woods's thesis: Complex systems are not stable artifacts; they are continuously adapting joint cognitive systems made up of humans, organizations, and machines. Their survival depends not on being robust to known threats (that property is locally useful but globally fragile), but on having reserves of adaptive capacity that humans supply in real time when surprises arrive. Every optimization push consumes that capacity; performance-pressured systems operate closer to their limits and absorb less surprise. Graceful extensibility (the ability to stretch performance under previously-unimagined conditions) is what you actually want, and it is exactly the property that efficiency-focused management most reliably destroys.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:camille-fournier]** Fournier treats engineering leadership as a series of distinct jobs, and *The Manager's Path* maps them one role at a time, from mentoring to running an organization. Its central advice is that each step changes the work itself, so people struggle when they keep doing the job they were promoted out of. *Platform Engineering*, written with Ian Nowland, turns the same attention to internal systems. Its core diagnosis is the over-general swamp: every team picks its own building blocks and writes its own glue, so applications get easier to build and harder to maintain. The book's answer is a platform curated like a product that teams choose to use, because standards imposed by authority do not hold.
- **[R:john-allspaw]** Allspaw treats an incident as a sample of how a system really operates, carrying information no design document or runbook can supply. Getting that information requires conditions where engineers can say honestly what they saw and decided without career risk, which is the purpose of the blameless postmortem. The analysis then looks above the line, at the mental models and coordination that operate the system, as well as below it at code and configuration. Dark debt names what that analysis keeps finding: failure modes that live in the interactions between components and cannot be found by inspecting any one of them. The frame fails when blameless gets read as consequence-free, which keeps the label and drops the learning discipline.
- **[R:nicole-forsgren]** The part of Forsgren that travels furthest is a finding: in well-run delivery systems, throughput and stability rise together. That finding removes the most common argument for slowing change down, the belief that each additional deploy spends safety. DORA's stability measures, change failure rate and time to restore, make the safety question observable: how often a change degrades service, and how fast the team recovers when one does. Because DORA is itself the doctrine here, the four keys belong to the delivery system they were built to measure. Turning them into targets for teams invites the misuse the frame's own limits describe, where people manage to the number until it stops describing the system.
- **[R:david-woods]** Woods describes complex systems as joint cognitive systems, in which people and automation do the thinking together and failures live in the joint between them. Such systems survive surprises through adaptive capacity: reserves that people and organizational arrangements supply, and that stay invisible until they are needed. The Law of Stretched Systems explains why those reserves shrink: slack looks like inefficiency, so every system gets pushed toward the edge of its envelope, where small disturbances produce large failures. Graceful extensibility, the ability to stretch under conditions nobody designed for, is the property resilience engineering aims at. The original papers are dense, which is why carriers such as Allspaw brought the vocabulary into software.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Majors judges telemetry by one test: can an engineer ask a question of a live system that nobody thought to ask while designing it. Pre-aggregated metrics fail that test, because the choice of what to count was made before the question existed. Her answer is one wide structured record per unit of work, with its high-cardinality fields kept whole, so views can be derived from it at read time instead of being fixed at write time. The fields she insists on keeping are the ones that identify a single request: user id, build id, feature flag. The pattern came from Scuba at Facebook, and the company she co-founded sells it. The same instinct runs through her other positions, that staging cannot reproduce production and that management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering.

> Prompt injection has no architectural fix, so mitigation stays layered and partial, and the incident that gets through arrives in a form nobody wrote a detection for. Her test transfers directly: after an agent does something unexpected, can anyone ask which tool call, which prompt, which user, without shipping new instrumentation first. That argues for one wide record per agent action, carrying the identifiers an investigation needs rather than counts of blocked attempts. Sampling to afford that record removes the rare event an investigation wants, the objection her critic raises about forensics, and agent loops generate more calls than the traffic that critique assumed, so the reach into security work stays unproven. A record built from full prompts and tool payloads also becomes a store of secrets and personal data to defend, and it still says nothing about whether the action was permitted, so this buys investigation and not assurance.
