## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** A pattern language was built to generate, and the mechanism is order. Each pattern states a problem and what Alexander calls a current best guess at what will resolve it, and the 253 in the 1977 book are meant to be worked through in sequence so that what comes out is coherent. His name for the instructions that do this is generative schemes, sets of steps that let a person or a group make a coherent artifact simply. The claim underneath is that coherence cannot be assembled out of parts chosen one at a time, however good each part is alone. Software took the pattern; whether it took the sequence he declined to rule on. At OOPSLA in 1996 he granted the audience the format, in those words, and named three things a pattern language is also for: a moral component, the aim of creating coherence, and generativity. He would not say they were missing here, only that he did not know whether they had been translated into the field. On the mechanism he was exact, quoting his own two books to say that the language is used sequentially and that in practice the instruction dropped out of sight and was not emphasized in use. That last sentence describes the fate of his own languages. It lays no charge against programmers. The charge is this page's inference from the two, and is labelled as one.
- **[T:working-line]** In fact, both A Pattern Language and The Timeless Way Of Building say that the pattern language is to be used sequentially. In practice, however, this feature dropped out of sight, and was not emphasized in use. (OOPSLA keynote, San Jose, October 1996.)
- **[F:pattern-language]** Pattern language (co-authored, with sara-ishikawa, murray-silverstein): A body of patterns that work together as a language, each one stating a problem and a current best guess at its resolution, published in 1977 with Sara Ishikawa and Murray Silverstein and stating of itself that all 253 patterns together form a language. The phrase is older than the book: it is in the title of a 1968 Center for Environmental Structure paper that Alexander's own vitae bylines to the same three authors, against a catalogue record that gives him alone. The antecedent Alexander credits is not a person. He says the language was based on generative schemes that exist in traditional cultures.
- **[F:generative-schemes]** Generative schemes (original): In his own words at OOPSLA in 1996, "sets of instructions which, carried out sequentially, will allow a person or a group of people to create a coherent artifact, beautifully and simply." He traces them to traditional cultures rather than to any named thinker, saying that when he first constructed the pattern language it was based on generative schemes that exist in those cultures. In the same talk he names generativity as one of three things the pattern language was for besides the format, and says he does not know whether those features have yet been translated into software, so the frame's only recorded career is his own account of what it was supposed to do.
- **[F:quality-without-a-name]** The quality without a name (original): The property a place has when it is alive, introduced in *The Timeless Way of Building* (1979), which he approaches by surrounding it with existing concepts that each reflect part of it and none of which defines it alone. This round's search found no use of it outside architecture.
- **[F:living-structure]** Living structure (original): What he says a generative process produces, and the unfinished work he handed the OOPSLA audience: the problem of generating living structure is not being handled well, and the capacity to handle it is theirs. The four volumes of *The Nature of Order* (2002 to 2004) set it out alongside centers and structure-preserving transformations.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:ward-cunningham]** extends, primary → Ward Cunningham (has a page): Cunningham is where the vocabulary crossed into software. The technical report he wrote with Kent Beck in September 1987 proposes setting the design of programs down as a pattern language, and credits the borrowing inside its own proposal sentence, describing concepts adapted from the work of Christopher Alexander, an architect and founder of the Center for Environmental Structures. The programming version of the idea is a frame of its own on his page.
- **[N:ward-cunningham:thesis]** Ward Cunningham's thesis: Cunningham's subject is the distance between a program and what the people who own it currently understand about the problem. Code written to reflect a partial understanding is legitimate and useful in his account, so long as the understanding is allowed to sharpen and the code is rewritten promptly to match. What costs money is the gap left open. Every minute spent working against not-quite-right code is interest on that debt, and an entire engineering organization can be brought to a standstill under the load. The wiki is the same premise applied to prose. A group working out a shared understanding needs a medium cheap enough to revise that people who would not normally author will edit it, which is why he made the editing surface as thin as he could and delegated control to the people using it. Both positions rest on a repair step that somebody has to actually take, and on a community that can be trusted to take it.
- **[E:out:kent-beck]** extends, primary → Kent Beck (has a page): First byline on that 1987 report, writing from Apple while Cunningham was at Tektronix. Its bibliography cites one work of Alexander's and cites it as "Christopher Alexander et. al.," abbreviating the co-authors rather than dropping them. Beck's own later argument is about the cost of changing software, which is a different question from composing a coherent whole, so the pattern language reads as an early borrowing in his corpus rather than the spine of it.
- **[N:kent-beck:thesis]** Kent Beck's thesis: Software costs what changing it costs. Beck states that as Constantine's Equivalence, cost(software) ~= cost(change), and credits the coupling and cohesion work in *Structured Design* for the underlying result. Coupling is then the thing to watch, because it is what makes one change drag others behind it, and design is whatever brings it down. The work happens in tidyings, small changes to structure that leave behavior alone. The two kinds of change are asymmetrical. A structure change can be undone, while a behavior change that has reached customers cannot, which is the reason his practices favor small steps with a test behind each one. Whether to do the structural work first is an investment question rather than a matter of virtue, and his own answer runs both ways: discounted cash flow argues for earning sooner and spending later, options argue for spending now, and the decision is the inequality between them.
- **[E:out:martin-fowler]** extends, primary → Martin Fowler (has a page): Fowler credits Alexander by name for the pattern-language form and keeps the attribution attached. He also has the order claim in his own words: writing in 2006 he sets a language against "merely a catalog of patterns" and says the difference is that each pattern leads you to the next. So the distinction survived the crossing in print. What no source here shows is anyone working a language through in order the way the books ask, which is the gap Alexander described in 1996 as a feature not emphasized in use.
- **[N:martin-fowler:thesis]** Martin Fowler's thesis: Software is a perishable medium for shared understanding. It rots when nobody is tending it, and the cost of letting it rot is paid not in some distant future but in next week's velocity. The way to keep software cheap to change is to evolve practice in public: name the moves, share the vocabulary, run the build-test-deploy loop tight, place the boundaries where change wants to flow, model the domain in the language the team already uses with the customer, and write the practice down as you discover it. Quality IS speed. Naming the implicit is how the argument becomes negotiable. The catalog, the bliki entry, the pattern, the named refactoring, the smell: these are the cartographer's instruments.
- **[E:out:erich-gamma]** extends, secondary → Erich Gamma: *Design Patterns* (1994), with Richard Helm, Ralph Johnson and John Vlissides, is the descendant with the widest reach on the record: more than half a million copies in English and thirteen other languages. Alexander addressed the OOPSLA audience two years after it appeared, and what he granted them there was the format.
- **[E:out:peter-eisenman]** critiques, secondary → Peter Eisenman: "Contrasting Concepts of Harmony in Architecture," their debate at Harvard in November 1982, published in *Lotus International* in 1983 and reprinted in 2000. ## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:ward-cunningham]** Cunningham puts a price on the gap between a program and what its owning team now understands about the problem. Writing code to a partial understanding is legitimate in his account, and the interest is the friction of working against the gap until it is rewritten. The wiki applies the same premise to prose, with an editing surface thin enough that people who would not normally author will revise what is there. What the site takes is the pattern across the vocabulary. Two phrases he coined reached practitioners with the condition stripped: debt as permission to write bad code, the simplest thing as a command. Cunningham's Law carries his name and was never his. He answered the first two on the record, and his own wiki's community posted a warning above the phrase for later arrivals. The correction reached fewer people than the phrase each time. Vocabulary that needs a warning label has stopped belonging to its author.
- **[R:kent-beck]** Beck's subject is the price of the next change, which Constantine's Equivalence states as cost(software) ~= cost(change). Coupling is what makes that price high, so design is whatever brings it down. The work happens in tidyings, small changes to structure that leave behavior alone. The two kinds are asymmetrical: a helper extracted can be inlined again, and a behavior change that reached customers cannot. Small steps with a test behind each one follow from that asymmetry, and he calls the test-first technique a rediscovery whose limits he states himself. His answer to whether to tidy first is an inequality rather than a rule, and it sometimes comes out no. Extreme Programming is the method he wrote up in 1999, out of the way of working a payroll team adopted in 1997. His 3X phases add the axis the others lack, since a practice that fits a product being explored is waste once it is being extracted.
- **[R:martin-fowler]** In Fowler's account, software is a medium for shared understanding, and it decays whenever nobody tends it. The cost of that decay arrives quickly, in next week's velocity, and his design stamina hypothesis makes a parallel claim that internal quality pays back within weeks. His method is to name the moves (refactoring, code smell, strangler fig, bounded context), attribute each to its origin, and revise them in public on the bliki. Naming turns tacit design choices into distinctions a team can argue about. The method has limits of its own: his 2014 microservices article undersold the cost of distribution, and later follow-ups had to correct it.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Sequence is the working part of a pattern language. Each of the 253 patterns in the 1977 book states a problem and a current best guess at its resolution, and in order they are meant to compose into a whole. He calls the instructions that do that generative schemes. At OOPSLA in 1996 he granted software the format. Of his own languages he said in the same talk that the sequential instruction was not emphasized in use. That gap is between a language and a catalogue: the same entries, without the mechanism. The catalogue is what travelled, from the 1987 report through Design Patterns into fields with no buildings. On whether his condition carries across he stopped short, saying he does not know whether the moral component exists in computer science. That leaves the order claim, which any library of patterns can be asked about.

> A software factory ontology puts security constraints inside the spec, which makes the security content an enumeration of objects. Alexander's claim is what an enumeration cannot answer, that a coherent whole comes from applying parts in an order rather than from each part being right alone. Read that way, the missing field on a control object is its dependence: what must hold before it applies, and what it opens next. Auditability then has an order to judge against, since a dated record of what happened asserts no required sequence. Someone has to own the sequence, since one nobody may change is a waterfall and one nobody enforces is a comment. The risk runs two ways: the order hardens into another gate, and a control whose precondition goes unmet never fires at all. Outside his own books the sequential reading survives as an argument about what makes a language a language, and not as a practice anyone was found running.
