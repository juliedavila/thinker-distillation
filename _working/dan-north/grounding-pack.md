## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** The 2006 essay starts from the confusion he kept meeting while teaching test-driven development, and answers it with a change of vocabulary, from tests to behaviour. Test methods become sentences beginning "should," and the same behaviour vocabulary, carried into analysis with Chris Matts, lets a team state a story's acceptance criteria as a context, an event and an outcome before the work starts. The later essays hold related positions, one about planning and one about code. Deliberate Discovery argues that ignorance is "the single greatest impediment to throughput" and that a team should spend effort early finding out what it does not know, including what it does not know it does not know. CUPID, in his phrase "properties over principles," proposes five qualities of code in reply to SOLID. The site reads one thread through all three, that the words a team works in decide what it notices; he does not state it.
- **[T:working-line]** I found the shift from thinking in tests to thinking in behaviour so profound that I started to refer to TDD as BDD. ("Introducing BDD," 2006.)
- **[F:behaviour-driven-development]** Behaviour-driven development (original, with chris-matts; derived from test-driven-development): Test-driven development described as the specification of behaviour: test methods named as sentences about what the code should do, and the same vocabulary carried up into requirements. His share is the name and that reframing. He credits the rest himself, to Chris Stevenson's agiledox tool, a "should" naming convention he came across, Chris Matts on business value and analysis, and Eric Evans's ubiquitous language. Cucumber's history also names Liz Keogh as a pioneer from 2004.
- **[F:given-when-then]** Given/When/Then (co-authored, with chris-matts): A scenario stated as a context, an event and an outcome, written by Terhorst-North and Chris Matts to capture a story's acceptance criteria. It grew partly from the "As a, I want, so that" story template Cucumber credits to Rachel Davies at Connextra. Fowler's 2013 bliki entry reports that Terhorst-North, reviewing it, credited Ivan Moore for "a significant amount of inspiration," and calls the template "a reformulation of the Four-Phase Test pattern." Gherkin now carries the three words as step keywords.
- **[F:deliberate-discovery]** Deliberate Discovery (original): Ignorance is the constraint on a delivery project, so a team should spend effort early on discovering what it does not know, including what it does not know it does not know. Second-order ignorance has an earlier statement: Phillip G. Armour's "The Five Orders of Ignorance" (CACM, 2000) names, as Mark Needham quotes it, a "2nd Order Ignorance," and Terhorst-North's 2010 essay uses "second-order ignorance" without citing him. No source shows whether Terhorst-North read it. The essay credits Liz Keogh for one of its thought experiments.
- **[F:cupid]** CUPID (original): Five properties of code proposed in place of SOLID's five principles: composable, Unix philosophy, predictable, idiomatic and domain-based. It began as a five-minute talk arguing that every SOLID principle was wrong, in which the alternative kept turning out to be "Write simple code." The Unix philosophy property names its own source.
- **[F:deployment-pipeline]** The deployment pipeline (co-authored, with chris-read, dan-north): One automated route from version control to production. It was written up in "The Deployment Production Line" (AGILE 2006) by Jez Humble, Chris Read and Terhorst-North, the third of three authors. Terhorst-North's profile calls it "the build pipeline" and gives the conference title as "Build Production Line"; the published paper is "The Deployment Production Line."
- **[F:blue-green-deployment]** Blue-green deployment (co-authored, with dan-north): Two production environments with traffic switched from one to the other to release. Fowler writes that the technique "has been 'out there' for ages" and that "some foggy combination" of Terhorst-North and Humble came up with the name, so the credit is for the name and it is hedged.
- **[L:1]** His own profile claims more than his essays do (structural): The profile says he "invented Behaviour-Driven Development, co-invented the build pipeline, and pioneered many of the Continuous Delivery practices that software teams now take for granted," and the about page calls him BDD's "originator." - **[L:3]** A passing scenario speaks only for the contexts someone wrote down (structural): Given/When/Then pins behaviour inside the contexts a team named, and a suite of green scenarios is evidence about those contexts and nothing else. His own Deliberate Discovery essay warns that "you are usually ignorant of how ignorant you are." None of the essays behind this page considers a context chosen by someone who wants the software to misbehave, so a scenario suite read as assurance claims more than it tested. Apart from the quoted warnings, this limit is the site's reading of his two frames together.
- **[L:4]** As Needham quotes him, Armour named second-order ignorance a decade before Deliberate Discovery, and the frame's record of use is thin (local): Phillip G. Armour's "The Five Orders of Ignorance" appeared in Communications of the ACM in October 2000, a decade before Terhorst-North's essay, and names, as Mark Needham quotes it, a "2nd Order Ignorance"; the essay uses "second-order ignorance" without citing Armour. Needham came across Armour's paper within five months, in the comments on Terhorst-North's post. Needham is also the one outside practitioner found applying the frame, in 2011. - **[T:still-load-bearing]** The template still gives a team a check on a requirement before it is built, by asking for its context, its event and its outcome. A requirement a team cannot put that way marks the kind of ignorance Deliberate Discovery asks it to go looking for early. The renaming, once Chris Matts heard it as analysis, did the other durable work, carrying a testing practice into conversations about requirements, where Gherkin's step keywords still hold the shape of the template.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:jez-humble]** parallel, primary → Jez Humble (has a page): Humble made the route to production into a discipline. He is the first of three authors on "The Deployment Production Line" (AGILE 2006), Terhorst-North the third, and Humble's own first-person credit names all three; Fowler's hedged credit for the blue-green name pairs the two of them. - **[N:jez-humble:thesis]** Jez Humble's thesis: One route to production, taken by every change, is the claim underneath all of it. The artifact is built once and promoted through each environment, so what reaches production is the thing that was tested. Every stage is automated and every stage can reject the change, which makes the pipeline the release process itself instead of a document describing one. The discipline the 2010 book defines follows from that: keep the software in a releasable state continuously, and the question of when to release stops being an engineering question at all. In his account continuous delivery puts the release schedule in the hands of the business, which only works if any good build can go out on demand. The distinction that makes it possible is that deploying is not releasing. Code can run in production with no user able to reach it, and the two decisions can then be taken by different people, at different times, for different reasons. The principles underneath are borrowed and his own site says so, with Deming on building quality in and Toyota's jidoka and Taiichi Ohno's kaizen behind two of the others.
- **[E:out:martin-fowler]** parallel, primary → Martin Fowler (has a page): Fowler's bliki recorded two of the credits this page leans on for joint work. The 2013 entry on Given/When/Then says it was developed by Terhorst-North and Chris Matts and, in a footnote, reports that Terhorst-North's review comments credited Ivan Moore. The entry on blue-green deployment hedges the name across Terhorst-North and Humble.
- **[N:martin-fowler:thesis]** Martin Fowler's thesis: Software is a perishable medium for shared understanding. It rots when nobody is tending it, and the cost of letting it rot is paid not in some distant future but in next week's velocity. The way to keep software cheap to change is to evolve practice in public: name the moves, share the vocabulary, run the build-test-deploy loop tight, place the boundaries where change wants to flow, model the domain in the language the team already uses with the customer, and write the practice down as you discover it. Quality IS speed. Naming the implicit is how the argument becomes negotiable. The catalog, the bliki entry, the pattern, the named refactoring, the smell: these are the cartographer's instruments.
- **[E:out:nicole-forsgren]** parallel, secondary → Nicole Forsgren (has a page): His 2023 review of McKinsey's developer productivity framework faults its authors for using DORA and SPACE without citing her, the lead researcher on both. The review is a critique of McKinsey, and on the question of her research he takes her side.
- **[N:nicole-forsgren:thesis]** Nicole Forsgren's thesis: Engineering performance is empirically tractable, and the instruments to prove it are real. The intuition that good software organizations differ structurally from bad ones, in kind rather than merely in degree, turns out to be testable, and the testing keeps confirming the claim. Throughput and stability do not trade off in well-run systems; they correlate. Elite performers cluster; the gap between elite and low is not normally distributed. The measurement substrate is operational: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore service. The decomposition is multi-dimensional: satisfaction, performance, activity, communication, efficiency become the SPACE; feedback loops, cognitive load, flow state become DevEx. Each layer is a sharper instrument for the same underlying argument: engineering work is a system, the system has lawful properties, the properties can be measured, and the measurement changes how the work gets organized. The cost of skipping the empirical work is that every measurement argument stays a measurement *argument* instead of a measurement program.
- **[E:out:chris-matts]** parallel, primary → Chris Matts: Co-originator of two frames on this page. A business analyst, he met the behaviour vocabulary with "But that's just like analysis," which turned BDD toward requirements, and he wrote the Given/When/Then template with Terhorst-North.
- **[E:out:robert-c-martin]** critiques, secondary → Robert C. Martin: "Solid Relevance" (2020) answers the 2016 slides that called every SOLID principle wrong. Martin grants that "just write simple code" is good advice and that the slides are "entirely correct" on Liskov substitution. He says they "completely miss the point" of the single responsibility principle, adds that irony, "knowing Dan, is far more likely" than misunderstanding, and holds that simplicity needs principles to guide it. The essay predates CUPID and does not address BDD.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:jez-humble]** The 2006 paper argues that a change should take exactly one route to production, and every other claim here follows from it, the discipline of the 2010 book included. That is what makes the discipline work: software kept releasable at all times, with any good build deployable on demand. One distinction inside the discipline is smaller and separable: deploying code and releasing a feature are two decisions, so code can run in production with nobody able to reach it. Almost none of it is his alone and he is usually the one saying so: Chris Read and Dan North on the pipeline paper, Dave Farley on the book, Deming and Toyota behind the principles. What is his is the writing down: the paper set an existing practice out in an order other people could follow.
- **[R:martin-fowler]** In Fowler's account, software is a medium for shared understanding, and it decays whenever nobody tends it. The cost of that decay arrives quickly, in next week's velocity, and his design stamina hypothesis makes a parallel claim that internal quality pays back within weeks. His method is to name the moves (refactoring, code smell, strangler fig, bounded context), attribute each to its origin, and revise them in public on the bliki. Naming turns tacit design choices into distinctions a team can argue about. The method has limits of its own: his 2014 microservices article undersold the cost of distribution, and later follow-ups had to correct it.
- **[R:nicole-forsgren]** The part of Forsgren that travels furthest is a finding: in well-run delivery systems, throughput and stability rise together. That finding removes the most common argument for slowing change down, the belief that each additional deploy spends safety. DORA's stability measures, change failure rate and time to restore, make the safety question observable: how often a change degrades service, and how fast the team recovers when one does. Because DORA is itself the doctrine here, the four keys belong to the delivery system they were built to measure. Turning them into targets for teams invites the misuse the frame's own limits describe, where people manage to the number until it stops describing the system.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Terhorst-North's part in behaviour-driven development is a change of vocabulary, made while teaching test-driven development: think in behaviour instead of tests. Most pieces came from other people, and his 2006 essay names them: Chris Stevenson's agiledox, a "should" convention he came across, Chris Matts on analysis, Eric Evans's ubiquitous language. With Matts he wrote the template Gherkin now uses as step keywords, which states a story's acceptance criteria as a context, an event and an outcome before the work starts. Deliberate Discovery turns to planning: ignorance is the constraint on delivery, including the ignorance a team does not know it has, so finding it out is work to schedule early. As Mark Needham quotes it, Phillip Armour's 2000 paper had already named a second order of ignorance. CUPID, grown from a 2016 talk against SOLID, answers SOLID with five properties of code. The two signed critiques found answer that talk, and neither addresses CUPID or BDD.

> In a scenario the team picks the Given; against an agent, an attacker writes some of them. Prompt injection stays open in the architecture. Read through Deliberate Discovery, the Givens the team has not named are the constraint: adversarial discovery becomes planned work funded from the start, each finding written as a new Given with the outcome the team will accept. Each Given pins one payload, and a model that passed it once can fail a variant of it or a rerun. The essay front-loads discovery to relieve the constraint; here the constraint never lifts, so a release can gate only on the Givens already found.
