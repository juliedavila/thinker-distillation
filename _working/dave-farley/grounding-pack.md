- **[N:jez-humble:thesis]** Jez Humble's thesis: One route to production, taken by every change, is the claim underneath all of it. The artifact is built once and promoted through each environment, so what reaches production is the thing that was tested. Every stage is automated and every stage can reject the change, which makes the pipeline the release process itself instead of a document describing one. The discipline the 2010 book defines follows from that: keep the software in a releasable state continuously, and the question of when to release stops being an engineering question at all. In his account continuous delivery puts the release schedule in the hands of the business, which only works if any good build can go out on demand. The distinction that makes it possible is that deploying is not releasing. Code can run in production with no user able to reach it, and the two decisions can then be taken by different people, at different times, for different reasons. The principles underneath are borrowed and his own site says so, with Deming on building quality in and Toyota's jidoka and Taiichi Ohno's kaizen behind two of the others.
- **[E:out:martin-fowler]** extends, primary → Martin Fowler (has a page): The 2007 paper presents itself in its subtitle as "Extending the range of Continuous Integration". Fowler wrote the foreword to the 2010 book and wrote up the LMAX architecture in 2011 from a walk-through Martin Thompson and Farley gave him, and his 2004 Window Driver pattern is six years older than the book's window driver section.
- **[N:martin-fowler:thesis]** Martin Fowler's thesis: Software is a perishable medium for shared understanding. It rots when nobody is tending it, and the cost of letting it rot is paid not in some distant future but in next week's velocity. The way to keep software cheap to change is to evolve practice in public: name the moves, share the vocabulary, run the build-test-deploy loop tight, place the boundaries where change wants to flow, model the domain in the language the team already uses with the customer, and write the practice down as you discover it. Quality IS speed. Naming the implicit is how the argument becomes negotiable. The catalog, the bliki entry, the pattern, the named refactoring, the smell: these are the cartographer's instruments.
- **[E:out:nicole-forsgren]** extends, secondary → Nicole Forsgren (has a page): *Modern Software Engineering* uses stability and throughput as its measures, sends readers to *Accelerate* for that model, and gives the finding on change approval boards to "Forsgren and her co-authors". The measures belong to the research; the book puts them to work.
- **[N:nicole-forsgren:thesis]** Nicole Forsgren's thesis: Engineering performance is empirically tractable, and the instruments to prove it are real. The intuition that good software organizations differ structurally from bad ones, in kind rather than merely in degree, turns out to be testable, and the testing keeps confirming the claim. Throughput and stability do not trade off in well-run systems; they correlate. Elite performers cluster; the gap between elite and low is not normally distributed. The measurement substrate is operational: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore service. The decomposition is multi-dimensional: satisfaction, performance, activity, communication, efficiency become the SPACE; feedback loops, cognitive load, flow state become DevEx. Each layer is a sharper instrument for the same underlying argument: engineering work is a system, the system has lawful properties, the properties can be measured, and the measurement changes how the work gets organized. The cost of skipping the empirical work is that every measurement argument stays a measurement *argument* instead of a measurement program.

- **[N:dan-north:thesis]** Daniel Terhorst-North's thesis: The 2006 essay starts from the confusion he kept meeting while teaching test-driven development, and answers it with a change of vocabulary, from tests to behaviour. - **[N:kent-beck:thesis]** Kent Beck's thesis: Software costs what changing it costs. Beck states that as Constantine's Equivalence, cost(software) ~= cost(change), and credits the coupling and cohesion work in *Structured Design* for the underlying result. Coupling is then the thing to watch, because it is what makes one change drag others behind it, and design is whatever brings it down. The work happens in tidyings, small changes to structure that leave behavior alone. The two kinds of change are asymmetrical. A structure change can be undone, while a behavior change that has reached customers cannot, which is the reason his practices favor small steps with a test behind each one. Whether to do the structural work first is an investment question rather than a matter of virtue, and his own answer runs both ways: discounted cash flow argues for earning sooner and spending later, options argue for spending now, and the decision is the inequality between them.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:jez-humble]** The 2006 paper argues that a change should take exactly one route to production, and every other claim here follows from it, the discipline of the 2010 book included. That is what makes the discipline work: software kept releasable at all times, with any good build deployable on demand. One distinction inside the discipline is smaller and separable: deploying code and releasing a feature are two decisions, so code can run in production with nobody able to reach it. Almost none of it is his alone and he is usually the one saying so: Chris Read and Dan North on the pipeline paper, Dave Farley on the book, Deming and Toyota behind the principles. What is his is the writing down: the paper set an existing practice out in an order other people could follow.
- **[R:martin-fowler]** In Fowler's account, software is a medium for shared understanding, and it decays whenever nobody tends it. The cost of that decay arrives quickly, in next week's velocity, and his design stamina hypothesis makes a parallel claim that internal quality pays back within weeks. His method is to name the moves (refactoring, code smell, strangler fig, bounded context), attribute each to its origin, and revise them in public on the bliki. Naming turns tacit design choices into distinctions a team can argue about. The method has limits of its own: his 2014 microservices article undersold the cost of distribution, and later follow-ups had to correct it.
- **[R:nicole-forsgren]** The part of Forsgren that travels furthest is a finding: in well-run delivery systems, throughput and stability rise together. That finding removes the most common argument for slowing change down, the belief that each additional deploy spends safety. DORA's stability measures, change failure rate and time to restore, make the safety question observable: how often a change degrades service, and how fast the team recovers when one does. Because DORA is itself the doctrine here, the four keys belong to the delivery system they were built to measure. Turning them into targets for teams invites the misuse the frame's own limits describe, where people manage to the number until it stops describing the system.
- **[R:dan-north]** Terhorst-North's part in behaviour-driven development is a change of vocabulary, made while teaching test-driven development: think in behaviour instead of tests. Most pieces came from other people, and his 2006 essay names them: Chris Stevenson's agiledox, a "should" convention he came across, Chris Matts on analysis, Eric Evans's ubiquitous language. With Matts he wrote the template Gherkin now uses as step keywords, which states a story's acceptance criteria as a context, an event and an outcome before the work starts. Deliberate Discovery turns to planning: ignorance is the constraint on delivery, including the ignorance a team does not know it has, so finding it out is work to schedule early. As Mark Needham quotes it, Phillip Armour's 2000 paper had already named a second order of ignorance. CUPID, grown from a 2016 talk against SOLID, answers SOLID with five properties of code. The two signed critiques found answer that talk, and neither addresses CUPID or BDD.
- **[R:kent-beck]** Beck's subject is the price of the next change, which Constantine's Equivalence states as cost(software) ~= cost(change). Coupling is what makes that price high, so design is whatever brings it down. The work happens in tidyings, small changes to structure that leave behavior alone. The two kinds are asymmetrical: a helper extracted can be inlined again, and a behavior change that reached customers cannot. Small steps with a test behind each one follow from that asymmetry, and he calls the test-first technique a rediscovery whose limits he states himself. His answer to whether to tidy first is an inequality rather than a rule, and it sometimes comes out no. Extreme Programming is the method he wrote up in 1999, out of the way of working a payroll team adopted in 1997. His 3X phases add the axis the others lack, since a practice that fits a product being explored is waste once it is being extracted.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Two pieces of Farley's work stand fourteen years apart. The 2007 paper sets out the deployment pipeline: a release candidate started at each check-in and carried through a sequence of gates. The idea is older than the paper, and Farley is one of several parents on the record. The 2021 book defines software engineering as an empirical, scientific approach to practical problems, and divides the work into learning and managing complexity. Chris James teaches the layered acceptance test, specifications on top and drivers underneath, and credits it to Farley by name; its driver layer already appears in the 2010 book by Humble and Farley.

> A rule like "a suspended account cannot approve a payment" outlives every interface built for it, so a software factory keeping such rules in the spec needs specs that last as long. The four-layer test has that shape: specifications state behaviour in the problem's terms, and only the drivers know how the system is reached. That holds only while the domain's owners can read the specifications; a rule nobody outside engineering can follow is still code. Chris James explains the split as letting a specification be reused through configurable drivers. The weak point moves into the drivers. A prohibition passes when its driver never reaches the system, or when the attempt fails for some other reason. Each such rule needs a positive control, an active account approving through the same driver, and each refusal traced to the rule; any interface no driver reaches stays unchecked. The security use is the site's inference; the structure was taught for acceptance tests.
