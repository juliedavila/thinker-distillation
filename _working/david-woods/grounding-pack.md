## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Complex systems are not stable artifacts; they are continuously adapting joint cognitive systems made up of humans, organizations, and machines. Their survival depends not on being robust to known threats (that property is locally useful but globally fragile), but on having reserves of adaptive capacity that humans supply in real time when surprises arrive. Every optimization push consumes that capacity; performance-pressured systems operate closer to their limits and absorb less surprise. Graceful extensibility (the ability to stretch performance under previously-unimagined conditions) is what you actually want, and it is exactly the property that efficiency-focused management most reliably destroys.
- **[T:working-line]** Under pressure, systems are pushed to operate at the boundary of their performance envelope, where small perturbations produce large failures. (*The Law of Stretched Systems*.)
- **[F:adaptive-capacity]** Adaptive capacity (extended, with lance-gunderson): A system's reserve ability to absorb surprises that exceed its designed-for operating envelope, supplied by humans, teams, and organizational arrangements that are mostly invisible until they are needed.
- **[F:graceful-extensibility]** Graceful extensibility (original): The system property (distinct from robustness) of being able to *stretch* performance under conditions the system was not explicitly designed for. The actual goal of resilience engineering.
- **[F:law-of-stretched-systems]** The Law of Stretched Systems (original, with jens-rasmussen, larry-hirschhorn): Every system is continuously pushed toward operating at the limit of its capability, because slack reads as inefficiency to management. The closer the system runs to its envelope, the less it can absorb perturbation.
- **[F:joint-cognitive-system]** Joint cognitive system (co-authored, with erik-hollnagel): The framing that complex systems are not machines with humans attached; they are joint cognitive systems where humans and automation together perform the cognitive work. Failures live in the joint, not in either component.
- **[F:new-view-of-human-error]** The new view of human error (distributed, with sidney-dekker, jens-rasmussen): "Human error" is not a cause; it is a *symptom* of deeper system conditions. The diagnosis stops where most postmortems start; the real work is upstream of the operator. Dekker's 2002 paper cites five sources for the view. Woods is an author of two, and the earliest is by Rasmussen and Batstone (1989).
- **[L:1]** The vocabulary is academic-dense (structural): Reading Woods directly is harder than reading any of his industry carriers. The papers are written for the safety-science community first; the resilience-engineering taxonomy gets technical fast. This is partly why Allspaw and others have been so necessary: the substrate doesn't carry without translation.
- **[L:2]** The framework's silence on adversarial actors (structural): Woods's resilience-engineering work is rooted in industries where the disturbances are nature (weather, equipment failure, human slip) or accident (ill-formed procedures, attention lapse), not intent. The framework holds in security but the adversarial layer is bolted on, not built in. Same gap as Komoroske's gardener-vs-builder frame: silent on actors optimizing against you.
- **[L:3]** Empirical grounding is mostly outside software (local): The case studies are aviation, healthcare, nuclear, military. Software-system case studies are mostly downstream (Allspaw, Cook, Maguire). Practitioners new to the frame sometimes assume software systems work like cockpits or ORs; the analogies are useful but not perfect.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** The four concepts of resilience (robustness, rebound, graceful extensibility, sustained adaptability) and the Law of Stretched Systems are foundational enough that you cannot do serious resilience-engineering work in software without internalizing them. Two of those terms, graceful extensibility and the Law of Stretched Systems, are names Woods gave.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:john-allspaw]** extends, primary → John Allspaw (has a page): A carrier into software ops. Allspaw's 2012 Etsy post on blameless postmortems quotes *Behind Human Error* on first and second stories; in 2017 he co-founded Adaptive Capacity Labs with Woods and Richard Cook, and in 2020 the two wrote that people are "the unique source of the adaptive capacity" in incident response.
- **[N:john-allspaw:thesis]** John Allspaw's thesis: An incident is not a defect to be eliminated; it is a sample drawn from your system's actual operating envelope, telling you things about how the work is done that no design document, runbook, or simulation can show you. The way to honor that signal is to set up the conditions where engineers will tell you honestly what happened, what they thought was happening, what they almost did, and what they would do differently. That requires removing blame as a default, recognizing the gap between *work-as-imagined* and *work-as-done*, and treating the human side of the system as the primary source of expertise about how the system actually behaves.
- **[E:out:sidney-dekker]** parallel, primary → Sidney Dekker (has a page): Co-builder of the new-view-of-human-error frame. Dekker is the safety-culture side, Woods is the cognitive-systems side. Different emphases, same lineage; Rasmussen sits behind both.
- **[N:sidney-dekker:thesis]** Sidney Dekker's thesis: Treat human error as a *consequence*, not a cause. The investigator's job is not to determine what someone failed to do, but to reconstruct how the situation made sense to them at the time, because if it made sense, it will make sense again, and the next operator in that position will make the same choice. *Just culture* names the institutional posture that makes this kind of investigation possible. *Drift into failure* names the slow-motion process by which good organizations end up in bad places without anyone making a single obviously wrong decision. *Safety differently* names the prescription: invest in human adaptive capacity, not procedural compliance.
- **[E:out:erik-hollnagel]** parallel, primary → Erik Hollnagel (has a page): Partner on cognitive systems engineering from a 1983 paper through both *Joint Cognitive Systems* books (2005, 2006). The two-view device Hollnagel later used for Safety-I and Safety-II has a precedent he cites in *A Tale of Two Stories* (1998), a paper by Cook, Woods and Miller.
- **[N:erik-hollnagel:thesis]** Erik Hollnagel's thesis: Hollnagel argues that safety has traditionally been defined by what goes wrong, and that this leaves the object of study too small. Failures are rare, and the same adjustments that let work succeed under changing conditions are what occasionally let it fail. So the useful question for safety management is how things usually go right, and an investigation explains the rare failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism. People cannot be as efficient as possible and as thorough as possible at once, the trade between the two is normal, and the shortcuts that make work efficient are the same ones that sometimes let a failure through.
- **[E:out:richard-cook]** parallel, primary → Richard Cook (has a page): Cook condensed the program the two ran together into How Complex Systems Fail (1998), whose propositions were first squeezed onto two pages of card stock, by Allspaw's account.
- **[N:richard-cook:thesis]** Richard Cook's thesis: On Cook's account (a synthesis of the program he shared with Woods, which borrows Reason's language of latent failures), failure in a complex system belongs to the system. Such a system is heavily defended and always runs with flaws present, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together, and the people working in it create its safety continuously. An account that names one root cause, or one person's error, is then a social judgment made with the outcome known, and it does not explain how the failure worked. The explaining is done by the second story, the one about the systemic vulnerabilities the people involved were working inside (the pair set out in his 1998 report with Woods and Miller). His later work takes up efficiency gains that couple distant parts of a system, and the line of representation he presented with Woods, across which operators see the system only through what represents it.
- **[E:out:jens-rasmussen]** antecedent, secondary → Jens Rasmussen (has a page): In his 1997 paper, pressure to save effort and cost very likely moves work toward the boundary of acceptable performance, a movement he calls migration. Woods's 2002 text places the Law of Stretched Systems within a general law of adaptive systems noted by many researchers, and the examples it cites include Rasmussen's 1986 book, Information Processing and Human-Machine Interaction.
- **[N:jens-rasmussen:thesis]** Jens Rasmussen's thesis: Rasmussen read the court reports from Bhopal, Flixborough, Zeebrügge and Chernobyl as showing that those accidents "have not been caused by a coincidence of independent failures and human errors, but by a systematic migration of organisational behaviour toward accident under the influence of pressure toward cost-effectiveness". Task analysis built on action sequences and occasional errors should therefore give way, he argued, to a model of "work system constraints, boundaries of acceptable performance, and subjective criteria guiding adaptation to change", with risk management treated as a control problem across the disciplines that shape the work. Improvement is aimed at the boundary: identify it, make it visible to the people who work near it, and give them the chance to learn to cope with it, on his view that "operation close to known boundaries may be safer than requiring excessive margins". His 1990 paper on causality gives the investigative side: what an analysis accepts as a cause depends on stop rules set by its aim, and errors as "a separate class of behaviour" grow harder to pick out in modern work.
- **[E:out:nicole-forsgren]** parallel, secondary → Nicole Forsgren (has a page): DORA's time to restore measures one operational footprint of the resilience Woods describes structurally.
- **[N:nicole-forsgren:thesis]** Nicole Forsgren's thesis: Engineering performance is empirically tractable, and the instruments to prove it are real. The intuition that good software organizations differ structurally from bad ones, in kind rather than merely in degree, turns out to be testable, and the testing keeps confirming the claim. Throughput and stability do not trade off in well-run systems; they correlate. Elite performers cluster; the gap between elite and low is not normally distributed. The measurement substrate is operational: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore service. The decomposition is multi-dimensional: satisfaction, performance, activity, communication, efficiency become the SPACE; feedback loops, cognitive load, flow state become DevEx. Each layer is a sharper instrument for the same underlying argument: engineering work is a system, the system has lawful properties, the properties can be measured, and the measurement changes how the work gets organized. The cost of skipping the empirical work is that every measurement argument stays a measurement *argument* instead of a measurement program.
- **[E:out:charity-majors]** parallel, secondary → Charity Majors (has a page): Both work on systems that surprise their operators. Woods names the reserves that absorb surprise; Majors asks whether the instruments can describe one after it arrives.
- **[N:charity-majors:thesis]** Charity Majors's thesis: Complex systems fail in ways their builders did not predict, so the useful question about telemetry is whether it can answer a question formed after the failure began, whatever it collects along the way. Pre-aggregated metrics cannot, because the decision about what to count was made before the question existed. Her remedy is to record one wide, structured event per unit of work, keep its high-cardinality fields intact, and derive dashboards, traces and alerts from that single record at read time. The same instinct drives her other positions: staging environments never match production, so the useful testing happens in production behind guardrails, and management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering, so good engineers should be able to move between the two.
- **[E:out:ross-anderson]** parallel, primary → Ross Anderson (has a page): Anderson puts an adversary inside the feedback argument. His 1993 survey of cash machine fraud collected how the losses actually happened and found them concentrated in implementation error and management failure, while the field designed against cryptanalysis. The paper opens by contrasting cryptography with aviation, where accidents are investigated and the findings feed back into practice, and it credits no source for that comparison. He reaches it from banking rather than from resilience engineering.
- **[N:ross-anderson:thesis]** Ross Anderson's thesis: Anderson's argument is that security failures are distributed by incentive more than by technical difficulty, and that the field keeps looking in the wrong place because nothing tells it where the losses are. The 1993 work set the shape. Aviation institutionalised learning from accidents and cryptography did not, and the absence of that feedback let a false threat model stand for years while the real frauds ran through implementation and management. Once the losses are visible, the pattern in them is economic. Where the party who can protect a system is not the party who suffers when it fails, failure should be expected, and better cryptography does not change that arithmetic. The remedy he reached for sits mostly outside the security team. The 2001 paper borrows liability dumping, network externalities, moral hazard and the tragedy of the commons from microeconomics, because the interesting variable is who bears the cost. His work for the British Medical Association made a matching argument about aggregation: the likelihood a resource is abused depends on its value and on the number of people who can reach it, and putting personal information into one large database raises both at once. And where no venue existed for the questions that followed, he convened one.
- **[E:out:kelly-shortridge]** parallel, secondary → Kelly Shortridge (has a page): Shortridge's 2020 primer on resilience in security insists that "Robustness does not equal resilience," close to Woods's point that robustness to known threats is locally useful and globally fragile.
- **[N:kelly-shortridge:thesis]** Kelly Shortridge's thesis: Traditional information security, in Shortridge's account, "presents a poor ROI": its payoff arrives only when an attack is prevented, and attacks are inevitable. Security Obstructionism is her name for the effort that persists anyway, which she attributes to the outputs, control and status it yields inside the organization and to a definition of "secure" vague enough that nobody can check it. Her remedy is to spend where the return survives a successful attack: systems designed to absorb an attack and reorganize, controls built into the design ahead of controls that rely on training and policy, security programs that software engineers and architects build "in parallel," and experiments that test how those controls behave in practice. Her early account of resilience comes from ecology (C.S. Holling, and Walker, Holling, Carpenter and Kinzig, 2004).
- **[E:out:nancy-leveson]** parallel, secondary → Nancy Leveson (has a page): Author of STAMP, which analyses an accident through the control structure that should have prevented it, and an editor, with Hollnagel and Woods, of the 2006 resilience engineering anthology.
- **[N:nancy-leveson:thesis]** Nancy Leveson's thesis: In Leveson's account, safety is an emergent property of a system's interacting components, and reliability is a property of one component measured against its specification. The two come apart in system accidents, where parts that worked as specified combine into a hazardous state. Of one such loss, a reactor example, her 2004 paper says "Merely increasing the reliability of the individual components or protecting against their failure would not have prevented the loss." By the same paper's account, event models and analysis methods "that focus on classic types of failure events will not apply to software." STAMP treats an accident as a failure to enforce a constraint, asks why the control structure was ineffective, and puts prevention into designing a control structure that enforces the necessary constraints across development and operation (a model built, by her own credit, on Rasmussen and Svedung's framework and on Ashby's control concepts).

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:john-allspaw]** Allspaw treats an incident as a sample of how a system really operates, carrying information no design document or runbook can supply. Getting that information requires conditions where engineers can say honestly what they saw and decided without career risk, which is the purpose of the blameless postmortem. The analysis then looks above the line, at the mental models and coordination that operate the system, as well as below it at code and configuration. Dark debt names what that analysis keeps finding: failure modes that live in the interactions between components and cannot be found by inspecting any one of them. The frame fails when blameless gets read as consequence-free, which keeps the label and drops the learning discipline.
- **[R:sidney-dekker]** Dekker's starting point is that human error is a consequence of system conditions, so the investigator's job is to reconstruct how an action made sense to the person at the time. If it made sense once, it will make sense to the next person in the same position. Just culture draws a line between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour, and Dekker's version asks who gets to draw it, since culpability does not inhere in the act itself. Drift into failure explains how organizations reach unsafe states through many small, reasonable choices toward efficiency, none of them obviously wrong. The frame works best when used surgically, because invoking drift for every incident can close off the question of which decisions could have gone differently.
- **[R:erik-hollnagel]** Hollnagel moves the object of safety management from the rare accident to the ordinary working day. Safety-I counts adverse outcomes and aims to minimise them, which confines its evidence to rare events. Safety-II asks how work usually succeeds under varying conditions, and explains the occasional failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism: nobody can be as efficient and as thorough as possible at once, and trading one for the other is normal work. He catalogues the shortcuts that trade produces, among them "Looks fine" and "Has been checked by someone else". In his account, those shortcuts are how the work gets done and also what occasionally lets a failure through. The contrast is disputed at its root: Leveson says the Safety-I he describes bears little resemblance to safety engineering as practised, and Cooper groups Safety-II with other approaches into a New View whose propositions he calls untested.
- **[R:richard-cook]** The eighteen propositions of How Complex Systems Fail run from the nature of hazard, through how blame gets assigned, to safety as something people create. In Cook's account a complex system is heavily defended and always carries flaws, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together. It follows that naming a single root cause after an accident answers a social need to blame and leaves the mechanism of the failure unexplained. The 1998 report he wrote with Woods and Miller gives the alternative: the first story is one of human error, the second one of the systemic vulnerabilities behind it. Going solid, written with Rasmussen in 2005, describes efficiency gains stripping the buffers between distant parts of a hospital until they are tightly coupled. Cook's 2020 statement of the line of representation, a frame set out in the 2017 STELLA report, is written for software operators, who reach the system only through representations.
- **[R:jens-rasmussen]** Rasmussen gives one account of how work moves before an accident and another of how the analysis stops after one. Workers follow an effort gradient, management supplies a cost gradient, and together they very likely produce a systematic migration toward the boundary of acceptable performance. Under that pressure defences in depth are likely to degenerate over time, and in his account the accidents he studied were caused by the migration, with a coincidence of independent failures and human errors ruled out. He aims improvement at the boundary and away from errors: make it visible to the people who work near it, and control performance where work meets it. The analysis afterwards typically ends by stop rules that are rarely stated: the causal trail runs out, a familiar abnormal event explains enough, or a cure is available. Which rule ends it depends on whether the analysis means to explain the events, to allocate responsibility and blame or to improve the system.
- **[R:nicole-forsgren]** The part of Forsgren that travels furthest is a finding: in well-run delivery systems, throughput and stability rise together. That finding removes the most common argument for slowing change down, the belief that each additional deploy spends safety. DORA's stability measures, change failure rate and time to restore, make the safety question observable: how often a change degrades service, and how fast the team recovers when one does. Because DORA is itself the doctrine here, the four keys belong to the delivery system they were built to measure. Turning them into targets for teams invites the misuse the frame's own limits describe, where people manage to the number until it stops describing the system.
- **[R:charity-majors]** Majors judges telemetry by one test: can an engineer ask a question of a live system that nobody thought to ask while designing it. Pre-aggregated metrics fail that test, because the choice of what to count was made before the question existed. Her answer is one wide structured record per unit of work, with its high-cardinality fields kept whole, so views can be derived from it at read time instead of being fixed at write time. The fields she insists on keeping are the ones that identify a single request: user id, build id, feature flag. The pattern came from Scuba at Facebook, and the company she co-founded sells it. The same instinct runs through her other positions, that staging cannot reproduce production and that management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering.
- **[R:ross-anderson]** Anderson's 1993 finding came from collecting how cash machine fraud happened: most of it ran through implementation error and management failure while the field designed against cryptanalysis. The paper blames a missing feedback loop: aviation investigates its accidents and cryptography had no equivalent. Once the losses are visible the pattern is economic, and his rule is that problems may be expected wherever the party able to protect a system is not the one who suffers. That reading began in a conversation with the economist Hal Varian, borrowing moral hazard and network externalities from economics. The phrasing that travelled is "misaligned incentives", a section heading in the 2006 paper with Tyler Moore. The late papers carry his name inside other people's work: second to his student Nicholas Boucher on Trojan Source, fifth of six behind Ilia Shumailov on model collapse. He wrote a standard textbook, and his biographer credits him with catalysing the communities that outlived him.
- **[R:kelly-shortridge]** Shortridge weighs a security program as an investment, and the return that counts is what remains after an attack succeeds. Prevention, she argues, pays only when an attack is stopped, so she moves effort toward "a flexible system that can absorb an attack and reorganize around the threat." Security Obstructionism names where part of the budget goes instead: tools, policies and practices that impede progress "for security's (speculative) sake" and, in her account, persist because they yield outputs, control and status. The Ice Cream Cone, her name for a ranking that closely parallels the system-safety order of precedence, puts hazards designed out at the top and administrative controls "last and least effective." Security chaos engineering, a practice she shares with Aaron Rinehart, tests by experiment whether the controls behave as their builders believe. Her hazard is an opponent, and the premise she states is that "attackers will adapt in response to your mitigations."
- **[R:nancy-leveson]** Leveson asks what a system failed to enforce, and sets STAMP against what the STPA Handbook calls "chain-of-failure-events" models. In STAMP, which she says is built on Rasmussen and Svedung's framework, the basic unit is a constraint, and an accident comes from inadequate control or enforcement of safety-related constraints, even when every component worked as specified. By her 2004 account most software-related accidents had been of this kind. In her view, safety belongs to the interacting system, and reliability to one component judged against its specification. STPA can apply the model from early concept work: draw the control structure, then search for control actions that are unsafe in a particular context. CAST applies it after a loss, asking why the preventive structures did not work, without trying to assign blame. William Young and Leveson extend STPA to security, treating a loss alike whatever the actor intended and deferring the adversary on purpose.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Woods describes complex systems as joint cognitive systems, in which people and automation do the thinking together and failures live in the joint between them. Such systems survive surprises through adaptive capacity: reserves that people and organizational arrangements supply, and that stay invisible until they are needed. The Law of Stretched Systems explains why those reserves shrink: slack looks like inefficiency, so every system gets pushed toward the edge of its envelope, where small disturbances produce large failures. Graceful extensibility, the ability to stretch under conditions nobody designed for, is the property resilience engineering aims at. The original papers are dense, which is why carriers such as Allspaw brought the vocabulary into software.

> The Law of Stretched Systems predicts what happens to security automation gains: the capacity they free up gets spent on more load. A security leader who wants resilience has to defend slack as reserve for surprises, and should expect that defense to be contested for as long as the same pressure reads slack as waste. That reserve is where security behaves as an emergent property: no single control absorbs a novel attack, and what absorbs it is people with the time and context to adapt. A program that lets its automation savings turn into throughput ends up as stretched as before, and the reserve it never rebuilt is the one an attacker eventually finds.
