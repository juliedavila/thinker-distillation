## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Safety has traditionally been defined by what goes wrong, and Hollnagel argues that this leaves the object of study too small. Failures are rare, and the same adjustments that let work succeed under changing conditions are what occasionally let it fail. So the useful question for safety management is how things usually go right, and an investigation explains the rare failure against that background (he names his own precedents for contrasting two views of safety: Dougherty in 1990, and Cook, Woods and Miller in 1998). ETTO supplies the mechanism. People cannot be as efficient as possible and as thorough as possible at once, the trade between the two is normal, and the shortcuts that make work efficient are the same ones that sometimes let a failure through.
- **[T:working-line]** Safety-II is the ability to succeed under varying conditions: look at what goes right, and use it to understand everyday performance. ("A bird's eye view of resilience engineering," lecture slides, 2012.)
- **[F:safety-i-and-safety-ii]** Safety-I and Safety-II (original): Safety-I defines safety as a condition where the number of adverse outcomes is as low as possible, and manages it by finding and removing the causes of what goes wrong. Safety-II defines it as the ability to succeed under varying conditions, and studies everyday work to learn how it usually goes right. The pair is in his lecture slides by 2012 and in print from 2013. He names Dougherty (1990) and Cook, Woods and Miller (1998) as the best-known earlier examples of contrasting two views of safety.
- **[F:efficiency-thoroughness-trade-off]** Efficiency-thoroughness trade-off (ETTO) (original): Nobody can be as efficient as possible and as thorough as possible at once, so people trade one against the other all day, and Hollnagel treats that trade as normal. His lists of ETTO heuristics include "Looks fine", "Will be checked by someone else" and "Has been checked by someone else". Set out in a 2009 book.
- **[F:work-as-imagined-vs-work-as-done]** Work-as-imagined vs. work-as-done (popularized, with ombredane, faverge, jacques-leplat, jean-michel-hoc): The gap between work as procedures, designers and managers imagine it and work as it is actually done. The distinction comes from French ergonomics. Hollnagel traces it to task against activity in Leplat and Hoc (1983); Shorrock traces prescribed against real work to Ombredane and Faverge (1955). He carried the English pair through resilience engineering and safety management. Dekker used it in the field's first anthology, and Steven Shorrock's four varieties of work extend it.
- **[F:joint-cognitive-system]** Joint cognitive system (co-authored, with david-woods): Human and machine studied as one unified system, whose behaviour is what needs explaining. Set out with David Woods in two books written together (2005 and 2006). The two had already co-authored a 1983 paper on cognitive systems engineering.
- **[F:functional-resonance-analysis-method]** Functional Resonance Analysis Method (FRAM) (original): A method for describing work as a set of functions, characterising how each can vary, and tracing how that variability resonates through the couplings between them into an outcome. Its own site dates it to 2004 and 2012.
- **[F:resilience-potentials]** The four resilience potentials and the Resilience Assessment Grid (original): Resilient performance depends on the ability to respond, to monitor, to learn and to anticipate. His Resilience Assessment Grid turns the four into sets of diagnostic questions an organisation can ask of itself (Safety-II in Practice, 2018).
- **[L:1]** Safety-II has no empirical base yet (structural): M. D. Cooper's peer-reviewed critique finds the tenets "a collection of untested propositions" and says "no published, peer-reviewed empirical evidence" shows whether any of them hold. His target is wider than Hollnagel: he folds Safety-II, Safety Differently, resilience engineering and related approaches into one "New View". The charge is still aimed squarely at the frame this page leads with, and a reader adopting Safety-II is adopting a proposal rather than a finding.
- **[L:2]** A co-founder says the Safety-I he rejects misdescribes safety engineering (structural): Nancy Leveson, who co-edited the field's founding anthology with him, argues that the Safety-I he describes "has very little or no resemblance to what is done today or to what has been done in safety engineering for at least 70 years", and proposes a systems approach she calls Safety-III. His reply quotes the goal she sets for Safety-III, to "eliminate, mitigate, or control hazards", and calls it "essentially my definition of Safety-I".
- **[L:3]** ETTO may be a new name for satisficing (local): A 2013 review asks whether ETTO offers new insight or "merely updated terminology from earlier work (most notably, Herbert Simon's 'satisficing')". The same reviewer grants that a name for a practice "we've all seen and used" is "of some value in itself". Read ETTO as a working vocabulary with a known ancestor.
- **[L:4]** The variability Safety-II studies has no hostile intent behind it (structural): This limit is the site's reading, and none of the critics cited here makes it. Most of the domains his publisher lists, such as nuclear power, aviation and healthcare, face hazards that do not study the people managing them. Safety-II treats the variability of everyday work as the source of both success and failure. In security an attacker studies that variability on purpose, and a shortcut such as "has been checked by someone else" becomes a route to aim at. The transfer holds for learning how work succeeds, and it stops at the assumption that nobody hostile is steering the variability.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** Whatever the verdict on Safety-II as a programme for safety management, the question at its center costs little to ask of any team: how does this work usually succeed, and what does that success depend on? ETTO keeps its value for a similar reason. An incident review needs a word for the shortcut that worked a hundred times before the time it failed, and his word lets the review describe the trade without treating the person who made it as the cause.

- **[N:david-woods:thesis]** David Woods's thesis: Complex systems are not stable artifacts; they are continuously adapting joint cognitive systems made up of humans, organizations, and machines. Their survival depends not on being robust to known threats (that property is locally useful but globally fragile), but on having reserves of adaptive capacity that humans supply in real time when surprises arrive. Every optimization push consumes that capacity; performance-pressured systems operate closer to their limits and absorb less surprise. Graceful extensibility (the ability to stretch performance under previously-unimagined conditions) is what you actually want, and it is exactly the property that efficiency-focused management most reliably destroys.
- **[E:out:sidney-dekker]** parallel, primary → Sidney Dekker (has a page): Cooper's 2022 critique treats Safety-II and Dekker's Safety Differently as parts of one "New View", and Hollnagel notes that Dekker mentioned work-as-imagined and work-as-done in his chapter of the field's first anthology.
- **[N:sidney-dekker:thesis]** Sidney Dekker's thesis: Treat human error as a *consequence*, not a cause. The investigator's job is not to determine what someone failed to do, but to reconstruct how the situation made sense to them at the time, because if it made sense, it will make sense again, and the next operator in that position will make the same choice. *Just culture* names the institutional posture that makes this kind of investigation possible. *Drift into failure* names the slow-motion process by which good organizations end up in bad places without anyone making a single obviously wrong decision. *Safety differently* names the prescription: invest in human adaptive capacity, not procedural compliance.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:david-woods]** Woods describes complex systems as joint cognitive systems, in which people and automation do the thinking together and failures live in the joint between them. Such systems survive surprises through adaptive capacity: reserves that people and organizational arrangements supply, and that stay invisible until they are needed. The Law of Stretched Systems explains why those reserves shrink: slack looks like inefficiency, so every system gets pushed toward the edge of its envelope, where small disturbances produce large failures. Graceful extensibility, the ability to stretch under conditions nobody designed for, is the property resilience engineering aims at. The original papers are dense, which is why carriers such as Allspaw brought the vocabulary into software.
- **[R:sidney-dekker]** Dekker's starting point is that human error is a consequence of system conditions, so the investigator's job is to reconstruct how an action made sense to the person at the time. If it made sense once, it will make sense to the next person in the same position. Just culture draws the accountability line: people answer for willful violations, while honest mistakes and system-induced failures become material for learning. Drift into failure explains how organizations reach unsafe states through many small, reasonable choices toward efficiency, none of them obviously wrong. The frame works best when used surgically, because invoking drift for every incident can close off the question of which decisions could have gone differently.
- **[R:john-allspaw]** Allspaw treats an incident as a sample of how a system really operates, carrying information no design document or runbook can supply. Getting that information requires conditions where engineers can say honestly what they saw and decided without career risk, which is the purpose of the blameless postmortem. The analysis then looks above the line, at the mental models and coordination that operate the system, as well as below it at code and configuration. Dark debt names what that analysis keeps finding: failure modes that live in the interactions between components and cannot be found by inspecting any one of them. The frame fails when blameless gets read as consequence-free, which keeps the label and drops the learning discipline.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Hollnagel moves the object of safety management from the rare accident to the ordinary working day. Safety-I counts adverse outcomes and removes their causes, which confines its evidence to rare events. Safety-II asks how work usually succeeds under varying conditions, and explains the occasional failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism: nobody can be as efficient and as thorough as possible at once, and trading one for the other is normal work. He catalogues the shortcuts that trade produces, among them "Looks fine" and "Has been checked by someone else". Those shortcuts are how the work gets done, and they are also what occasionally lets a failure through. The contrast is disputed at its root: Leveson, a co-editor of the field's founding anthology, says the Safety-I he rejects misdescribes safety engineering as practised, and Cooper groups Safety-II with other approaches into a New View whose propositions he calls untested.

> His heuristics "Will be checked by someone else" and "Has been checked by someone else" describe a chain in which each stage relies on a check it assumes another made. Agent pipelines build the same chain: a tool call runs because an upstream step was assumed to validate the request, and that step passed it on because a later one was assumed to check. The result is a confused deputy built from two efficient shortcuts, exercising authority on a judgement no stage made. Safety-II's attention to work that went right says where to look: trace which stage checked what on requests that went through cleanly, and find the paths where both heuristics meet, which look like success until someone aims at them. A further check would face the same trade each time it runs, so the repair belongs in the structure: the tool call carries the originating requester's authority and nothing more.
