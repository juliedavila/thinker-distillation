## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** One argument runs under all of it. Technology work is a system, and its performance is set by how work moves through it, how quickly the news of a problem comes back, and whether the people inside it are free to experiment. The Three Ways state that as a sequence. The First Way is put as the performance of the entire system rather than the performance of a specific silo, and Deming is credited inside it for always seeking to achieve profound understanding of the system. The Second Way is the right-to-left feedback loop, shortened and amplified so corrections can be made continually. The Third Way is a culture that fosters experimentation and learning from failure while treating repetition and practice as the prerequisite to mastery. The later sets come at the same claim from other sides, and both are lists of names before they are arguments. The Five Ideals read as the conditions the work is meant to meet, in an order that starts at locality and simplicity. The three mechanisms of the 2023 book answer a question about where problem solving should happen, and the book's answer is that an organization wins by moving it out of the zones where it is dangerous. The lean lineage is named by both authors in their own prefaces (Steven Spear and H. Kent Bowen's *Harvard Business Review* article decoding the Toyota Production System), and Goldratt's theory of constraints is credited inside the novel's own text.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:jez-humble]** parallel, primary → Jez Humble (has a page): Kim and Humble co-wrote both *The DevOps Handbook* and *Accelerate*, and Humble was the second of the three into the delivery research, brought in by Kim. - **[N:jez-humble:thesis]** Jez Humble's thesis: One route to production, taken by every change, is the claim underneath all of it. The artifact is built once and promoted through each environment, so what reaches production is the thing that was tested. Every stage is automated and every stage can reject the change, which makes the pipeline the release process itself instead of a document describing one. The discipline the 2010 book defines follows from that: keep the software in a releasable state continuously, and the question of when to release stops being an engineering question at all. In his account continuous delivery puts the release schedule in the hands of the business, which only works if any good build can go out on demand. The distinction that makes it possible is that deploying is not releasing. Code can run in production with no user able to reach it, and the two decisions can then be taken by different people, at different times, for different reasons. The principles underneath are borrowed and his own site says so, with Deming on building quality in and Toyota's jidoka and Taiichi Ohno's kaizen behind two of the others.
- **[E:out:nicole-forsgren]** parallel, primary → Nicole Forsgren (has a page): The instrument and the doctrine, out of one programme. Forsgren's research produced the measures and her page holds them. Kim's handbook leans on that research by name, citing the six-year study she led across the 2014 to 2019 State of DevOps Reports. He entered the study in 2012 and she joined for the 2014 report, which is why four of the frames on her page credit him.
- **[N:nicole-forsgren:thesis]** Nicole Forsgren's thesis: Engineering performance is empirically tractable, and the instruments to prove it are real. The intuition that good software organizations differ structurally from bad ones, in kind rather than merely in degree, turns out to be testable, and the testing keeps confirming the claim. Throughput and stability do not trade off in well-run systems; they correlate. Elite performers cluster; the gap between elite and low is not normally distributed. The measurement substrate is operational: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore service. The decomposition is multi-dimensional: satisfaction, performance, activity, communication, efficiency become the SPACE; feedback loops, cognitive load, flow state become DevEx. Each layer is a sharper instrument for the same underlying argument: engineering work is a system, the system has lawful properties, the properties can be measured, and the measurement changes how the work gets organized. The cost of skipping the empirical work is that every measurement argument stays a measurement *argument* instead of a measurement program.
- **[E:out:john-allspaw]** parallel, primary → John Allspaw (has a page): Learning from failure, approached from opposite ends. The Third Way asks an organization to make experiment and failure ordinary; Allspaw studies what happens to people handling a failure nobody chose. Allspaw also wrote the foreword to the first edition of *The DevOps Handbook*, which Kim co-wrote with Jez Humble, Patrick Debois and John Willis.
- **[N:john-allspaw:thesis]** John Allspaw's thesis: An incident is not a defect to be eliminated; it is a sample drawn from your system's actual operating envelope, telling you things about how the work is done that no design document, runbook, or simulation can show you. The way to honor that signal is to set up the conditions where engineers will tell you honestly what happened, what they thought was happening, what they almost did, and what they would do differently. That requires removing blame as a default, recognizing the gap between *work-as-imagined* and *work-as-done*, and treating the human side of the system as the primary source of expertise about how the system actually behaves.
- **[E:out:martin-fowler]** parallel, primary → Martin Fowler (has a page): Fowler wrote one of the two forewords to *Accelerate*, alongside Courtney Kissler's. Same neighbourhood, different register: this corpus arrives as a novel, a handbook and a conference.
- **[N:martin-fowler:thesis]** Martin Fowler's thesis: Software is a perishable medium for shared understanding. It rots when nobody is tending it, and the cost of letting it rot is paid not in some distant future but in next week's velocity. The way to keep software cheap to change is to evolve practice in public: name the moves, share the vocabulary, run the build-test-deploy loop tight, place the boundaries where change wants to flow, model the domain in the language the team already uses with the customer, and write the practice down as you discover it. - **[N:simon-willison:thesis]** Simon Willison's thesis: Prompt injection, in Willison's definition, is what happens when an application concatenates a developer's trusted instruction with untrusted text and the model acts on the combination. He treats that as the reason the defenses built for older injection attacks have no equivalent here: SQL injection has a known mitigation that is guaranteed to work, in escaping and quoting user-provided strings, and no such function exists for natural language. From there he draws a rule about what a fix may be made of. A hole patched with another model cannot be verified, because the model is a black box and an adversary keeps trying until something works, so the direction he trusts is architectural, keeping untrusted content away from anything that holds privilege. His claim about the state of the art is narrower than the rule, and he states it plainly: two and a half years into the problem he had seen alarmingly little progress toward a robust solution, and nobody yet knows how to prevent it 100 percent reliably.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:jez-humble]** The 2006 paper argues that a change should take exactly one route to production, and every other claim here follows from it, the discipline of the 2010 book included. That is what makes the discipline work: software kept releasable at all times, with any good build deployable on demand. One distinction inside the discipline is smaller and separable: deploying code and releasing a feature are two decisions, so code can run in production with nobody able to reach it. Almost none of it is his alone and he is usually the one saying so: Chris Read and Dan North on the pipeline paper, Dave Farley on the book, Deming and Toyota behind the principles. What is his is the writing down: the paper set an existing practice out in an order other people could follow.
- **[R:nicole-forsgren]** The part of Forsgren that travels furthest is a finding: in well-run delivery systems, throughput and stability rise together. That finding removes the most common argument for slowing change down, the belief that each additional deploy spends safety. DORA's stability measures, change failure rate and time to restore, make the safety question observable: how often a change degrades service, and how fast the team recovers when one does. Because DORA is itself the doctrine here, the four keys belong to the delivery system they were built to measure. Turning them into targets for teams invites the misuse the frame's own limits describe, where people manage to the number until it stops describing the system.
- **[R:john-allspaw]** Allspaw treats an incident as a sample of how a system really operates, carrying information no design document or runbook can supply. Getting that information requires conditions where engineers can say honestly what they saw and decided without career risk, which is the purpose of the blameless postmortem. The analysis then looks above the line, at the mental models and coordination that operate the system, as well as below it at code and configuration. Dark debt names what that analysis keeps finding: failure modes that live in the interactions between components and cannot be found by inspecting any one of them. The frame fails when blameless gets read as consequence-free, which keeps the label and drops the learning discipline.
- **[R:martin-fowler]** In Fowler's account, software is a medium for shared understanding, and it decays whenever nobody tends it. The cost of that decay arrives quickly, in next week's velocity, and his design stamina hypothesis makes a parallel claim that internal quality pays back within weeks. His method is to name the moves (refactoring, code smell, strangler fig, bounded context), attribute each to its origin, and revise them in public on the bliki. Naming turns tacit design choices into distinctions a team can argue about. The method has limits of its own: his 2014 microservices article undersold the cost of distribution, and later follow-ups had to correct it.
- **[R:simon-willison]** A single channel carries the developer's trusted instruction and untrusted text, and the model acts on the combination; that join is the problem Willison works. He proposed the name in September 2022, in a post documenting Riley Goodside's demonstration, four months after @himbodhisattva used the phrase and Preamble disclosed it as command injection. Five days later he ruled out defenses built from another model, which cannot be shown to hold against an attacker who retries. His answers are structural: a privileged model holding the tools, kept apart from a quarantined model that reads untrusted content, with ordinary software between them. The lethal trifecta names a configuration: three capabilities each ordinary alone and dangerous together. He polices the term afterwards, ruling that without a concatenation of trusted and untrusted strings it is not prompt injection. Most of the blog is links and quotes carrying other people's work; the authored spine is the twenty-three-post series behind these frames.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Kim's unit is the numbered set, and the earliest of them is published in order. Fast flow of work comes first, then feedback loops shortened and amplified, then a culture where experiment and failure are ordinary and repetition is the prerequisite to mastery. The later sets arrive as named lists, the Five Ideals from the solo book and the post naming them, and three mechanisms with Steven Spear the book says move problem solving out of high-risk zones. Each of the sets comes at one claim, that technology work is a system whose performance is set by flow, feedback and experiment. The distribution is the second contribution: a novel, a summit he has run since 2014, and a handbook crediting the 2012 post for the frame.

> The Third Way asks for a culture where taking risks and learning from failure are ordinary, and where repetition and practice are the prerequisite to mastery. Held against a security organization, that is a claim about which difficulty in the job is the right one, because mastery by repetition requires that the repeated work be the work itself. Where the repeated work is the approval and the sign-off, the mastery that accrues is in coordination. The First Way points at the test rather than the design: judge the whole system's performance, and a control that moves work between departments has to earn its place there. The Second Way asks for feedback shortened and amplified, and the loop that matters here runs from a control failing to anyone hearing of it, which no approval shortens. And the transfer stops at the blast radius, since failure can be made ordinary only where it is recoverable.
