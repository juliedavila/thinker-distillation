## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** On Reason's account, accidents in complex systems come from unsafe acts at the front line triggering latent conditions that earlier decisions by designers and managers put in place (an observation he credited to Barry Turner and Charles Perrow), and defences fail when their weaknesses coincide. On that account, error management that looks only at the person who erred misses where the conditions came from, and in his 1997 words "identifying and eliminating latent conditions proactively still offer the best routes to improving system fitness." The argument extends to culture. A safe culture, in his 1998 argument, is an informed one, which depends on people reporting, which rests on a just culture in which the line between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour is clearly drawn; he rejected the blanket "no blame" alternative as neither feasible nor desirable.
- **[F:swiss-cheese-model]** The Swiss cheese model (original, with john-wreathall, charles-perrow): Defences drawn as a stack of slices, each with holes, so that an accident needs the holes in many layers to line up at once. Reason and the engineer John Wreathall drew the first version as five planes for *Human Error* (1990), and the planes became explicit cheese slices in 1997. By his own co-authored account the "Swiss cheese" label was probably Rob Lee's, then director of the Bureau of Air Safety Investigation in Canberra, and Charles Perrow had already stressed defences in depth.
- **[F:latent-conditions]** Active failures and latent conditions (extended, with barry-turner, charles-perrow, jens-rasmussen): Active failures are unsafe acts by people in direct contact with the system; latent conditions are "resident pathogens" left in the system by earlier decisions. His wording moved from "latent errors" (1987) through "latent failures" (1990) to "latent conditions" (1997), renamed because "a condition is not a cause, but it is necessary for a causal factor to have an impact." His own co-authored history says there was "nothing new" in the observation behind the pair and credits Barry Turner, Charles Perrow, and Jens Rasmussen with Pedersen.
- **[F:just-culture]** Just culture (original, with david-marx, neil-johnston): An organisation learns about its hazards only if people report them, and a reporting culture rests on "a just culture in which the line between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour is clearly drawn and understood." Reason set this out in his 1997 book and a 1998 paper, rejecting a blanket "no blame" amnesty, and inside the argument he credits David Marx for work on where the disciplinary line must be drawn and Neil Johnston for the substitution test.
- **[F:person-and-system-approaches]** The person approach and the system approach (original): His 2000 BMJ paper opens: "The problem of human error can be viewed in 2 ways: the person approach and the system approach." Each, the paper says, has its own model of error causation and its own philosophy of error management.
- **[F:slips-lapses-mistakes-violations]** Slips, lapses, mistakes and violations (extended, with don-norman, klara-mycielska): A classification of unsafe acts: slips are actions gone wrong, lapses are memory failures, mistakes are flawed decisions, and violations break rules. A secondary account credits Don Norman (1981) with first drawing the slip and mistake distinction. The violations work rests on the 1990 Driver Behaviour Questionnaire paper by Reason, Manstead, Stradling, Baxter and Campbell; by the British Academy memoir's account, lapses proved less predictive of accidents than rule violations.
- **[F:generic-error-modelling-system]** GEMS, the Generic Error Modelling System (extended, with jens-rasmussen): Maps error types onto Jens Rasmussen's skill, rule and knowledge levels: skill-based slips, rule-based mistakes and knowledge-based mistakes. A secondary account calls it "an extension of the SRK approach."
- **[L:1]** Reason raised the model's limits himself (structural): In the 1997 book he wrote that "the pendulum may have swung too far" in tracking down contributions "widely separated in both time and place from the events themselves," and at a 2004 workshop at Brétigny he titled a talk "Üeberlingen: Is Swiss cheese past its sell-by date?" The 2006 report he co-wrote gives his definition of a root cause as "the contributing factor that you are working on when the money or the time runs out."
- **[L:2]** Used as a procedure, its critics argue, it moves blame upstream (structural): The cost falls on any team that adopts the model as an investigation procedure. A review required to end at a latent condition can reach one in hindsight even where an active error carried the event, and that error then drops out of the account. The 2006 report Reason co-wrote lists their point as a shift "from a 'blame the pilot' to 'blame the management' culture."
- **[L:3]** Two of its adopters say the holes were never defined (local): Shappell and Wiegmann, who built the HFACS classification on the model, wrote that "the theory never defines what the 'holes in the cheese' really are," as the 2006 report quotes them. A team using the picture still has to decide for itself what counts as a hole.
- **[L:4]** The model was built for accidents; on this site's reading, an attacker lines the holes up on purpose (structural): The BMJ paper has accidents happen when the holes in many layers "momentarily line up to permit a trajectory of accident opportunity." On the site's reading, a defence-in-depth argument borrowed from it needs adjusting for an adversary, who searches for the alignment and can work on several layers at once.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** None of the objections reaches the split itself. An unsafe act by the person at the front line and a condition someone built into the system earlier are different kinds of thing, they call for different fixes, and an investigation that names only the first has stopped early. His critics on this page keep the model. Young, Shorrock and their co-authors dispute how far upstream to take it, a worry he had voiced in 1997, and find his own case studies open to hindsight bias; their own summary aims both points at where and how the model is applied. Just culture holds for a related reason: the line between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour is a question every reporting system has to answer, and he gave it a form that can be argued about.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:richard-cook]** extends, primary → Richard Cook (has a page): Cook and Woods (1994) placed practitioners at "what Reason calls the 'sharp end'" and continued his analogy to a blunt end of their own. - **[N:richard-cook:thesis]** Richard Cook's thesis: On Cook's account (a synthesis of the program he shared with Woods, which borrows Reason's language of latent failures), failure in a complex system belongs to the system. Such a system is heavily defended and always runs with flaws present, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together, and the people working in it create its safety continuously. An account that names one root cause, or one person's error, is then a social judgment made with the outcome known, and it does not explain how the failure worked. The explaining is done by the second story, the one about the systemic vulnerabilities the people involved were working inside (the pair set out in his 1998 report with Woods and Miller). His later work takes up efficiency gains that couple distant parts of a system, and the line of representation he presented with Woods, across which operators see the system only through what represents it.
- **[E:out:sidney-dekker]** extends, primary → Sidney Dekker (has a page): Dekker's *Field Guide* (2002), as the 2006 report quotes it, calls the Swiss cheese analogy useful and then lists what it does not explain.
- **[N:sidney-dekker:thesis]** Sidney Dekker's thesis: Treat human error as a *consequence*, not a cause. The investigator's job is not to determine what someone failed to do, but to reconstruct how the situation made sense to them at the time, because if it made sense, it will make sense again, and the next operator in that position will make the same choice. *Just culture* names the institutional posture that makes this kind of investigation possible. *Drift into failure* names the slow-motion process by which good organizations end up in bad places without anyone making a single obviously wrong decision. *Safety differently* names the prescription: invest in human adaptive capacity, not procedural compliance.
- **[E:out:erik-hollnagel]** parallel, primary → Erik Hollnagel (has a page): Co-author, with Reason and Paries, of the 2006 Eurocontrol report that revisits the Swiss cheese model.
- **[N:erik-hollnagel:thesis]** Erik Hollnagel's thesis: Hollnagel argues that safety has traditionally been defined by what goes wrong, and that this leaves the object of study too small. Failures are rare, and the same adjustments that let work succeed under changing conditions are what occasionally let it fail. So the useful question for safety management is how things usually go right, and an investigation explains the rare failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism. People cannot be as efficient as possible and as thorough as possible at once, the trade between the two is normal, and the shortcuts that make work efficient are the same ones that sometimes let a failure through.
- **[E:out:jens-rasmussen]** antecedent, primary → Jens Rasmussen: Two credits on the record. Reason's co-authored history says the latent-error notion "was implicit in the Rasmussen and Pedersen (1984) discussion" of an accident's anatomy, and GEMS maps error types onto Rasmussen's skill, rule and knowledge levels.
- **[E:out:barry-turner]** antecedent, secondary → Barry Turner: Reason's co-authored history names Turner (1978), with Perrow, among those who had "already given eloquent and persuasive descriptions" of the systemic influences behind accidents. By the British Academy memoir's account, Turner's group suggested he adopt a systemic, organisational approach after a 1987 seminar at Exeter.
- **[E:out:steven-shorrock]** critiques, secondary → Steven Shorrock: Young, Shorrock, Faulkner and Braithwaite (2004), and a shorter 2005 version by Shorrock, Young and Faulkner, argue that investigators took up the model with a "dogmatic insistence" on latent conditions, producing "an illusion of management responsibility for all errors", and find Reason's own case studies open to hindsight bias. They keep the model and object to its use as a prescriptive technique.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:richard-cook]** The eighteen propositions of How Complex Systems Fail run from the nature of hazard, through how blame gets assigned, to safety as something people create. In Cook's account a complex system is heavily defended and always carries flaws, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together. It follows that naming a single root cause after an accident answers a social need to blame and leaves the mechanism of the failure unexplained. The 1998 report he wrote with Woods and Miller gives the alternative: the first story is one of human error, the second one of the systemic vulnerabilities behind it. Going solid, written with Rasmussen in 2005, describes efficiency gains stripping the buffers between distant parts of a hospital until they are tightly coupled. Cook's 2020 statement of the line of representation, a frame set out in the 2017 STELLA report, is written for software operators, who reach the system only through representations.
- **[R:sidney-dekker]** Dekker's starting point is that human error is a consequence of system conditions, so the investigator's job is to reconstruct how an action made sense to the person at the time. If it made sense once, it will make sense to the next person in the same position. Just culture draws the accountability line: people answer for willful violations, while honest mistakes and system-induced failures become material for learning. Drift into failure explains how organizations reach unsafe states through many small, reasonable choices toward efficiency, none of them obviously wrong. The frame works best when used surgically, because invoking drift for every incident can close off the question of which decisions could have gone differently.
- **[R:erik-hollnagel]** Hollnagel moves the object of safety management from the rare accident to the ordinary working day. Safety-I counts adverse outcomes and aims to minimise them, which confines its evidence to rare events. Safety-II asks how work usually succeeds under varying conditions, and explains the occasional failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism: nobody can be as efficient and as thorough as possible at once, and trading one for the other is normal work. He catalogues the shortcuts that trade produces, among them "Looks fine" and "Has been checked by someone else". In his account, those shortcuts are how the work gets done and also what occasionally lets a failure through. The contrast is disputed at its root: Leveson says the Safety-I he describes bears little resemblance to safety engineering as practised, and Cooper groups Safety-II with other approaches into a New View whose propositions he calls untested.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Reason separates active failures, the unsafe acts at a system's front line, from latent conditions, the "resident pathogens" left in place by earlier decisions. His co-authored history calls the observation behind the pair "nothing new", crediting Barry Turner and Charles Perrow. He and John Wreathall drew the account as five planes, which later became slices of Swiss cheese, a label someone else supplied; an accident needs their holes to align. In 1997 latent failures became latent conditions, because "a condition is not a cause, but it is necessary for a causal factor to have an impact." He argued that an organisation learns of its hazards through its people's reports, and that reporting rests on a just culture that separates acceptable from unacceptable behaviour. He questioned the model's use, writing in 1997 that the search for distant contributions may have gone too far and asking in a 2004 talk whether it was past its sell-by date.

> Defence in depth around an AI agent is a stack of partial controls, the arrangement Reason drew as slices. His model was built to explain accidents, which nobody intends. An injected instruction is written to find the alignment, inside the text every filtering layer reads. So those layers can fail together, and counting them overstates the protection. The steadier lever is on the latent side: what the agent could reach before any text arrived. A standing permission, the authority a confused deputy needs, works like his resident pathogen, and the attacker supplies the trigger his account leaves to front-line acts. Removing it narrows what any injection can do, whichever layer it slipped past. That fix carries a hazard close to the one his critics named: once a review finds the permission, it has a latent condition to blame, and the crafted text can drop out of the account.
