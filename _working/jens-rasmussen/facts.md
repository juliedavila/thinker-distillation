## Frame 4: stop rules in accident analysis

- [S:26] Rasmussen, J. (1990). "Human error and the problem of causality in analysis of accidents." *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 327(1241): 449–462. Sole author, Risø National Laboratory. Abstract addresses "the ambiguity of the rules applied for identification of the events to include in analysis and for termination of the search for 'causes'", and concludes "identification of errors as a separate class of behaviour is becoming increasingly difficult in modern work environments." | https://maritimesafetyinnovationlab.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Human-error-and-the-problem-of-causality-in-analysis-of-accidents-Rasmussen-1990.pdf | 2026-10-05
- [S:27] Same paper: "Stop-rules are not usually formulated explicitly. The search will typically be terminated pragmatically in one of the following ways: (a) An event will be accepted as a cause and the search terminated if the causal path can no longer be followed because information is missing; (b) A familiar, abnormal event is found to be a reasonable explanation; or (c) A cure is available." And: "identification of accident causes is controlled by pragmatic, subjective stop-rules. These rules depend on the aim of the analysis, i.e., whether the aim is to explain the course of events, to allocate responsibility and blame, or to identify possible system improvements". It also warns: "There is a tendency to see what you expect to find; during one period, technical faults were in focus as causes of accidents, then human errors predominated while in the future focus will probably move up-stream to designers and managers." | https://maritimesafetyinnovationlab.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Human-error-and-the-problem-of-causality-in-analysis-of-accidents-Rasmussen-1990.pdf | 2026-10-05
- [S:28] Credited premises in the same section: the "stop rule" point is illustrated with "Kuhn (1976)" on "John Dalton's chemical atom theory" (the reference list dates the Kuhn work 1962), and the latent upstream conditions are credited out: "these resident pathogens (Reason, 1989)". Supports: in 1990 Rasmussen credits Reason for "resident pathogens", while Reason's 2006 co-authored history credits Rasmussen and Pedersen (1984) for the latent-error notion ([S:39]). The credit runs both ways between the two. | https://maritimesafetyinnovationlab.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Human-error-and-the-problem-of-causality-in-analysis-of-accidents-Rasmussen-1990.pdf | 2026-10-05
