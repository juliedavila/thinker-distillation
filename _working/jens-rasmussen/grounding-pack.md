## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Rasmussen read the court reports from Bhopal, Flixborough, Zeebrügge and Chernobyl as showing that those accidents "have not been caused by a coincidence of independent failures and human errors, but by a systematic migration of organisational behaviour toward accident under the influence of pressure toward cost-effectiveness". Task analysis built on action sequences and occasional errors should therefore give way, he argued, to a model of "work system constraints, boundaries of acceptable performance, and subjective criteria guiding adaptation to change", with risk management treated as a control problem across the disciplines that shape the work. Improvement is aimed at the boundary: identify it, make it visible to the people who work near it, and give them the chance to learn to cope with it, on his view that "operation close to known boundaries may be safer than requiring excessive margins". His 1990 paper on causality gives the investigative side: what an analysis accepts as a cause depends on stop rules set by its aim, and errors as "a separate class of behaviour" grow harder to pick out in modern work.
- **[T:working-line]** Effort to improve safety should go into how performance is controlled where it meets the boundary, and the control of errors is the wrong target. (Paraphrased from "Risk management in a dynamic society", 1997.)
- **[F:migration-toward-the-boundary]** Migration toward the boundary of acceptable performance (original): Work varies locally like the "Brownian movements" of gas molecules, inside a space bounded by economic failure, unacceptable workload and functionally acceptable performance. The people doing it find "an effort gradient", management supplies "a cost gradient", and the result is "a systematic migration toward the boundary of functionally acceptable performance", where, if the crossing is irreversible, an error or an accident may occur. The 1997 paper is the canonical statement and cites his own 1993 and 1994 work for the claim; Rankin and colleagues date the idea to his 1986 book. Hochstein calls it the dynamic safety model, and later writers describe it as drift; the paper's word is migration.
- **[F:stop-rules]** Stop rules in accident analysis (original): The search for an accident's causes ends by rules that "are not usually formulated explicitly": when the causal path can no longer be followed for missing information, when "a familiar, abnormal event" offers a reasonable explanation, or when "a cure is available." The rules "depend on the aim of the analysis", whether that is to explain the events, "to allocate responsibility and blame, or to identify possible system improvements." He illustrates the point with Kuhn's account of Dalton's atomic theory.
- **[F:skill-rule-knowledge]** Skill-, rule- and knowledge-based behaviour (SRK) (original): Three levels at which people control what they do: a signal-processing, skill-based level; a sign-responding, rule-based level; and a symbolic, problem-solving level. His 1997 retrospective says studies of US Nuclear Regulatory Commission event reports contributed to the model, which aimed to explain their error modes as failures of control at each level. The canonical paper is the 1983 IEEE article, and David Woods dates the framework to a 1979 Risø technical report.
- **[F:abstraction-hierarchy]** The abstraction hierarchy (co-authored, with morten-lind): A technical system described at five levels, from functional purpose through abstract function, generalised functions and physical functions down to physical form. Moving up a level adds the principles, derived from the system's purpose, that govern how the parts below work together. Set out with Morten Lind in a 1981 Risø report, and restated in his 1985 IEEE paper.
- **[F:accimap]** AcciMap (co-authored, with jens-rasmussen): A graphic representation of an accident scenario that maps the structure of the system together with the causation of the accident. The 1997 paper shows AcciMaps in two figures and credits the approach to "Svedung and Rasmussen, 1997"; the method paper is Svedung and Rasmussen (2002).
- **[F:ecological-interface-design]** Ecological interface design (co-authored, with jens-rasmussen): A framework for designing interfaces to complex human-machine systems, built on the skill, rule and knowledge levels and meant to support each of them, set out in Vicente and Rasmussen's 1992 paper on its theoretical foundations.
- **[L:1]** On this site's reading, the model has no adversary in it (structural): The cases behind the 1997 paper are Bhopal, Flixborough, Zeebrügge and Chernobyl, and its remedy rests on boundaries that can be identified and made "visible to the actors". On the site's reading, an attacker moves that boundary and hides where it is, and a security crossing can stay silent long after it happens.
- **[L:2]** Dekker and Pruchnicki explain margin erosion by a mechanism he doubted (local): Their 2013 paper calls widening the margin between normal operation and the safety boundary "another strategy proposed by Rasmussen", and finds it "only partially effective due to risk homeostasis", because "normal operations will likely follow" a boundary moved further away. The 1997 paper lists that approach and already expects "the natural adaptation to the boundary" to "compensate", and its most promising approach "appears to be" identifying boundaries and making them visible. The two differ on the cause. He wrote that risk homeostasis "can be an artefact" of modelling behaviour from accident and error analysis, and held that performance stays near the boundary through perceived "dynamic control characteristics", where Dekker and Pruchnicki describe a system gravitating back "to a certain level of risk acceptance".
- **[L:3]** Leveson finds his influence concentrated at the interface (structural): In her account of his legacy, "his ideas have had the most impact on human factors and cognitive systems engineering and not on the engineering of the hardware and software beyond the interface design." The software uptake belongs to later writers such as Hochstein and Shortridge.
- **[L:4]** Hochstein reads the model as having a single safety boundary (local): In Rasmussen's model "there is only one boundary in the work space related to accidents: the safety boundary", while in Woods's account "the competence envelope is defined by multiple boundaries". Hochstein presents Woods as extending the model; a team using Rasmussen alone watches one accident boundary where its work may have several.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** The mechanism does not depend on the points the limits raise. Whatever a team makes of known boundaries, and whether perceived control or accepted risk keeps performance near the boundary, the account of how work moves still describes something the team can check: local adaptation to an effort gradient and a cost gradient pushes performance one way, and layered defences wear down under the same pressure. The stop rules hold for a similar reason. Every investigation stops somewhere, and asking which of his three rules ended it, and what the analysis was for, is a test a review team can run on its own report.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:david-woods]** extends, primary → David Woods (has a page): Woods's 2002 statement of the Law of Stretched Systems presents it as one case of "a more general law of adaptive systems", citing "Rasmussen, 1986; Hirschhorn, 1997", where the Rasmussen item is his 1986 book. - **[N:david-woods:thesis]** David Woods's thesis: Complex systems are not stable artifacts; they are continuously adapting joint cognitive systems made up of humans, organizations, and machines. Their survival depends not on being robust to known threats (that property is locally useful but globally fragile), but on having reserves of adaptive capacity that humans supply in real time when surprises arrive. Every optimization push consumes that capacity; performance-pressured systems operate closer to their limits and absorb less surprise. Graceful extensibility (the ability to stretch performance under previously-unimagined conditions) is what you actually want, and it is exactly the property that efficiency-focused management most reliably destroys.
- **[E:out:sidney-dekker]** extends, primary → Sidney Dekker (has a page): Dekker and Pruchnicki's 2013 paper on drifting into failure builds its account of operations nudged toward their boundaries on "Rasmussen 1997", and takes from it the prescription to make those boundaries explicit.
- **[N:sidney-dekker:thesis]** Sidney Dekker's thesis: Treat human error as a *consequence*, not a cause. The investigator's job is not to determine what someone failed to do, but to reconstruct how the situation made sense to them at the time, because if it made sense, it will make sense again, and the next operator in that position will make the same choice. *Just culture* names the institutional posture that makes this kind of investigation possible. *Drift into failure* names the slow-motion process by which good organizations end up in bad places without anyone making a single obviously wrong decision. *Safety differently* names the prescription: invest in human adaptive capacity, not procedural compliance.
- **[E:out:james-reason]** parallel, primary → James Reason (has a page): Credit runs in both directions. Rasmussen's 1990 paper attributes "resident pathogens" to Reason (1989), his 1997 retrospective uses "latent errors" with a 1982 citation of his own, and Reason's 2006 co-authored history says the latent-error notion was implicit in a 1984 Rasmussen and Pedersen discussion. Neither side's account settles priority.
- **[N:james-reason:thesis]** James Reason's thesis: On Reason's account, accidents in complex systems come from unsafe acts at the front line triggering latent conditions that earlier decisions by designers and managers put in place (an observation he credited to Barry Turner and Charles Perrow), and defences fail when their weaknesses coincide. On that account, error management that looks only at the person who erred misses where the conditions came from, and in his 1997 words "identifying and eliminating latent conditions proactively still offer the best routes to improving system fitness." The argument extends to culture. A safe culture, in his 1998 argument, is an informed one, which depends on people reporting, which rests on a just culture in which the line between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour is clearly drawn; he rejected the blanket "no blame" alternative as neither feasible nor desirable.
- **[E:out:richard-cook]** extends, primary → Richard Cook (has a page): "Going solid" (2005), by Cook and Rasmussen, uses "Rasmussen's dynamic model of risk and safety" to formulate a model of patient safety dynamics.
- **[N:richard-cook:thesis]** Richard Cook's thesis: On Cook's account (a synthesis of the program he shared with Woods, which borrows Reason's language of latent failures), failure in a complex system belongs to the system. Such a system is heavily defended and always runs with flaws present, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together, and the people working in it create its safety continuously. An account that names one root cause, or one person's error, is then a social judgment made with the outcome known, and it does not explain how the failure worked. The explaining is done by the second story, the one about the systemic vulnerabilities the people involved were working inside (the pair set out in his 1998 report with Woods and Miller). His later work takes up efficiency gains that couple distant parts of a system, and the line of representation he presented with Woods, across which operators see the system only through what represents it.
- **[E:out:erik-hollnagel]** parallel, secondary → Erik Hollnagel (has a page): Rankin, Lundberg, Woltjer, Rollenhagen and Hollnagel (2013) set the migrating effect Rasmussen described in 1986, forces of cost and effectiveness pushing work toward the edge of what is acceptable, beside the law of stretched systems.
- **[N:erik-hollnagel:thesis]** Erik Hollnagel's thesis: Hollnagel argues that safety has traditionally been defined by what goes wrong, and that this leaves the object of study too small. Failures are rare, and the same adjustments that let work succeed under changing conditions are what occasionally let it fail. So the useful question for safety management is how things usually go right, and an investigation explains the rare failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism. People cannot be as efficient as possible and as thorough as possible at once, the trade between the two is normal, and the shortcuts that make work efficient are the same ones that sometimes let a failure through.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:david-woods]** Woods describes complex systems as joint cognitive systems, in which people and automation do the thinking together and failures live in the joint between them. Such systems survive surprises through adaptive capacity: reserves that people and organizational arrangements supply, and that stay invisible until they are needed. The Law of Stretched Systems explains why those reserves shrink: slack looks like inefficiency, so every system gets pushed toward the edge of its envelope, where small disturbances produce large failures. Graceful extensibility, the ability to stretch under conditions nobody designed for, is the property resilience engineering aims at. The original papers are dense, which is why carriers such as Allspaw brought the vocabulary into software.
- **[R:sidney-dekker]** Dekker's starting point is that human error is a consequence of system conditions, so the investigator's job is to reconstruct how an action made sense to the person at the time. If it made sense once, it will make sense to the next person in the same position. Just culture draws the accountability line: people answer for willful violations, while honest mistakes and system-induced failures become material for learning. Drift into failure explains how organizations reach unsafe states through many small, reasonable choices toward efficiency, none of them obviously wrong. The frame works best when used surgically, because invoking drift for every incident can close off the question of which decisions could have gone differently.
- **[R:james-reason]** Reason separates active failures, the unsafe acts at a system's front line, from latent conditions, the "resident pathogens" left in place by earlier decisions. His co-authored history calls the observation behind the pair "nothing new", crediting Barry Turner and Charles Perrow. He and John Wreathall drew the account as five planes, which later became slices of Swiss cheese, a label someone else supplied; an accident needs their holes to align. In 1997 latent failures became latent conditions, because "a condition is not a cause, but it is necessary for a causal factor to have an impact." He argued that an organisation learns of its hazards through its people's reports, and that reporting rests on a just culture that separates acceptable from unacceptable behaviour. He questioned the model's use, writing in 1997 that the search for distant contributions may have gone too far and asking in a 2004 talk whether it was past its sell-by date.
- **[R:richard-cook]** The eighteen propositions of How Complex Systems Fail run from the nature of hazard, through how blame gets assigned, to safety as something people create. In Cook's account a complex system is heavily defended and always carries flaws, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together. It follows that naming a single root cause after an accident answers a social need to blame and leaves the mechanism of the failure unexplained. The 1998 report he wrote with Woods and Miller gives the alternative: the first story is one of human error, the second one of the systemic vulnerabilities behind it. Going solid, written with Rasmussen in 2005, describes efficiency gains stripping the buffers between distant parts of a hospital until they are tightly coupled. Cook's 2020 statement of the line of representation, a frame set out in the 2017 STELLA report, is written for software operators, who reach the system only through representations.
- **[R:erik-hollnagel]** Hollnagel moves the object of safety management from the rare accident to the ordinary working day. Safety-I counts adverse outcomes and aims to minimise them, which confines its evidence to rare events. Safety-II asks how work usually succeeds under varying conditions, and explains the occasional failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism: nobody can be as efficient and as thorough as possible at once, and trading one for the other is normal work. He catalogues the shortcuts that trade produces, among them "Looks fine" and "Has been checked by someone else". In his account, those shortcuts are how the work gets done and also what occasionally lets a failure through. The contrast is disputed at its root: Leveson says the Safety-I he describes bears little resemblance to safety engineering as practised, and Cooper groups Safety-II with other approaches into a New View whose propositions he calls untested.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Rasmussen gives one account of how work moves before an accident and another of how the analysis stops after one. The people doing the work follow an effort gradient, management supplies a cost gradient, and together they produce a systematic migration toward the boundary of acceptable performance. Under that pressure defences in depth degenerate over time, and in his account the accidents he studied were caused by the migration, with a coincidence of independent failures and human errors ruled out. He aims improvement at the boundary and away from errors: make the boundary visible to the people who work near it, and control performance where work meets it. The analysis afterwards ends by stop rules that are rarely stated: the causal trail runs out, a familiar abnormal event explains enough, or a cure is available. Which rule ends it depends on whether the analysis means to explain the events, to allocate responsibility and blame or to improve the system.

> A security review stops by the same unstated rules, and on an attack surface whose exposures multiply one another, his third becomes dangerous: once a cure such as an unapplied patch appears, the review can end. The patch closes one path through the combination and leaves the others open. An attacker changes the mechanism by planting a familiar explanation, such as a known exploit, to trip the second rule. Writing down, before the review starts, its aim, its stop rule and the evidence that would rule out a familiar explanation makes the rules checkable. After a breach, advice on preserving privilege can put the forensic work under counsel, aimed at "litigation preparation" and kept "need-to-know", so a review meant to improve the system can lose the analyses it needs and stop at the first rule. Writing the rules down costs time his cost gradient will press the team to give back. The transfer is this site's argument.
