## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** One route to production, taken by every change, is the claim underneath all of it. The artifact is built once and promoted through each environment, so what reaches production is the thing that was tested. Every stage is automated and every stage can reject the change, which makes the pipeline the release process itself instead of a document describing one. The discipline the 2010 book defines follows from that: keep the software in a releasable state continuously, and the question of when to release stops being an engineering question at all. In his account continuous delivery puts the release schedule in the hands of the business, which only works if any good build can go out on demand. The distinction that makes it possible is that deploying is not releasing. Code can run in production with no user able to reach it, and the two decisions can then be taken by different people, at different times, for different reasons. The principles underneath are borrowed and his own site says so, with Deming on building quality in and Toyota's jidoka and Taiichi Ohno's kaizen behind two of the others.
- **[T:working-line]** Continuous delivery is about putting the release schedule in the hands of the business, not in the hands of IT. ("Continuous Delivery vs Continuous Deployment," continuousdelivery.com, 2010.)
- **[F:deployment-pipeline]** The deployment pipeline (co-authored, with chris-read, dan-north): One automated route from version control to production, in stages, where each stage either rejects the change or passes the same artifact along. The paper that first describes it is by Humble, Chris Read and Dan North, all three then at ThoughtWorks, and Humble credits all three by name in the first person. The paper calls it a deployment production line, and the name that stuck arrived later.
- **[F:continuous-delivery]** Continuous delivery (popularized, with dave-farley): A discipline in which software is built so that it can be released to production at any time, with any good build releasable on demand. The two words are older than the book. The Agile Manifesto asked in its first principle for "early and continuous delivery of valuable software" in 2001, nine years earlier. What the 2010 book with Dave Farley added is the discipline it defines, and the verb for what the book did to the phrase is popularized.
- **[F:release-decoupled-from-deploy]** Release decoupled from deploy (co-authored, with dave-farley): Putting code on a production machine and making a feature available to users are two decisions, separable in time and takeable by different people. The distinction is stated in the 2010 book and restated on his site seventeen days after the book shipped. An earlier public description of the practice is on the record: Ross Harmes wrote up Flickr's feature flags in December 2009, with new code sitting on production servers for months until a single false is changed to true. Nothing connects the two documents, so the record holds an earlier independent description and no lineage.
- **[F:blue-green-deployment]** Blue-green deployment (co-authored, with dan-north): Two production environments, one of them live, with a release performed by routing traffic to the other. Fowler wrote the technique up in 2010 and hedged the credit for the name across "some foggy combination" of Dan North and Jez Humble. The hedge travels with the frame.
- **[F:continuous-delivery-principles]** The five continuous delivery principles (extended, with w-edwards-deming, taiichi-ohno): Build quality in; work in small batches; computers perform repetitive tasks and people solve problems; relentlessly pursue continuous improvement; everyone is responsible. His own principles page credits each one to its source before it credits him, quoting W. Edwards Deming on building quality in and naming Toyota's jidoka and Taiichi Ohno's kaizen for two of the others.
- **[F:dora-four-keys]** The four DORA measures (distributed, with jez-humble, gene-kim): Deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate and time to restore service, from the DORA research programme and *Accelerate*. The research is Nicole Forsgren's and the measures are published on her page here. Humble co-founded DORA with her and Gene Kim and co-wrote the book. The measures read the delivery system the rest of this page describes.
- **[L:1]** The science behind *Accelerate* is contested (structural): Keunwoo Lee's 2022 review argues on four grounds that the research does not support what the book claims for it: the measures are incomplete and sometimes circular, the survey method is open to halo effects and other threats to validity, the statistics are presented loosely, and neither the full survey questions nor the gathered data have been released, so the results cannot be evaluated or replicated. He is courteous about it and says he has tried to present the book sympathetically, which is civility wrapped around a sustained attack on its central claim. The reply, written by Humble with Nicole Forsgren, defends the word choice: the results are inferential, so the authors use "drives", "predicts" and "impacts" and avoid "causes." That is a defence of vocabulary and never a concession.
- **[L:2]** It applies where delivery is the constraint, and the authors say so (structural): Answering Lee, Humble and Forsgren accept his first-principles point in their own words. Effort spent on something that is not a constraint is waste, they write, grounding the reply in the theory of constraints, and if the speed and stability of software delivery are not the constraint on an organization's mission then it should not focus there. They say the book is written for people who do have that constraint. Read the whole corpus with that boundary in place. Rebuilding the route to production is a large programme, and the case for it rests on delivery being the thing that is actually holding the organization back.
- **[L:3]** Almost none of it is his alone (structural): Four books, four sets of co-authors, no solo book. The deployment pipeline is co-authored and he says so himself, naming Dan North and Chris Read. The five principles are credited on his own site to Deming and to Toyota. Fowler's credit for the blue-green name is "some foggy combination" of Dan North and Jez Humble. The DORA measures come out of Nicole Forsgren's research. A sentence that begins "Humble argues" is misattributing on nearly every claim this page carries, and the accurate reading treats him as the person who wrote the practice down with others and kept saying who else was in the room.
- **[L:4]** The route to production is described as machinery and never as a target (structural): The sources behind this page treat the path to production as an engineering problem: automate it, let every stage reject a change, promote one artifact. What they do not treat is the path as a system that has to be defended. Deploy credentials, signing authority and an unattended privileged route into production end up in one place, and nothing in those sources addresses who may authorize a release or what record survives one. The same absence runs through the flag side of the argument. Once releasing is a runtime switch, that switch holds a decision the change process used to hold, and nothing here says whose switch it is. The absence recorded here is an absence in what was checked: the 512-page book was never opened for this page, and every claim here about what it says rests on a publisher's listing and other people's accounts.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** The mechanism survives every limit above, because it is a claim about routes and not a claim about outcomes. If every change reaches production one way, that way can be inspected and what it does can be stated. If changes reach production several ways, no measurement of the delivery system means much, including the measurements this page hands back to Forsgren's. The release-versus-deploy distinction holds for the same reason. It takes a decision that used to be smeared across a change process and makes it a separate, explicit act, and an explicit decision is one that can be refused.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:nicole-forsgren]** parallel, primary → Nicole Forsgren (has a page): The instrument and the machine it reads. Forsgren's research measures how a delivery system performs; the deployment pipeline and the release-versus-deploy split are the machinery being measured. They co-founded DORA with Gene Kim and co-wrote *Accelerate*, and the four measures are published on her page rather than restated on this one.
- **[N:nicole-forsgren:thesis]** Nicole Forsgren's thesis: Engineering performance is empirically tractable, and the instruments to prove it are real. The intuition that good software organizations differ structurally from bad ones, in kind rather than merely in degree, turns out to be testable, and the testing keeps confirming the claim. Throughput and stability do not trade off in well-run systems; they correlate. Elite performers cluster; the gap between elite and low is not normally distributed. The measurement substrate is operational: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore service. The decomposition is multi-dimensional: satisfaction, performance, activity, communication, efficiency become the SPACE; feedback loops, cognitive load, flow state become DevEx. Each layer is a sharper instrument for the same underlying argument: engineering work is a system, the system has lawful properties, the properties can be measured, and the measurement changes how the work gets organized. The cost of skipping the empirical work is that every measurement argument stays a measurement *argument* instead of a measurement program.
- **[E:out:martin-fowler]** extends, primary → Martin Fowler (has a page): Continuous delivery picks up where continuous integration stops, on the far side of the build. Fowler's article on that practice credits Kent Beck with developing it inside Extreme Programming, and it sends readers to Humble and Farley's book for the broader process that follows. Fowler also published the book in his signature series and wrote the bliki entries for the pipeline and for continuous delivery, acknowledging Humble's detailed help on both.
- **[N:martin-fowler:thesis]** Martin Fowler's thesis: Software is a perishable medium for shared understanding. It rots when nobody is tending it, and the cost of letting it rot is paid not in some distant future but in next week's velocity. The way to keep software cheap to change is to evolve practice in public: name the moves, share the vocabulary, run the build-test-deploy loop tight, place the boundaries where change wants to flow, model the domain in the language the team already uses with the customer, and write the practice down as you discover it. Quality IS speed. Naming the implicit is how the argument becomes negotiable. The catalog, the bliki entry, the pattern, the named refactoring, the smell: these are the cartographer's instruments.
- **[E:out:john-allspaw]** parallel, primary → John Allspaw (has a page): Allspaw wrote the foreword to the first edition of *The DevOps Handbook*, which Humble co-wrote with Gene Kim, Patrick Debois and John Willis. The two sit on either side of the same deploy. Humble designs the route a change takes into production; Allspaw studies what happens to the people when a change already in production goes wrong.
- **[N:john-allspaw:thesis]** John Allspaw's thesis: An incident is not a defect to be eliminated; it is a sample drawn from your system's actual operating envelope, telling you things about how the work is done that no design document, runbook, or simulation can show you. The way to honor that signal is to set up the conditions where engineers will tell you honestly what happened, what they thought was happening, what they almost did, and what they would do differently. That requires removing blame as a default, recognizing the gap between *work-as-imagined* and *work-as-done*, and treating the human side of the system as the primary source of expertise about how the system actually behaves.
- **[E:out:charity-majors]** parallel, primary → Charity Majors (has a page): Releasing separately from deploying is what makes her position workable. Majors argues that staging cannot reproduce production, so the honest testing happens against real traffic behind guardrails, and a feature flag is one of those guardrails. Humble's distinction is the thing a flag exercises.
- **[N:charity-majors:thesis]** Charity Majors's thesis: Complex systems fail in ways their builders did not predict, so the useful question about telemetry is whether it can answer a question formed after the failure began, whatever it collects along the way. Pre-aggregated metrics cannot, because the decision about what to count was made before the question existed. Her remedy is to record one wide, structured event per unit of work, keep its high-cardinality fields intact, and derive dashboards, traces and alerts from that single record at read time. The same instinct drives her other positions: staging environments never match production, so the useful testing happens in production behind guardrails, and management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering, so good engineers should be able to move between the two.
- **[E:out:dave-farley]** parallel, primary → Dave Farley: Co-author of *Continuous Delivery* (2010). The discipline described on this page is the book's, and the book is equally his, including the release-versus-deploy distinction that Humble's own post attributes back to it. ## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:nicole-forsgren]** The part of Forsgren that travels furthest is a finding: in well-run delivery systems, throughput and stability rise together. That finding removes the most common argument for slowing change down, the belief that each additional deploy spends safety. DORA's stability measures, change failure rate and time to restore, make the safety question observable: how often a change degrades service, and how fast the team recovers when one does. Because DORA is itself the doctrine here, the four keys belong to the delivery system they were built to measure. Turning them into targets for teams invites the misuse the frame's own limits describe, where people manage to the number until it stops describing the system.
- **[R:martin-fowler]** In Fowler's account, software is a medium for shared understanding, and it decays whenever nobody tends it. The cost of that decay arrives quickly, in next week's velocity, and his design stamina hypothesis makes a parallel claim that internal quality pays back within weeks. His method is to name the moves (refactoring, code smell, strangler fig, bounded context), attribute each to its origin, and revise them in public on the bliki. Naming turns tacit design choices into distinctions a team can argue about. The method has limits of its own: his 2014 microservices article undersold the cost of distribution, and later follow-ups had to correct it.
- **[R:john-allspaw]** Allspaw treats an incident as a sample of how a system really operates, carrying information no design document or runbook can supply. Getting that information requires conditions where engineers can say honestly what they saw and decided without career risk, which is the purpose of the blameless postmortem. The analysis then looks above the line, at the mental models and coordination that operate the system, as well as below it at code and configuration. Dark debt names what that analysis keeps finding: failure modes that live in the interactions between components and cannot be found by inspecting any one of them. The frame fails when blameless gets read as consequence-free, which keeps the label and drops the learning discipline.
- **[R:charity-majors]** Majors judges telemetry by one test: can an engineer ask a question of a live system that nobody thought to ask while designing it. Pre-aggregated metrics fail that test, because the choice of what to count was made before the question existed. Her answer is one wide structured record per unit of work, with its high-cardinality fields kept whole, so views can be derived from it at read time instead of being fixed at write time. The fields she insists on keeping are the ones that identify a single request: user id, build id, feature flag. The pattern came from Scuba at Facebook, and the company she co-founded sells it. The same instinct runs through her other positions, that staging cannot reproduce production and that management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> The 2006 paper argues that a change should take exactly one route to production, and every other claim here follows from it, the discipline of the 2010 book included. That is what makes the discipline work: software kept releasable at all times, with any good build deployable on demand. One distinction inside the discipline is smaller and separable: deploying code and releasing a feature are two decisions, so code can run in production with nobody able to reach it. Almost none of it is his alone and he is usually the one saying so: Chris Read and Dan North on the pipeline paper, Dave Farley on the book, Deming and Toyota behind the principles. What is his is the writing down: the paper set an existing practice out in an order other people could follow.

> One route to production is the condition under which a security constraint can be a stage that rejects a change instead of a document somebody complies with, which is what auditability across a software factory asks for. The authors fence their own case at organizations where delivery is the constraint. An auditability case argues on different ground. Releasing separately from deploying takes the last decision back out of it. What reaches production is built, tested and promoted on the audited path, while the act that exposes it to users is a runtime switch sitting outside that path, so the flag service inherits an authorization decision the discipline never names as one. Closing that seam takes more than a log. Each switch needs a named party entitled to move it, and a record of who did, which brings the switch inside the factory. Until it closes, the audited path stops at the deploy and the release sits outside it.
