## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** An incident is not a defect to be eliminated; it is a sample drawn from your system's actual operating envelope, telling you things about how the work is done that no design document, runbook, or simulation can show you. The way to honor that signal is to set up the conditions where engineers will tell you honestly what happened, what they thought was happening, what they almost did, and what they would do differently. That requires removing blame as a default, recognizing the gap between *work-as-imagined* and *work-as-done*, and treating the human side of the system as the primary source of expertise about how the system actually behaves.
- **[T:working-line]** "Having a Just Culture means that you're making effort to balance safety and accountability." (Blameless PostMortems and a Just Culture, Etsy, 2012.)
- **[F:blameless-postmortem]** Blameless postmortem (popularized, with sidney-dekker): An incident review whose stated goal is learning, conducted under conditions where engineers can be honest about what they thought, did, and decided without career risk.
- **[F:above-the-line-below-the-line]** Above the line / below the line (popularized, with david-woods, richard-cook, john-allspaw): Operators never touch the software and hardware of a running system directly: it sits below a line of representation, and they work with it through the representations that cross that line, while the learning from an incident lies mostly in the cognitive work above. Outlined in the 2017 STELLA report; Allspaw presented it to practitioners that year, and a 2019 post of his credits the report.
- **[F:dark-debt]** Dark debt (distributed, with john-allspaw): Latent failure modes that exist in complex systems' interactions, unknown until an incident reveals them, and that are not discoverable by inspection of any individual component. Different from tech debt; closer to "unknown unknowns" as a structural class. The STELLA report attributes the term to a workshop participant it does not identify.
- **[F:adaptive-capacity]** Adaptive capacity (extended, with david-woods, john-allspaw): A system's ability to absorb disturbances and continue functioning through human and organizational reserves that can recognize and respond to novel threats. Robustness to known threats is a different property. The resilience-engineering core concept.
- **[F:work-as-imagined-vs-work-as-done]** Work-as-imagined vs. work-as-done (extended, with erik-hollnagel, john-allspaw): The distinction between how leadership / documentation / design thinks the work happens (imagined) and how it actually happens under real conditions (done). The gap is where adaptive capacity lives, and where incidents originate.
- **[L:1]** The "blameless" label gets misapplied (structural): Allspaw's blameless framing is about creating the conditions for honest learning while keeping accountability. The industry has partially adopted the label without the discipline. "Blameless" becomes a euphemism for "no consequences," which produces a different failure mode: incidents recur because no one feels ownership. The argument is harder than the label.
- **[L:2]** The model assumes a learning-receptive organization (structural): The Allspaw move works when leadership wants to learn from incidents. In organizations where incidents are treated as reputation events (public companies under regulatory pressure, vendors fighting churn, security orgs measured on incident count), the conditions for blameless analysis cannot be created from the bottom. The framework has no story for getting executives to tolerate the truth the framework would surface.
- **[L:3]** Adaptive-capacity vocabulary stays inside the resilience tradition (local): Outside the small community of practitioners who have done the Hollnagel / Woods / Cook / Dekker reading, the vocabulary doesn't translate cleanly. Most engineering orgs that use "blameless postmortem" don't recognize the full resilience-engineering frame underneath; the vocabulary spreads faster than the substrate. Worth naming the gap when carrying the work.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** The Etsy blameless-postmortem post, a blog entry of about 1,400 words, is still the text Google's SRE book and later writing on the practice cite. The Adaptive Capacity Labs work is the deeper version that the next decade of incident-response practitioners will keep returning to. Carrying Allspaw forward is how the resilience-engineering tradition stays in working contact with the software industry.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:david-woods]** extends, primary → David Woods (has a page): Allspaw's theoretical predecessor and Adaptive Capacity Labs co-founder. Woods built the resilience-engineering frame in academic safety science; Allspaw carried it into software ops. Read Woods on graceful extensibility before Allspaw on dark debt and the lineage clicks.
- **[N:david-woods:thesis]** David Woods's thesis: Complex systems are not stable artifacts; they are continuously adapting joint cognitive systems made up of humans, organizations, and machines. Their survival depends not on being robust to known threats (that property is locally useful but globally fragile), but on having reserves of adaptive capacity that humans supply in real time when surprises arrive. Every optimization push consumes that capacity; performance-pressured systems operate closer to their limits and absorb less surprise. Graceful extensibility (the ability to stretch performance under previously-unimagined conditions) is what you actually want, and it is exactly the property that efficiency-focused management most reliably destroys.
- **[E:out:sidney-dekker]** extends, primary → Sidney Dekker (has a page): The "new view of human error" and *just culture* tradition that the blameless postmortem puts into practice for software. Same lineage; Dekker is the safety side, Allspaw the engineering side.
- **[N:sidney-dekker:thesis]** Sidney Dekker's thesis: Treat human error as a *consequence*, not a cause. The investigator's job is not to determine what someone failed to do, but to reconstruct how the situation made sense to them at the time, because if it made sense, it will make sense again, and the next operator in that position will make the same choice. *Just culture* names the institutional posture that makes this kind of investigation possible. *Drift into failure* names the slow-motion process by which good organizations end up in bad places without anyone making a single obviously wrong decision. *Safety differently* names the prescription: invest in human adaptive capacity, not procedural compliance.
- **[E:out:fred-brooks]** parallel, secondary → Fred Brooks (has a page): The vocabulary move at a different scale. Brooks named the large-scale physics of software engineering; Allspaw names the incident-as-sample physics of running it in production. Same cartographer instinct, different layer of the stack.
- **[N:fred-brooks:thesis]** Fred Brooks's thesis: Software is fundamentally a communication problem dressed up as an engineering problem. The cost of building large systems is mostly the cost of coordinating the people building them; that cost grows superlinearly with team size and is bounded below by communication topology, not by individual programmer productivity. Therefore: adding people to a late project makes it later (Brooks's Law). Therefore: conceptual integrity is the single most important property of a system and must be defended by keeping the design in a small number of heads. Therefore: tools and methodologies that target *accidental* complexity (syntax, memory, type errors) can yield big productivity gains; tools and methodologies that promise to reduce *essential* complexity (the problem domain itself) are silver bullets and they do not exist.
- **[E:out:nora-jones]** parallel, secondary → Nora Jones: Launched the *Learning From Incidents* community in 2019 and co-wrote the *Howie* post-incident guide, which Allspaw reviewed. The next-generation carry of the same tradition into industry incident-analysis practice.
- **[E:out:richard-cook]** extends, primary → Richard Cook (has a page): Adaptive Capacity Labs co-founder and *How Complex Systems Fail* author. The medical-error-and-complex-systems lineage that Allspaw's software-ops work is built from.
- **[N:richard-cook:thesis]** Richard Cook's thesis: On Cook's account (a synthesis of the program he shared with Woods, which borrows Reason's language of latent failures), failure in a complex system belongs to the system. Such a system is heavily defended and always runs with flaws present, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together, and the people working in it create its safety continuously. An account that names one root cause, or one person's error, is then a social judgment made with the outcome known, and it does not explain how the failure worked. The explaining is done by the second story, the one about the systemic vulnerabilities the people involved were working inside (the pair set out in his 1998 report with Woods and Miller). His later work takes up efficiency gains that couple distant parts of a system, and the line of representation he presented with Woods, across which operators see the system only through what represents it.
- **[E:out:nicole-forsgren]** parallel, secondary → Nicole Forsgren (has a page): DORA's time to restore and resilience engineering converge on the same property from different methods: how fast a team recovers.
- **[N:nicole-forsgren:thesis]** Nicole Forsgren's thesis: Engineering performance is empirically tractable, and the instruments to prove it are real. The intuition that good software organizations differ structurally from bad ones, in kind rather than merely in degree, turns out to be testable, and the testing keeps confirming the claim. Throughput and stability do not trade off in well-run systems; they correlate. Elite performers cluster; the gap between elite and low is not normally distributed. The measurement substrate is operational: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore service. The decomposition is multi-dimensional: satisfaction, performance, activity, communication, efficiency become the SPACE; feedback loops, cognitive load, flow state become DevEx. Each layer is a sharper instrument for the same underlying argument: engineering work is a system, the system has lawful properties, the properties can be measured, and the measurement changes how the work gets organized. The cost of skipping the empirical work is that every measurement argument stays a measurement *argument* instead of a measurement program.
- **[E:out:charity-majors]** parallel, secondary → Charity Majors (has a page): The instrumentation layer beneath sensemaking. Majors argues telemetry should keep one wide record per request so responders can ask questions nobody predicted before the incident.
- **[N:charity-majors:thesis]** Charity Majors's thesis: Complex systems fail in ways their builders did not predict, so the useful question about telemetry is whether it can answer a question formed after the failure began, whatever it collects along the way. Pre-aggregated metrics cannot, because the decision about what to count was made before the question existed. Her remedy is to record one wide, structured event per unit of work, keep its high-cardinality fields intact, and derive dashboards, traces and alerts from that single record at read time. The same instinct drives her other positions: staging environments never match production, so the useful testing happens in production behind guardrails, and management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering, so good engineers should be able to move between the two.
- **[E:out:jez-humble]** parallel, primary → Jez Humble (has a page): Humble designs the route a change takes into production, which is the route that delivered the change this page's incidents start from. Allspaw wrote the foreword to the first edition of *The DevOps Handbook*, which Humble co-wrote with Gene Kim, Patrick Debois and John Willis.
- **[N:jez-humble:thesis]** Jez Humble's thesis: One route to production, taken by every change, is the claim underneath all of it. The artifact is built once and promoted through each environment, so what reaches production is the thing that was tested. Every stage is automated and every stage can reject the change, which makes the pipeline the release process itself instead of a document describing one. The discipline the 2010 book defines follows from that: keep the software in a releasable state continuously, and the question of when to release stops being an engineering question at all. In his account continuous delivery puts the release schedule in the hands of the business, which only works if any good build can go out on demand. The distinction that makes it possible is that deploying is not releasing. Code can run in production with no user able to reach it, and the two decisions can then be taken by different people, at different times, for different reasons. The principles underneath are borrowed and his own site says so, with Deming on building quality in and Toyota's jidoka and Taiichi Ohno's kaizen behind two of the others.
- **[E:out:gene-kim]** parallel, primary → Gene Kim (has a page): Kim supplies the management programme the incidents on this page arrive inside. The third of his numbered principles instructs an organization from above on how to treat a failure it chose to risk; Allspaw's subject begins after a failure nobody chose has already happened, with the question of what makes an account of it trustworthy.
- **[N:gene-kim:thesis]** Gene Kim's thesis: One argument runs under all of it. Technology work is a system, and its performance is set by how work moves through it, how quickly the news of a problem comes back, and whether the people inside it are free to experiment. The Three Ways state that as a sequence. The First Way is put as the performance of the entire system rather than the performance of a specific silo, and Deming is credited inside it for always seeking to achieve profound understanding of the system. The Second Way is the right-to-left feedback loop, shortened and amplified so corrections can be made continually. The Third Way is a culture that fosters experimentation and learning from failure while treating repetition and practice as the prerequisite to mastery. The later sets come at the same claim from other sides, and both are lists of names before they are arguments. The Five Ideals read as the conditions the work is meant to meet, in an order that starts at locality and simplicity. The three mechanisms of the 2023 book answer a question about where problem solving should happen, and the book's answer is that an organization wins by moving it out of the zones where it is dangerous. The lean lineage is named by both authors in their own prefaces (Steven Spear and H. Kent Bowen's *Harvard Business Review* article decoding the Toyota Production System), and Goldratt's theory of constraints is credited inside the novel's own text.
- **[E:out:erik-hollnagel]** parallel, primary → Erik Hollnagel (has a page): Named the efficiency-thoroughness trade-off and gave the shortcuts it produces names of their own, such as "Will be checked by someone else". Allspaw's master's thesis took up that catalogue of judgement heuristics.
- **[N:erik-hollnagel:thesis]** Erik Hollnagel's thesis: Hollnagel argues that safety has traditionally been defined by what goes wrong, and that this leaves the object of study too small. Failures are rare, and the same adjustments that let work succeed under changing conditions are what occasionally let it fail. So the useful question for safety management is how things usually go right, and an investigation explains the rare failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism. People cannot be as efficient as possible and as thorough as possible at once, the trade between the two is normal, and the shortcuts that make work efficient are the same ones that sometimes let a failure through.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:david-woods]** Woods describes complex systems as joint cognitive systems, in which people and automation do the thinking together and failures live in the joint between them. Such systems survive surprises through adaptive capacity: reserves that people and organizational arrangements supply, and that stay invisible until they are needed. The Law of Stretched Systems explains why those reserves shrink: slack looks like inefficiency, so every system gets pushed toward the edge of its envelope, where small disturbances produce large failures. Graceful extensibility, the ability to stretch under conditions nobody designed for, is the property resilience engineering aims at. The original papers are dense, which is why carriers such as Allspaw brought the vocabulary into software.
- **[R:sidney-dekker]** Dekker's starting point is that human error is a consequence of system conditions, so the investigator's job is to reconstruct how an action made sense to the person at the time. If it made sense once, it will make sense to the next person in the same position. Just culture draws a line between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour, and Dekker's version asks who gets to draw it, since culpability does not inhere in the act itself. Drift into failure explains how organizations reach unsafe states through many small, reasonable choices toward efficiency, none of them obviously wrong. The frame works best when used surgically, because invoking drift for every incident can close off the question of which decisions could have gone differently.
- **[R:fred-brooks]** For Brooks, software is a communication problem before it is an engineering problem, and the cost of coordinating the people building a system grows faster than the team. Brooks's Law follows directly: adding people to a late project makes it later. No Silver Bullet sharpens the claim by separating essential complexity, which lives in the problem, from accidental complexity, which the tools add. Tooling can remove accidental complexity, sometimes dramatically, while no single development shrinks the essential part by an order of magnitude. The prediction dates from 1986 and still stands, which is why the vocabulary carries weight in any honest conversation about engineering.
- **[R:richard-cook]** The eighteen propositions of How Complex Systems Fail run from the nature of hazard, through how blame gets assigned, to safety as something people create. In Cook's account a complex system is heavily defended and always carries flaws, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together. It follows that naming a single root cause after an accident answers a social need to blame and leaves the mechanism of the failure unexplained. The 1998 report he wrote with Woods and Miller gives the alternative: the first story is one of human error, the second one of the systemic vulnerabilities behind it. Going solid, written with Rasmussen in 2005, describes efficiency gains stripping the buffers between distant parts of a hospital until they are tightly coupled. Cook's 2020 statement of the line of representation, a frame set out in the 2017 STELLA report, is written for software operators, who reach the system only through representations.
- **[R:nicole-forsgren]** The part of Forsgren that travels furthest is a finding: in well-run delivery systems, throughput and stability rise together. That finding removes the most common argument for slowing change down, the belief that each additional deploy spends safety. DORA's stability measures, change failure rate and time to restore, make the safety question observable: how often a change degrades service, and how fast the team recovers when one does. Because DORA is itself the doctrine here, the four keys belong to the delivery system they were built to measure. Turning them into targets for teams invites the misuse the frame's own limits describe, where people manage to the number until it stops describing the system.
- **[R:charity-majors]** Majors judges telemetry by one test: can an engineer ask a question of a live system that nobody thought to ask while designing it. Pre-aggregated metrics fail that test, because the choice of what to count was made before the question existed. Her answer is one wide structured record per unit of work, with its high-cardinality fields kept whole, so views can be derived from it at read time instead of being fixed at write time. The fields she insists on keeping are the ones that identify a single request: user id, build id, feature flag. The pattern came from Scuba at Facebook, and the company she co-founded sells it. The same instinct runs through her other positions, that staging cannot reproduce production and that management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering.
- **[R:jez-humble]** The 2006 paper argues that a change should take exactly one route to production, and every other claim here follows from it, the discipline of the 2010 book included. That is what makes the discipline work: software kept releasable at all times, with any good build deployable on demand. One distinction inside the discipline is smaller and separable: deploying code and releasing a feature are two decisions, so code can run in production with nobody able to reach it. Almost none of it is his alone and he is usually the one saying so: Chris Read and Dan North on the pipeline paper, Dave Farley on the book, Deming and Toyota behind the principles. What is his is the writing down: the paper set an existing practice out in an order other people could follow.
- **[R:gene-kim]** The Three Ways were the first of Kim's numbered sets, posted in August 2012. The later sets arrive as named lists, the Five Ideals from the solo book and the post naming them, and three mechanisms with Steven Spear the book says move problem solving out of high-risk zones. Each of the sets comes at one claim, that technology work is a system whose performance is set by flow, feedback and experiment. The distribution is the second contribution: a novel, a summit he has run since 2014, and a handbook crediting the 2012 post for the frame.
- **[R:erik-hollnagel]** Hollnagel moves the object of safety management from the rare accident to the ordinary working day. Safety-I counts adverse outcomes and aims to minimise them, which confines its evidence to rare events. Safety-II asks how work usually succeeds under varying conditions, and explains the occasional failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism: nobody can be as efficient and as thorough as possible at once, and trading one for the other is normal work. He catalogues the shortcuts that trade produces, among them "Looks fine" and "Has been checked by someone else". In his account, those shortcuts are how the work gets done and also what occasionally lets a failure through. The contrast is disputed at its root: Leveson says the Safety-I he describes bears little resemblance to safety engineering as practised, and Cooper groups Safety-II with other approaches into a New View whose propositions he calls untested.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Allspaw treats an incident as a sample of how a system really operates, carrying information no design document or runbook can supply. Getting that information requires conditions where engineers can say honestly what they saw and decided without career risk, which is the purpose of the blameless postmortem. The analysis then looks above the line, at the mental models and coordination that operate the system, as well as below it at code and configuration. Dark debt names what that analysis keeps finding: failure modes that live in the interactions between components and cannot be found by inspecting any one of them. The frame fails when blameless gets read as consequence-free, which keeps the label and drops the learning discipline.

> Security work generates dark debt quickly, because every new builder, API, agent, and workflow adds interactions that no single review sees. As those surfaces combine, their failure modes multiply, and they tend to surface only through incidents. That makes the incident stream a security organization's best instrument for finding dark debt, and measuring the team on incident count degrades the instrument. A team scored on fewer incidents learns to report fewer, and the samples that would have revealed the next interaction failure never reach review. The fix has to come from leadership, because a team already scored on incident count cannot rewrite its own scorecard. Once the scoring changes, reported incidents and near misses can be treated as signal worth rewarding.
