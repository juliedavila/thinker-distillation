## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Traditional information security, in Shortridge's account, "presents a poor ROI": its payoff arrives only when an attack is prevented, and attacks are inevitable. Security Obstructionism is her name for the effort that persists anyway, because it yields outputs, control and status inside the organization while the definition of "secure" stays vague enough that nobody can check it. Her remedy is to spend where the return survives a successful attack: systems designed to absorb an attack and reorganize, controls built into the design ahead of controls that rely on training and policy, security programs that software engineers and architects build "in parallel," and experiments that check whether those controls behave as believed. Her early account of resilience comes from ecology (C.S. Holling, and Walker, Holling, Carpenter and Kinzig, 2004).
- **[T:working-line]** Resilience in security means a flexible system that can absorb an attack and reorganize around the threat. ("Resilience in Security 101," kellyshortridge.com, 2020.)
- **[F:security-obstructionism]** Security Obstructionism (SecObs) (original): Her name, introduced in a 2022 essay, for "a category of tools, policies, and practices whose outcome is to impede or prevent progress for security's (speculative) sake." The complaint behind it is older, and the essay places the term against "the dreaded Department of No"; the category and the argument that it persists for control and status are hers. - **[F:ice-cream-cone-hierarchy]** Ice Cream Cone Hierarchy of Security Solutions (extended; derived from hierarchy-of-controls): Security solutions ranked from most to least effective: eliminate hazards by design, substitute less hazardous methods or materials, incorporate safety devices and guards, provide warning and awareness systems, and, "last and least effective," administrative controls. The ordering closely parallels the system safety design order of precedence in MIL-STD-882E and the NIOSH hierarchy of controls. The name and the move into software security are hers, in her 2023 writing.
- **[F:security-chaos-engineering]** Security chaos engineering (co-authored, with kelly-shortridge; derived from chaos-engineering): Continual experiments that inject adverse conditions to verify that a system's security behaves the way its builders believe. Her 2022 paper says it "extends chaos engineering ... to the domain of security." Aaron Rinehart released ChaoSlingr, a security chaos engineering tool, at UnitedHealth Group, and her own paper cites it as 2017; her 2017 resilience keynote separately proposed failure injection in the manner of Netflix's Chaos Monkey. The 2020 O'Reilly report puts Rinehart's name first and the 2023 book puts hers first.
- **[F:fomosec]** FOMOsec (original): Security strategy driven by fear of missing out, "one that frightens people into misallocating resources towards what makes them feel better." Named in 2020 as the opposite of YOLOsec, a strategy that "embodies the 'you only live once' mentality," which her post says she introduced in a 2017 Black Hat talk.
- **[F:effort-investment-portfolio]** Effort Investment Portfolio (original): Finite effort treated as capital: the concept "captures the need to balance our 'effort capital' across activities to best achieve our objectives." ## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:nicole-forsgren]** parallel, primary → Nicole Forsgren (has a page): Co-presented "Controlled Chaos: The Inevitable Marriage of DevOps & Security" at Black Hat USA 2019, which argued for time to recovery as a security metric and for chaos experiments on security controls. The SecObs essay borrows Forsgren's distinction between outputs and outcomes, citing Accelerate and the SPACE paper, and points it at security programs.
- **[N:nicole-forsgren:thesis]** Nicole Forsgren's thesis: Engineering performance is empirically tractable, and the instruments to prove it are real. The intuition that good software organizations differ structurally from bad ones, in kind rather than merely in degree, turns out to be testable, and the testing keeps confirming the claim. Throughput and stability do not trade off in well-run systems; they correlate. Elite performers cluster; the gap between elite and low is not normally distributed. The measurement substrate is operational: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore service. The decomposition is multi-dimensional: satisfaction, performance, activity, communication, efficiency become the SPACE; feedback loops, cognitive load, flow state become DevEx. Each layer is a sharper instrument for the same underlying argument: engineering work is a system, the system has lawful properties, the properties can be measured, and the measurement changes how the work gets organized. The cost of skipping the empirical work is that every measurement argument stays a measurement *argument* instead of a measurement program.
- **[E:out:erik-hollnagel]** parallel, primary → Erik Hollnagel (has a page): Her 2024 book summary describes the gap between "work-as-practiced" and "work-as-imagined", the pair Hollnagel carried through resilience engineering, without naming a source. Her primers on resilience credit ecology instead. What her version adds is an adversary who exploits the gap.
- **[N:erik-hollnagel:thesis]** Erik Hollnagel's thesis: Hollnagel argues that safety has traditionally been defined by what goes wrong, and that this leaves the object of study too small. Failures are rare, and the same adjustments that let work succeed under changing conditions are what occasionally let it fail. So the useful question for safety management is how things usually go right, and an investigation explains the rare failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism. People cannot be as efficient as possible and as thorough as possible at once, the trade between the two is normal, and the shortcuts that make work efficient are the same ones that sometimes let a failure through.
- **[E:out:ross-anderson]** parallel, primary → Ross Anderson (has a page): Both treat security as economics, from different ends. Anderson's security economics explains failure through externalities, asymmetric information and guardians who do not bear the loss. Shortridge prices the defender's own spending: what a program returns once prevention fails, and what obstruction costs the organization that pays for it.
- **[N:ross-anderson:thesis]** Ross Anderson's thesis: Anderson's argument is that security failures are distributed by incentive more than by technical difficulty, and that the field keeps looking in the wrong place because nothing tells it where the losses are. The 1993 work set the shape. Aviation institutionalised learning from accidents and cryptography did not, and the absence of that feedback let a false threat model stand for years while the real frauds ran through implementation and management. Once the losses are visible, the pattern in them is economic. Where the party who can protect a system is not the party who suffers when it fails, failure should be expected, and better cryptography does not change that arithmetic. The remedy he reached for sits mostly outside the security team. The 2001 paper borrows liability dumping, network externalities, moral hazard and the tragedy of the commons from microeconomics, because the interesting variable is who bears the cost. His work for the British Medical Association made a matching argument about aggregation: the likelihood a resource is abused depends on its value and on the number of people who can reach it, and putting personal information into one large database raises both at once. And where no venue existed for the questions that followed, he convened one.
- **[E:out:david-woods]** parallel, secondary → David Woods (has a page): The SecObs essay's claim that "Security is treated as a facet of resilience" cites "Features of resilience" (2017), a paper Woods co-wrote with Connelly, Linkov and others. Her own primers build resilience from ecology, so the two meet on how systems absorb surprise from different starting points.
- **[N:david-woods:thesis]** David Woods's thesis: Complex systems are not stable artifacts; they are continuously adapting joint cognitive systems made up of humans, organizations, and machines. Their survival depends not on being robust to known threats (that property is locally useful but globally fragile), but on having reserves of adaptive capacity that humans supply in real time when surprises arrive. Every optimization push consumes that capacity; performance-pressured systems operate closer to their limits and absorb less surprise. Graceful extensibility (the ability to stretch performance under previously-unimagined conditions) is what you actually want, and it is exactly the property that efficiency-focused management most reliably destroys.
- **[E:out:charity-majors]** parallel, secondary → Charity Majors (has a page): Both treat failure in production as certain and build practice around learning from it. Majors argues for testing against real traffic behind guardrails; Shortridge argues for injecting attack conditions to check that security controls behave as believed.
- **[N:charity-majors:thesis]** Charity Majors's thesis: Complex systems fail in ways their builders did not predict, so the useful question about telemetry is whether it can answer a question formed after the failure began, whatever it collects along the way. Pre-aggregated metrics cannot, because the decision about what to count was made before the question existed. Her remedy is to record one wide, structured event per unit of work, keep its high-cardinality fields intact, and derive dashboards, traces and alerts from that single record at read time. The same instinct drives her other positions: staging environments never match production, so the useful testing happens in production behind guardrails, and management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering, so good engineers should be able to move between the two.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:nicole-forsgren]** The part of Forsgren that travels furthest is a finding: in well-run delivery systems, throughput and stability rise together. That finding removes the most common argument for slowing change down, the belief that each additional deploy spends safety. DORA's stability measures, change failure rate and time to restore, make the safety question observable: how often a change degrades service, and how fast the team recovers when one does. Because DORA is itself the doctrine here, the four keys belong to the delivery system they were built to measure. Turning them into targets for teams invites the misuse the frame's own limits describe, where people manage to the number until it stops describing the system.
- **[R:erik-hollnagel]** Hollnagel moves the object of safety management from the rare accident to the ordinary working day. Safety-I counts adverse outcomes and aims to minimise them, which confines its evidence to rare events. Safety-II asks how work usually succeeds under varying conditions, and explains the occasional failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism: nobody can be as efficient and as thorough as possible at once, and trading one for the other is normal work. He catalogues the shortcuts that trade produces, among them "Looks fine" and "Has been checked by someone else". In his account, those shortcuts are how the work gets done and also what occasionally lets a failure through. The contrast is disputed at its root: Leveson says the Safety-I he describes bears little resemblance to safety engineering as practised, and Cooper groups Safety-II with other approaches into a New View whose propositions he calls untested.
- **[R:ross-anderson]** Anderson's 1993 finding came from collecting how cash machine fraud happened: most of it ran through implementation error and management failure while the field designed against cryptanalysis. The paper blames a missing feedback loop: aviation investigates its accidents and cryptography had no equivalent. Once the losses are visible the pattern is economic, and his rule is that problems may be expected wherever the party able to protect a system is not the one who suffers. That reading began in a conversation with the economist Hal Varian, borrowing moral hazard and network externalities from economics. The phrasing that travelled is "misaligned incentives", a section heading in the 2006 paper with Tyler Moore. The late papers carry his name inside other people's work: second to his student Nicholas Boucher on Trojan Source, fifth of six behind Ilia Shumailov on model collapse. He wrote a standard textbook, and his biographer credits him with catalysing the communities that outlived him.
- **[R:david-woods]** Woods describes complex systems as joint cognitive systems, in which people and automation do the thinking together and failures live in the joint between them. Such systems survive surprises through adaptive capacity: reserves that people and organizational arrangements supply, and that stay invisible until they are needed. The Law of Stretched Systems explains why those reserves shrink: slack looks like inefficiency, so every system gets pushed toward the edge of its envelope, where small disturbances produce large failures. Graceful extensibility, the ability to stretch under conditions nobody designed for, is the property resilience engineering aims at. The original papers are dense, which is why carriers such as Allspaw brought the vocabulary into software.
- **[R:charity-majors]** Majors judges telemetry by one test: can an engineer ask a question of a live system that nobody thought to ask while designing it. Pre-aggregated metrics fail that test, because the choice of what to count was made before the question existed. Her answer is one wide structured record per unit of work, with its high-cardinality fields kept whole, so views can be derived from it at read time instead of being fixed at write time. The fields she insists on keeping are the ones that identify a single request: user id, build id, feature flag. The pattern came from Scuba at Facebook, and the company she co-founded sells it. The same instinct runs through her other positions, that staging cannot reproduce production and that management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Shortridge weighs a security program as an investment, and the return that counts is what remains after an attack succeeds. Prevention pays only when an attack is stopped, so she moves effort toward "a flexible system that can absorb an attack and reorganize around the threat." Security Obstructionism names where part of the budget goes instead: tools, policies and practices that impede progress "for security's (speculative) sake" and persist because they yield outputs, control and status. The Ice Cream Cone, her name for a ranking that closely parallels the system-safety order of precedence, puts hazards designed out at the top and administrative controls "last and least effective." Security chaos engineering, a practice she shares with Aaron Rinehart, tests by experiment whether the controls behave as their builders believe. Her hazard is an opponent, and the premise she states is that "attackers will adapt in response to your mitigations."

> Her account of SecObs suggests why a program drifts toward the bottom of the cone: a guideline or a review yields countable outputs, and a hazard designed out leaves nothing to point to. Her definition also names tools, so obstruction can sit in the guard tier too. A sort by tier then asks what share of a program rests on people complying, and which higher-tier controls mainly produce a gate. The tiers hold per attack path, because an attacker whose route was designed out tries another. So the sort has to be rerun on the attacker's next path, which her 2017 decision trees drew and the cone, as her posts state it, does not. That needs engineers who own the design, and it inherits the tree's blind spot, a path nobody pictured. Her experiments, which check whether a system behaves as its builders believe, are aimed at that gap.
