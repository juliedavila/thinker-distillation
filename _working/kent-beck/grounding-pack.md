## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Software costs what changing it costs. Beck states that as Constantine's Equivalence, cost(software) ~= cost(change), and credits the coupling and cohesion work in *Structured Design* for the underlying result. Coupling is then the thing to watch, because it is what makes one change drag others behind it, and design is whatever brings it down. The work happens in tidyings, small changes to structure that leave behavior alone. The two kinds of change are asymmetrical. A structure change can be undone, while a behavior change that has reached customers cannot, which is the reason his practices favor small steps with a test behind each one. Whether to do the structural work first is an investment question rather than a matter of virtue, and his own answer runs both ways: discounted cash flow argues for earning sooner and spending later, options argue for spending now, and the decision is the inequality between them.
- **[T:working-line]** Tidy first? Yes. Also no. When the cost of tidying plus the cost of the eased change comes in under the cost of the change alone, tidy first. ("Options Versus Cash Flows," Software Design: Tidy First?, 2022.)
- **[F:test-driven-development]** Test-driven development (extended): Write the test that fails first, then the code that makes it pass, then clean up what you wrote. Beck declines the credit for inventing it. He traces the technique to an old programming book that told the reader to type the expected output tape by hand and program until the real output matched, says he tried it after writing the first xUnit framework in Smalltalk, and reports older programmers answering "Of course. How else could you program?" His own word for his role is "rediscovering." His account does not name the book. Test-first work is on record before him: Larman and Basili's 2003 history reports that the development team on NASA's Project Mercury, working in half-day iterations, planned and wrote tests before each micro-increment.
- **[F:extreme-programming]** Extreme Programming (distributed, with cynthia-andres): A whole method assembled in 1999 and written up by Beck, organized around a small set of values, principles and practices. The practice came out of a team: the Chrysler record reads that in 1997 the development team adopted the way of working now formalized as Extreme Programming. The second edition of 2004 is co-authored with Cynthia Andres and was rewritten rather than revised, so any count of the values, principles or practices belongs to that book and to her as well as to him.
- **[F:constantines-equivalence]** Constantine's Equivalence (extended, with larry-constantine, edward-yourdon): Beck's name for a result he credits to someone else: the cost of software is approximately the cost of changing it, which he writes as cost(software) ~= cost(change). He names the antecedent himself, the original work on coupling and cohesion in *Structured Design* by Edward Yourdon and Larry Constantine, and the name is his tribute to it. It is chapter 30 of *Tidy First?*, inside a part that introduces coupling, cohesion, discounted cash flows and optionality.
- **[F:tidying]** Tidying (original; derived from refactoring): A small change to the structure of code that leaves its behavior alone, made to cheapen the behavior change coming next. The two kinds of change differ by reversibility in his account: extract a helper you dislike and you can inline it again, while a behavior change that sends a hundred thousand wrong tax notices cannot be taken back. Whether to tidy first is an economic question he answers both ways, since discounted cash flow argues against it and options argue for it, and his rule is to tidy first when the tidying plus the eased change costs less than the change alone.
- **[F:make-the-change-easy]** Make the change easy, then make the easy change (original): Posted in 2012 as "for each desired change, make the change easy (warning: this may be hard), then make the easy change." The warning in the middle is the clause that gets dropped when the line is repeated, and it is where the cost sits. Fowler's 2015 article on preparatory refactoring is the durable carrier, quoting it twice under Beck's name.
- **[F:three-x]** 3X: Explore, Expand, Extract (original): Product development runs in three phases and the right practice differs in each: exploring is the risky search for a viable return on a viable investment, expanding is what happens when the thing takes, extracting is for when the shape of the problem and the solution is clear. Beck credits no antecedent for it, in a series whose stated aim is "no analogies, no persuasion, just the facts." Jason Yip's reading of it as Theodore Levitt's 1965 product life cycle is Yip's claim about Beck rather than a credit Beck gives, and the Kelly Criterion derivation Beck posted in 2021 came years after the frame.
- **[F:code-smell]** Code smell (co-authored, with martin-fowler): A surface sign in code that points at a deeper design problem. Fowler's account is unambiguous about where the term came from: "The term was first coined by Kent Beck while helping me with my Refactoring book." Fowler is the one who cataloged the smells and his page carries this frame in the foreground, which is why it sits in the deep tier here.
- **[F:crc-cards]** CRC cards (co-authored, with ward-cunningham): Index cards that characterize an object by its class name, its responsibilities and its collaborators, used to teach object-oriented design by moving cards around a table. One paper, written with Ward Cunningham and presented at OOPSLA in October 1989.
- **[L:1]** He states the boundaries of test-driven development himself (local): Writing in 2014, Beck said he was "puzzled by the limits of TDD" because "it works so well for algorithm-y, data-structure-y code," and set out where it stops: "I recognize that TDD loses value as tests take longer to run, as the number of possible faults per test failure increases, as tests become coupled to the implementation, and as tests lose fidelity with the production environment." Those four conditions describe a great deal of the software a large organization actually runs. The concession is his, and the words reach this page through a third-party blog that reproduced a Facebook post, since the original is no longer reachable.
- **[L:2]** A decade of studies has not converged (structural): Rafique and Mišić's 2013 meta-analysis of 27 studies found a small positive effect on quality alongside little or no effect on productivity, with both the quality gain and the productivity drop larger in industrial studies than in academic ones. Seven years later Ghafari, Gross, Fucci and Felderer asked why the literature is contradictory at all, and identified five categories of study-design factors that change what a TDD experiment finds. The strongest claims made for the method rest on evidence that does not settle, and that has been true for long enough to count as a property of the evidence rather than a gap in it.
- **[L:3]** The word travels further than the practice (structural): Reporting on a 2020 survey of developers found 41% saying their organization had fully adopted test-driven development while only 8% said they write tests before code at least 80% of the time. The gap is the page's structural limit and cannot be read the other way: universal vocabulary is not evidence of a working method. Beck's own career holds a version of it. By Gergely Orosz's account of an interview with him, Facebook "barely did any form of unit testing, while running a massive, stable, and fast-growing site," and a TDD class Beck offered at a company hackathon drew no signups at all.
- **[L:4]** The tidying repertoire is thin, by the strongest published critique (structural): Jimmy Koppel's 2024 review grants that *Tidy First?* achieves what it sets out to do, and calls the result "a rather impoverished repertoire" whose tidyings "can't solve any of the refactoring challenges I've been running lately." He also argues the book is assembled wrong, reading as if the chapters of a shallow book and a deep one were mixed together. The indictment is of that book. It says nothing about test-driven development, Extreme Programming or the measurement writing, and it should not be stretched to.
- **[L:5]** No adversary appears anywhere in the material behind this page (structural): Every source this page rests on describes software changed by the people who own it. Nothing in the corpus considers a change made by someone trying to cause harm, which means the economics have never been tested against an actor who benefits from a wrong decision. The extension above applies his pricing to that setting, and every sentence of it is the site's argument rather than his. A reader should treat any claim about security that arrives with Beck's name attached as the site speaking.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** The question survives all of it, because it is a question about money rather than a claim about a method: what will the next change to this system cost, and what would it cost to make that change cheaper first? A team that cannot answer is buying structural work on faith or refusing it on faith, and both are decisions made without the number. His refusal to answer it in one direction is the part most likely to outlast the practices, since it is the rare piece of engineering advice that tells a reader when to stop taking it.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:martin-fowler]** parallel, primary → Martin Fowler (has a page): Fowler is where Beck's vocabulary reached people who never read Beck. He cataloged the smells Beck named, published *Refactoring* with him in 1999 and *Planning Extreme Programming* with him in 2000, quoted the change-easy line twice under Beck's name in 2015, and hosted the public conversation about test-driven development in 2014. The division of labor is the interesting part. Beck names a practice and prices it; Fowler catalogs it and keeps the attribution attached to it.
- **[N:martin-fowler:thesis]** Martin Fowler's thesis: Software is a perishable medium for shared understanding. It rots when nobody is tending it, and the cost of letting it rot is paid not in some distant future but in next week's velocity. The way to keep software cheap to change is to evolve practice in public: name the moves, share the vocabulary, run the build-test-deploy loop tight, place the boundaries where change wants to flow, model the domain in the language the team already uses with the customer, and write the practice down as you discover it. Quality IS speed. Naming the implicit is how the argument becomes negotiable. The catalog, the bliki entry, the pattern, the named refactoring, the smell: these are the cartographer's instruments.
- **[E:out:nicole-forsgren]** parallel, primary → Nicole Forsgren (has a page): Both work on what a measurement does to the thing it measures, from opposite ends. Forsgren builds instruments meant to describe a delivery system. Beck writes the first-person account of one coming apart: at Facebook, survey scores "started cropping up in performance reviews," then became goals, then rolled up so that a manager's score was the average of their reports' scores, after which managers negotiated with individual contributors for better scores. His 2023 response with Gergely Orosz argues against McKinsey's framework for measuring effort rather than outcome, and Part 2 of it does not name Forsgren.
- **[N:nicole-forsgren:thesis]** Nicole Forsgren's thesis: Engineering performance is empirically tractable, and the instruments to prove it are real. The intuition that good software organizations differ structurally from bad ones, in kind rather than merely in degree, turns out to be testable, and the testing keeps confirming the claim. Throughput and stability do not trade off in well-run systems; they correlate. Elite performers cluster; the gap between elite and low is not normally distributed. The measurement substrate is operational: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore service. The decomposition is multi-dimensional: satisfaction, performance, activity, communication, efficiency become the SPACE; feedback loops, cognitive load, flow state become DevEx. Each layer is a sharper instrument for the same underlying argument: engineering work is a system, the system has lawful properties, the properties can be measured, and the measurement changes how the work gets organized. The cost of skipping the empirical work is that every measurement argument stays a measurement *argument* instead of a measurement program.
- **[E:out:rich-hickey]** parallel, primary → Rich Hickey (has a page): The same conclusion reached from opposite premises. Hickey argues from semantics that braiding independent concerns together is what makes software hard to change. Beck argues from money that the cost of software is the cost of changing it, and puts coupling in the place where that cost accumulates. Neither cites the other, so the agreement is the site's reading rather than a lineage.
- **[N:rich-hickey:thesis]** Rich Hickey's thesis: Reliability depends on being able to understand a system, and people can only reason about a few things at a time, so the property that matters most is whether the parts of a system are intertwined. Simplicity in that sense is objective and chosen; ease is relative to the person and says nothing about how tangled the result is. Incidental complexity enters when teams pick familiar constructs (mutable objects, in-place updates, breaking version changes) that braid together things the problem kept separate. The remedy is to build from values, plain data and composable pieces, and to treat tests and type systems as safety nets that cannot make a tangled design simple. (The premise that simplicity is a prerequisite for reliability is Dijkstra's, quoted at the start of "Simple Made Easy.")
- **[E:out:jimmy-koppel]** critiques, secondary → Jimmy Koppel: His 2024 review of *Tidy First?* is the one durable critique that takes Beck on by name. It grants the goal, then calls the result "a rather impoverished repertoire" whose tidyings "can't solve any of the refactoring challenges I've been running lately," and argues the chapters of two different books were mixed together. The scope is that book alone.
- **[E:out:dhh]** parallel, secondary → David Heinemeier Hansson: "TDD is dead. Long live testing." (2014) is the sharpest published case against test-first practice, and it never names Beck. The five-episode series that followed does name him, and Fowler's account is that Beck asked for the conversation, so Beck appears in it as a discussant. The Forsgren page types the same man as a critic of a different target, which is a separate edge and not this one.
- **[E:out:ward-cunningham]** parallel, secondary → Ward Cunningham: Co-author of the one CRC cards paper, presented at OOPSLA in 1989 when Beck was at Apple and Cunningham at Wyatt Software. The frame above carries the credit in full, so the edge sits in the deep tier.
- **[E:out:larry-constantine]** antecedent, secondary → Larry Constantine: With Edward Yourdon, the author of the coupling and cohesion work in *Structured Design* that Beck names as the source of the result he then named after Constantine. Beck quotes them both by name in his own newsletter, which is where the equivalence is stated.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:martin-fowler]** In Fowler's account, software is a medium for shared understanding, and it decays whenever nobody tends it. The cost of that decay arrives quickly, in next week's velocity, and his design stamina hypothesis makes a parallel claim that internal quality pays back within weeks. His method is to name the moves (refactoring, code smell, strangler fig, bounded context), attribute each to its origin, and revise them in public on the bliki. Naming turns tacit design choices into distinctions a team can argue about. The method has limits of its own: his 2014 microservices article undersold the cost of distribution, and later follow-ups had to correct it.
- **[R:nicole-forsgren]** The part of Forsgren that travels furthest is a finding: in well-run delivery systems, throughput and stability rise together. That finding removes the most common argument for slowing change down, the belief that each additional deploy spends safety. DORA's stability measures, change failure rate and time to restore, make the safety question observable: how often a change degrades service, and how fast the team recovers when one does. Because DORA is itself the doctrine here, the four keys belong to the delivery system they were built to measure. Turning them into targets for teams invites the misuse the frame's own limits describe, where people manage to the number until it stops describing the system.
- **[R:rich-hickey]** "Simple Made Easy" pulls apart two words the industry treats as one: simple means nothing is interleaved, a property of the artifact, and easy means near at hand, a property of the person. Hickey locates complexity in complecting, the braiding together of concerns that could stay independent. A familiar tool can bring complexity the problem never needed, which he calls incidental complexity, his name for what Brooks called accidental. Tests and type checkers catch mistakes after the fact, and in his account they are safety nets that cannot untangle a design. The later talks carry the same concern into data and change. Overwriting a record in place discards the fact it replaced, and removing anything from a published interface breaks whoever depends on it. He scopes these arguments to long-running information systems, and his case against static types remains contested.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Beck's subject is the price of the next change, which Constantine's Equivalence states as cost(software) ~= cost(change). Coupling is what makes that price high, so design is whatever brings it down. The work happens in tidyings, small changes to structure that leave behavior alone. The two kinds are asymmetrical: a helper extracted can be inlined again, and a behavior change that reached customers cannot. Small steps with a test behind each one follow from that asymmetry, and he calls the test-first technique a rediscovery whose limits he states himself. His answer to whether to tidy first is an inequality rather than a rule, and it sometimes comes out no. Extreme Programming is the method he wrote up in 1999, out of the way of working a payroll team adopted in 1997. His 3X phases add the axis the others lack, since a practice that fits a product being explored is waste once it is being extracted.

> Controls around an agent keep being revised, because prompt injection is mitigated rather than solved. Nothing in the material behind this page is about security; what follows is the site's use of his pricing. Each revision is a behavior change to a live control, and what it let through while it was wrong cannot be taken back. Coupling makes revisions expensive: the decision about what an agent may reach sits in every integration. The structural work worth buying puts that decision in one place and wires the integrations to it, so the next revision is an edit. His inequality is the brake, though its terms are cost alone and an attacker picks the moment. The 3X phases say which products are still moving, and an exploring one holding real customer data has an extracting product's blast radius. That structural work needs an owner and a budget no feature funds, and it concentrates into one target what was spread across many.
