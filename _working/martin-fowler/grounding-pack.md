## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Software is a perishable medium for shared understanding. It rots when nobody is tending it, and the cost of letting it rot is paid not in some distant future but in next week's velocity. The way to keep software cheap to change is to evolve practice in public: name the moves, share the vocabulary, run the build-test-deploy loop tight, place the boundaries where change wants to flow, model the domain in the language the team already uses with the customer, and write the practice down as you discover it. Quality IS speed. Naming the implicit is how the argument becomes negotiable. The catalog, the bliki entry, the pattern, the named refactoring, the smell: these are the cartographer's instruments.
- **[T:working-line]** Make the change easy, then make the easy change. (Kent Beck's line, which Fowler quotes under Beck's name.)
- **[F:refactoring]** Refactoring (extended, with william-opdyke, kent-beck; derived from opdyke-thesis-1992): A disciplined structure-changing move that does not change behavior, made safe by tests. Improves the design of existing code in small, reversible steps.
- **[F:code-smell]** Code smell (co-authored, with kent-beck, martin-fowler): A surface signal that something deeper in the code is wrong; a named heuristic for what to refactor away from.
- **[F:bounded-context]** Bounded context (popularized, with eric-evans): A region of the model inside which the language is consistent. Fowler's entry explains Eric Evans's pattern and says a different model is needed "when the language changes".
- **[F:strangler-fig-application]** Strangler fig application (original, with chris-stevenson, andy-pols): A migration pattern: grow the new system around the old one until the old one can be removed, instead of rewriting from scratch.
- **[F:continuous-integration]** Continuous integration (popularized, with kent-beck, jez-humble, dave-farley): A development practice: integrate work into the mainline at least daily, verified by an automated build, so defects surface in hours not weeks.
- **[F:design-stamina-hypothesis]** Design stamina hypothesis (original): A named claim (labeled as hypothesis, not law) that internal quality pays back faster than expected. The crossover where quality work outpaces shortcut work happens in weeks, not years.
- **[F:bliki]** The bliki (distributed, with ward-cunningham): A hybrid of blog and wiki: write up an idea while figuring it out, post it publicly, keep revising, mark the maturity (`growing`, `stable`), attribute origins. The form Fowler's argument evolves in. Ward Cunningham named Fowler's version in 2003, and by Fowler's own account the word and the form were already in use elsewhere.
- **[F:append-dont-overwrite]** Event sourcing (distributed, with greg-young): Record every change to application state as an event, and rebuild the state at any time by replaying the log. Fowler wrote the pattern up in draft in 2005 and returned to it in 2017, comparing the event log to a git history and to the accounting transactions behind a balance. Greg Young popularized it alongside CQRS from 2007.
- **[L:1]** The 2014 microservices article and its 2015 follow-ups support two readings (structural): The article by James Lewis and Fowler closes on its own caution: "not enough time has passed for us to make a full judgement". In 2015 Fowler's bliki entry Microservice Premium took aim at teams "too eager to embrace microservices", and Monolith First reported that "Almost all the successful microservice stories have started with a monolith that got too big and was broken up". Neither entry calls itself a correction or a retraction. One reading is that the article undersold the operational cost of distribution and the entries correct it; the other is that they work out the caution the article already stated. Read the follow-ups and Sam Newman's 2nd-edition *Building Microservices* alongside the original article.
- **[L:2]** The catalog form can become an end in itself (structural): Joshua Kerievsky's *Refactoring to Patterns* is both extension and pointed critique: mechanical refactoring without a pattern-target can produce code that is cleaner-but-not-better. The 2nd edition of *Refactoring* (2018) responds, but the critique is live: a named move with no design destination is still motion without direction.
- **[L:3]** The test pyramid is a heuristic, not a law (local): Vladimir Khorikov, Sam Newman and others argue strict pyramid orthodoxy can starve integration testing where the actual bugs live. Treat the pyramid as the *shape* of a reasonable starting place, not as the answer to where test effort belongs.
- **[L:4]** The discipline-of-attribution scales unevenly to the security world (local): Fowler's attribution chains work because his community is small, stable, and largely self-citing. Security has weaker citation discipline, faster vocabulary drift, more vendor-driven naming, and a meaningful adversarial layer that Fowler's collaborative-design framing doesn't acknowledge. The cartographer move still applies; the cartographer's working norms partially don't.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** The cartographer move is the most under-acknowledged contribution of the Fowler corpus. Even with the open question over the 2014 microservices article, even with the test-pyramid orthodoxy risk, even with the catalog-as-end-in-itself failure mode. Turning tacit design knowledge into named, attributed, debatable distinctions is what makes engineering practice transmissible across companies, generations, and disciplines. That doesn't go away when individual chapters age out.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:kent-beck]** extends, primary → Kent Beck (has a page): The deepest collaborator. Credited on both editions of *Refactoring*, defined TDD, coined code smell while helping with that book, contributed the two-hat rule and the "make the change easy" motto. Most of what Fowler argues, he argues alongside Beck.
- **[N:kent-beck:thesis]** Kent Beck's thesis: Software costs what changing it costs. Beck states that as Constantine's Equivalence, cost(software) ~= cost(change), and credits the coupling and cohesion work in *Structured Design* for the underlying result. Coupling is then the thing to watch, because it is what makes one change drag others behind it, and design is whatever brings it down. The work happens in tidyings, small changes to structure that leave behavior alone. The two kinds of change are asymmetrical. A structure change can be undone, while a behavior change that has reached customers cannot, which is the reason his practices favor small steps with a test behind each one. Whether to do the structural work first is an investment question rather than a matter of virtue, and his own answer runs both ways: discounted cash flow argues for earning sooner and spending later, options argue for spending now, and the decision is the inequality between them.
- **[E:out:ward-cunningham]** antecedent, primary → Ward Cunningham (has a page): Coined "technical debt" in 1992 and built the wiki, the form Fowler extended into the bliki. The genre of public-revision design writing starts with Cunningham; Fowler made a career of it.
- **[N:ward-cunningham:thesis]** Ward Cunningham's thesis: Cunningham's subject is the distance between a program and what the people who own it currently understand about the problem. Code written to reflect a partial understanding is legitimate and useful in his account, so long as the understanding is allowed to sharpen and the code is rewritten promptly to match. What costs money is the gap left open. Every minute spent working against not-quite-right code is interest on that debt, and an entire engineering organization can be brought to a standstill under the load. The wiki is the same premise applied to prose. A group working out a shared understanding needs a medium cheap enough to revise that people who would not normally author will edit it, which is why he made the editing surface as thin as he could and delegated control to the people using it. Both positions rest on a repair step that somebody has to actually take, and on a community that can be trusted to take it.
- **[E:out:eric-evans]** extends, primary → Eric Evans: Fowler wrote the foreword to Evans's *Domain-Driven Design* (2003) and explains its bounded context on the bliki, citing the book as "the canonical source".
- **[E:out:alex-komoroske]** parallel, primary → Alex Komoroske (has a page): Same cartographer move, different domain. Fowler builds named-vocabulary instruments for software design, Komoroske builds them for organization design. Coordination headwind, Schelling-point organizations and gardener-vs-builder are the org-design equivalents of refactoring, bounded context, ubiquitous language.
- **[N:alex-komoroske:thesis]** Alex Komoroske's thesis: Complex systems work best when they are gardened, not architected. The leader's job is not to plan the optimal answer and propagate it downward; the leader's job is to make defensible answers focal enough that local actors converge on them without being told. Organizations that ignore this pay a tax that grows faster than they expect, sooner than they expect. This is coordination headwind. The standard responses (more meetings, more reorgs, more hiring) make it worse. The fix is to push decisions to where local information lives, accept the messiness, and trust that recovery time in a self-correcting system is faster than freeze-up time in a centralized one.
- **[E:out:christopher-alexander]** antecedent, secondary → Christopher Alexander (has a page): The pattern-language form Fowler's catalogs descend from. Alexander went back to the borrowers in 1996 and named what a pattern language was for besides the format, which is part of why Fowler's catalog discipline matters: naming is necessary and it is not sufficient.
- **[N:christopher-alexander:thesis]** Christopher Alexander's thesis: A pattern language was built to generate, and the mechanism is order. Each pattern states a problem and what Alexander calls a current best guess at what will resolve it, and the 253 in the 1977 book are meant to be worked through in sequence so that what comes out is coherent. His name for the instructions that do this is generative schemes, sets of steps that let a person or a group make a coherent artifact simply. The claim underneath is that coherence cannot be assembled out of parts chosen one at a time, however good each part is alone. Software took the pattern; whether it took the sequence he declined to rule on. At OOPSLA in 1996 he named three things a pattern language is for besides its format: a moral component, the aim of creating coherence, and generativity. He would not say they were missing here, only that he did not know whether they had been translated into the field. On the mechanism he was exact, quoting his own two books to say that the language is used sequentially and that in practice the instruction dropped out of sight and was not emphasized in use. That last sentence describes the fate of his own languages. It lays no charge against programmers. The charge is this page's inference from the two.
- **[E:out:melvin-conway]** antecedent, secondary → Melvin Conway: Conway's Law (1968), to which Fowler sets out three responses. The third is the *inverse Conway maneuver*, a term he credits to Jonny LeRoy and Matt Simons (2010). The boundary arc of Fowler's work runs through Conway's claim that org structure ships in the system structure.
- **[E:out:ray-dalio]** parallel, secondary → Ray Dalio (has a page): Dalio writes down an organization's decision rules the way Fowler writes down design vocabulary: explicit, named, and open to revision.
- **[N:ray-dalio:thesis]** Ray Dalio's thesis: Most consequential mistakes are made because the decision algorithm was tacit, contradictory, or unexamined. The fix is to write the algorithm down (as numbered principles), expose it to critique from anyone willing to engage seriously, weight people's input by their *demonstrated* track record on the question at hand, and treat painful evidence that the algorithm is wrong as the primary source of improvement. Most organizations can't do this because they cannot tolerate the radical transparency required to surface the disagreements honestly. Therefore most organizations make systematic mistakes they could have avoided.
- **[E:out:rich-hickey]** parallel, secondary → Rich Hickey (has a page): Hickey calls reliance on tests to make change safe "guardrail programming," a direct counterpoint to refactoring under tests. His accretion-only interfaces pair with Fowler's Tolerant Reader.
- **[N:rich-hickey:thesis]** Rich Hickey's thesis: Reliability depends on being able to understand a system, and people can only reason about a few things at a time, so the property that matters most is whether the parts of a system are intertwined. Simplicity in that sense is objective and chosen; ease is relative to the person and says nothing about how tangled the result is. Incidental complexity enters when teams pick familiar constructs (mutable objects, in-place updates, breaking version changes) that braid together things the problem kept separate. The remedy is to build from values, plain data and composable pieces, and to treat tests and type systems as safety nets that cannot make a tangled design simple. (The premise that simplicity is a prerequisite for reliability is Dijkstra's, quoted at the start of "Simple Made Easy.")
- **[E:out:fred-brooks]** antecedent, primary → Fred Brooks (has a page): Brooks named the large-scale physics of software engineering; Fowler spent decades naming the daily practice inside that frame.
- **[N:fred-brooks:thesis]** Fred Brooks's thesis: Software is fundamentally a communication problem dressed up as an engineering problem. The cost of building large systems is mostly the cost of coordinating the people building them; that cost grows superlinearly with team size and is bounded below by communication topology, not by individual programmer productivity. Therefore: adding people to a late project makes it later (Brooks's Law). Therefore: conceptual integrity is the single most important property of a system and must be defended by keeping the design in a small number of heads. Therefore: tools and methodologies that target *accidental* complexity (syntax, memory, type errors) can yield big productivity gains; tools and methodologies that promise to reduce *essential* complexity (the problem domain itself) are silver bullets and they do not exist.
- **[E:out:zhamak-dehghani]** hosts, secondary → Zhamak Dehghani: Fowler published Dehghani's original Data Mesh articles on martinfowler.com, where she first laid out the approach in writing.
- **[E:out:simon-willison]** parallel, primary → Simon Willison (has a page): A case where the boundary has nowhere to land. Fowler places the line where the language changes; Willison's subject is a model that reads its developer's instructions and outside text as one stream, with no internal seam to draw a line along. Willison never uses this page's vocabulary, so the pairing is the site's own.
- **[N:simon-willison:thesis]** Simon Willison's thesis: Prompt injection, in Willison's definition, is what happens when an application concatenates a developer's trusted instruction with untrusted text and the model acts on the combination. He treats that as the reason the defenses built for older injection attacks have no equivalent here: SQL injection has a known mitigation that is guaranteed to work, in escaping and quoting user-provided strings, and no such function exists for natural language. From there he draws a rule about what a fix may be made of. A hole patched with another model cannot be verified, because the model is a black box and an adversary keeps trying until something works, so the direction he trusts is architectural, keeping untrusted content away from anything that holds privilege. His claim about the state of the art is narrower than the rule, and he states it plainly: two and a half years into the problem he had seen alarmingly little progress toward a robust solution, and nobody yet knows how to prevent it 100 percent reliably.
- **[E:out:jez-humble]** parallel, primary → Jez Humble (has a page): Humble carries this page's build-side practices out to the far end of the route. Fowler's article on continuous integration sends readers to *Continuous Delivery* for the broader process. He published that book in his own signature series, and he wrote the bliki entries for the deployment pipeline and for continuous delivery with Humble's detailed help acknowledged on both.
- **[N:jez-humble:thesis]** Jez Humble's thesis: One route to production, taken by every change, is the claim underneath all of it. The artifact is built once and promoted through each environment, so what reaches production is the thing that was tested. Every stage is automated and every stage can reject the change, which makes the pipeline the release process itself instead of a document describing one. The discipline the 2010 book defines follows from that: keep the software in a releasable state continuously, and the question of when to release stops being an engineering question at all. In his account continuous delivery puts the release schedule in the hands of the business, which only works if any good build can go out on demand. The distinction that makes it possible is that deploying is not releasing. Code can run in production with no user able to reach it, and the two decisions can then be taken by different people, at different times, for different reasons. The principles underneath are borrowed and his own site says so, with Deming on building quality in and Toyota's jidoka and Taiichi Ohno's kaizen behind two of the others.
- **[E:out:gene-kim]** parallel, primary → Gene Kim (has a page): *Accelerate*, which Kim wrote with Nicole Forsgren and Jez Humble, is one of the books Fowler wrote a foreword for. Past that the two barely touch, and the gap is worth seeing. Kim's doctrine ships as a numbered set inside a novel or a handbook, which is a different way of putting vocabulary into other people's hands from the one this page describes. Same decade, same industry.
- **[N:gene-kim:thesis]** Gene Kim's thesis: One argument runs under all of it. Technology work is a system, and its performance is set by how work moves through it, how quickly the news of a problem comes back, and whether the people inside it are free to experiment. The Three Ways state that as a sequence. The First Way is put as the performance of the entire system rather than the performance of a specific silo, and Deming is credited inside it for always seeking to achieve profound understanding of the system. The Second Way is the right-to-left feedback loop, shortened and amplified so corrections can be made continually. The Third Way is a culture that fosters experimentation and learning from failure while treating repetition and practice as the prerequisite to mastery. The later sets come at the same claim from other sides, and both are lists of names before they are arguments. The Five Ideals read as the conditions the work is meant to meet, in an order that starts at locality and simplicity. The three mechanisms of the 2023 book answer a question about where problem solving should happen, and the book's answer is that an organization wins by moving it out of the zones where it is dangerous. The lean lineage is named by both authors in their own prefaces (Steven Spear and H. Kent Bowen's *Harvard Business Review* article decoding the Toyota Production System), and Goldratt's theory of constraints is credited inside the novel's own text.
- **[E:out:dan-north]** parallel, secondary → Daniel Terhorst-North (has a page): Two bliki entries credit work Terhorst-North shared with others. The Given/When/Then entry records the template as developed with Chris Matts and adds a footnote on the credit he gave Ivan Moore when reviewing it; the blue-green entry leaves the name to "some foggy combination" of him and Humble.
- **[N:dan-north:thesis]** Daniel Terhorst-North's thesis: The 2006 essay starts from the confusion he kept meeting while teaching test-driven development, and answers it with a change of vocabulary, from tests to behaviour. Test methods become sentences beginning "should," and the same behaviour vocabulary, carried into analysis with Chris Matts, lets a team state a story's acceptance criteria as a context, an event and an outcome before the work starts. The later essays hold related positions, one about planning and one about code. Deliberate Discovery argues that ignorance is "the single greatest impediment to throughput" and that a team should spend effort early finding out what it does not know, including what it does not know it does not know. CUPID, in his phrase "properties over principles," proposes five qualities of code in reply to SOLID. The site reads one thread through all three, that the words a team works in decide what it notices; he does not state it.
- **[E:out:dave-farley]** parallel, secondary → Dave Farley (has a page): Farley carries continuous integration past the build. His 2007 paper is subtitled "Extending the range of Continuous Integration", and it takes each check-in on through a series of gates toward release.
- **[N:dave-farley:thesis]** Dave Farley's thesis: Read across the 2007 paper and the 2021 book, the site takes the argument to be that every change should produce evidence about whether the software works, early enough to act on. The 2007 paper applies that to release. A check-in starts a release candidate through a series of gates, and the same binary is meant to run in every environment, so that each gate is testing the thing that would ship. The 2021 book widens it into a definition of the discipline, "the application of an empirical, scientific approach to finding efficient, economic solutions to practical problems in software", and divides the skill in two: learning, through iteration, feedback, incrementalism and experiment, and managing complexity, through modularity, cohesion and coupling. The measures he uses to judge the outcome, stability and throughput, come from *Accelerate*, and he sends readers there for them.

## Shared concepts (frames other pages also hold)

- **[S:code-smell:kent-beck]** Kent Beck also holds Code smell: A surface sign in code that points at a deeper design problem. Fowler's account is unambiguous about where the term came from: "The term was first coined by Kent Beck while helping me with my Refactoring book." Fowler is the one who cataloged the smells and his page carries this frame in the foreground, which is why it sits in the deep tier here.
- **[S:append-dont-overwrite:rich-hickey]** Rich Hickey also holds Place-oriented programming (PLOP): Any design in which new information replaces old information in place. A habit born of early machines with little memory, kept long after the limits disappeared, at the cost of losing facts. The name is Hickey's; the critique of fetch-and-store programming goes back at least to Backus's 1977 Turing lecture and the functional programming tradition.
- **[C:append-dont-overwrite]** Concept page Append, Don't Overwrite: Record each change as a new entry and derive the current state from the accumulated history, instead of replacing old values in place. The present becomes a view over the record, and the record is what the system keeps.

## Lens: Julie's published positions

The extension must connect to at least one of these, or say plainly that none applies.

- **[P:1]** Prompt injection is architecturally unsolvable — mitigate, don't solve.
- **[P:2]** Security as emergent property, not imposed constraint. Make secure behavior the Schelling point.
- **[P:3]** Boundary enforcement over code inspection — capability-based security at infrastructure level scales where code review does not.
- **[P:4]** AI Comprehension Crisis — code generation outpaces understanding at 2.5:1; the enterprise that maintains semantic comprehension of its software estate has structural advantage.
- **[P:5]** Five Attack Surface Expansions — democratized builders + public APIs + agent access + autonomous reasoning + LLM workflows = combinatorial, not additive, threat surface.
- **[P:6]** Software Factory Ontology — specs as ontology objects, security constraints as part of the spec, end-to-end auditability.
- **[P:7]** Confused deputy problem (Hardy, 1988) is the correct framing for MCP/agent authorization failures.
- **[P:8]** "It's gotta be tough for all the right reasons." Criteria for meaningful work: autonomy, difficulty, people, mission.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:kent-beck]** Beck's subject is the price of the next change, which Constantine's Equivalence states as cost(software) ~= cost(change). Coupling is what makes that price high, so design is whatever brings it down. The work happens in tidyings, small changes to structure that leave behavior alone. The two kinds are asymmetrical: a helper extracted can be inlined again, and a behavior change that reached customers cannot. Small steps with a test behind each one follow from that asymmetry, and he calls the test-first technique a rediscovery whose limits he states himself. His answer to whether to tidy first is an inequality rather than a rule, and it sometimes comes out no. Extreme Programming is the method he wrote up in 1999, out of the way of working a payroll team adopted in 1997. His 3X phases add the axis the others lack, since a practice that fits a product being explored is waste once it is being extracted.
- **[R:ward-cunningham]** Cunningham puts a price on the gap between a program and what its owning team now understands about the problem. Writing code to a partial understanding is legitimate in his account, and the interest is the friction of working against the gap until it is rewritten. The wiki applies the same premise to prose, with an editing surface thin enough that people who would not normally author will revise what is there. What the site takes is the pattern across the vocabulary. Two phrases he coined reached practitioners with the condition stripped: debt as permission to write bad code, the simplest thing as a command. Cunningham's Law carries his name and was never his. He answered the first two on the record, and his own wiki's community posted a warning above the phrase for later arrivals. The correction reached fewer people than the phrase each time. Vocabulary that needs a warning label has stopped belonging to its author.
- **[R:alex-komoroske]** Komoroske's central claim is that coordination has a cost most organizations never see: every decision depends on agreements, and the drag of those agreements grows faster than headcount. Brooks named the same cost for software teams in 1975, and Komoroske extends it to all collaborative work. His answer is a change of leadership stance, from architect to gardener, in which the leader sets conditions that make good local choices the obvious ones. The heroics smell is the diagnostic that goes with it: a system that needs heroes to keep working is already broken. The frame earns its place by making costs an organization was paying invisibly show up where they can be counted.
- **[R:christopher-alexander]** Sequence is the working part of a pattern language. Each of the 253 patterns in the 1977 book states a problem and a current best guess at its resolution, and in order they are meant to compose into a whole. He calls the instructions that do that generative schemes. Of his own languages he said at OOPSLA in 1996 that the sequential instruction was not emphasized in use. That gap is between a language and a catalogue: the same entries, without the mechanism. The catalogue is what got copied into practice, from the 1987 report through Design Patterns into fields with no buildings. On whether his condition carries across he stopped short, saying he does not know whether the moral component exists in computer science. That leaves the order claim, which any library of patterns can be asked about.
- **[R:ray-dalio]** Dalio argues that most consequential mistakes come from decision rules that were tacit, contradictory, or never examined. His remedy is the principles document: numbered, written-down rules that anyone may challenge and that get revised when they produce bad outcomes. Radical transparency and believability-weighted decisions support the document, the first by exposing rationales and the second by weighting each person's input by their track record on that kind of question. The personal version is pain plus reflection equals progress. Reporting on Bridgewater describes a daily experience far harsher than the document's ideal, which makes the form and its implementation worth judging separately.
- **[R:rich-hickey]** "Simple Made Easy" pulls apart two words the industry treats as one: simple means nothing is interleaved, a property of the artifact, and easy means near at hand, a property of the person. Hickey locates complexity in complecting, the braiding together of concerns that could stay independent. A familiar tool can bring complexity the problem never needed, which he calls incidental complexity, his name for what Brooks called accidental. Tests and type checkers catch mistakes after the fact, and in his account they are safety nets that cannot untangle a design. The later talks carry the same concern into data and change. Overwriting a record in place discards the fact it replaced, and removing anything from a published interface breaks whoever depends on it. He scopes these arguments to long-running information systems, and his case against static types remains contested.
- **[R:fred-brooks]** For Brooks, software is a communication problem before it is an engineering problem, and the cost of coordinating the people building a system grows faster than the team. Brooks's Law follows directly: adding people to a late project makes it later. No Silver Bullet sharpens the claim by separating essential complexity, which lives in the problem, from accidental complexity, which the tools add. Tooling can remove accidental complexity, sometimes dramatically, while no single development shrinks the essential part by an order of magnitude. The prediction dates from 1986 and still stands, which is why the vocabulary carries weight in any honest conversation about engineering.
- **[R:simon-willison]** A single channel carries the developer's trusted instruction and untrusted text, and the model acts on the combination; that join is the problem Willison works. He proposed the name in September 2022, in a post documenting Riley Goodside's demonstration, four months after @himbodhisattva used the phrase and Preamble disclosed it as command injection. Five days later he ruled out defenses built from another model, which cannot be shown to hold against an attacker who retries. His answers are structural: a privileged model holding the tools, kept apart from a quarantined model that reads untrusted content, with ordinary software between them. The lethal trifecta names a configuration: three capabilities each ordinary alone and dangerous together. He polices the term afterwards, ruling that without a concatenation of trusted and untrusted strings it is not prompt injection. Most of the blog is links and quotes carrying other people's work; the authored spine is the twenty-three-post series behind these frames.
- **[R:jez-humble]** The 2006 paper argues that a change should take exactly one route to production, and every other claim here follows from it, the discipline of the 2010 book included. That is what makes the discipline work: software kept releasable at all times, with any good build deployable on demand. One distinction inside the discipline is smaller and separable: deploying code and releasing a feature are two decisions, so code can run in production with nobody able to reach it. Almost none of it is his alone and he is usually the one saying so: Chris Read and Dan North on the pipeline paper, Dave Farley on the book, Deming and Toyota behind the principles. What is his is the writing down: the paper set an existing practice out in an order other people could follow.
- **[R:gene-kim]** The Three Ways were the first of Kim's numbered sets, posted in August 2012. The later sets arrive as named lists, the Five Ideals from the solo book and the post naming them, and three mechanisms with Steven Spear the book says move problem solving out of high-risk zones. Each of the sets comes at one claim, that technology work is a system whose performance is set by flow, feedback and experiment. The distribution is the second contribution: a novel, a summit he has run since 2014, and a handbook crediting the 2012 post for the frame.
- **[R:dan-north]** Terhorst-North's part in behaviour-driven development is a change of vocabulary, made while teaching test-driven development: think in behaviour instead of tests. Most pieces came from other people, and his 2006 essay names them: Chris Stevenson's agiledox, a "should" convention he came across, Chris Matts on analysis, Eric Evans's ubiquitous language. With Matts he wrote the template Gherkin now uses as step keywords, which states a story's acceptance criteria as a context, an event and an outcome before the work starts. Deliberate Discovery turns to planning: ignorance is the constraint on delivery, including the ignorance a team does not know it has, so finding it out is work to schedule early. As Mark Needham quotes it, Phillip Armour's 2000 paper had already named a second order of ignorance. CUPID, grown from a 2016 talk against SOLID, answers SOLID with five properties of code. The two signed critiques found answer that talk, and neither addresses CUPID or BDD.
- **[R:dave-farley]** Two pieces of Farley's work stand fourteen years apart. The 2007 paper sets out the deployment pipeline: a release candidate started at each check-in and carried through a sequence of gates. The idea is older than the paper, and Farley is one of several parents on the record. The 2021 book defines software engineering as an empirical, scientific approach to practical problems, and divides the work into learning and managing complexity. Chris James teaches the layered acceptance test, specifications on top and drivers underneath, and credits it to Farley by name; its driver layer already appears in the 2010 book by Humble and Farley.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> In Fowler's account, software is a medium for shared understanding, and it decays whenever nobody tends it. The cost of that decay arrives quickly, in next week's velocity, and his design stamina hypothesis makes a parallel claim that internal quality pays back within weeks. His method is to write the moves down by name (refactoring, code smell, strangler fig, bounded context), attribute each to its origin, and revise them in public on the bliki. Naming turns tacit design choices into distinctions a team can argue about. His 2014 microservices article with James Lewis said it was too early for a full judgement, and his 2015 entries warned against teams too eager to adopt; the record supports reading those entries either as a correction of the article or as its stated caution worked out.

> Bounded context places a boundary where the language changes. That line is often a sensible place to draw an authority boundary as well, although in multi-tenant systems contexts and authority domains can cut across each other. The confused deputy problem is what happens when a request crosses an authority boundary carrying authority it gathered on the other side. Agent and tool integrations make this concrete: an agent working inside one context can be steered by text that came from another, and it acts with its own permissions. A trust boundary placed at the same seam as a bounded context gives a security team a concrete place to enforce authority at the infrastructure layer. The placement secures nothing until enforcement is wired to it, since a named boundary with nothing behind it is only a label. Security vocabulary drifts faster and is more vendor-driven than design vocabulary, so the naming discipline has to be kept on purpose.
