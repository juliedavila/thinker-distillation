## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** In Leveson's account, safety is an emergent property of a system's interacting components, and reliability is a property of one component measured against its specification. The two come apart in system accidents, where parts that worked as specified combine into a hazardous state. Of one such loss, a reactor example, her 2004 paper says "Merely increasing the reliability of the individual components or protecting against their failure would not have prevented the loss." By the same paper's account, event models and analysis methods "that focus on classic types of failure events will not apply to software." STAMP treats an accident as a failure to enforce a constraint, asks why the control structure was ineffective, and puts prevention into designing a control structure that enforces the necessary constraints across development and operation (a model built, by her own credit, on Rasmussen and Svedung's framework and on Ashby's control concepts).
- **[T:working-line]** Safety is a control problem: an accident is a constraint the system did not enforce, and parts that each worked as specified can still combine into a loss. (Paraphrased from "A New Accident Model for Engineering Safer Systems", 2004.)
- **[F:stamp]** STAMP (Systems-Theoretic Accident Model and Processes) (extended, with jens-rasmussen, inge-svedung): An accident model in which safety is a control problem and "the most basic concept" is "a constraint, rather than an event": losses come from inadequate control or enforcement of safety-related constraints, including losses in which no component failed. In the paper's Mars Polar Lander example, one of the system accidents it names, "the software did not adequately control the descent speed of the spacecraft", having "misinterpreted noise from a Hall effect sensor" as a sign that the spacecraft had landed. The 2004 paper says it is built on Rasmussen and Svedung's framework, takes its control concepts from Ashby, Conant and Ashby, and Checkland, and credits the term system accident to Perrow. It cites a 2003 STAMP paper with Daouk, Dulac and Marais as submitted.
- **[F:stpa]** STPA (System-Theoretic Process Analysis) (co-authored, with nicolas-dulac, john-thomas; derived from stamp): A hazard analysis built on STAMP. The analyst models the system as a hierarchical control structure of feedback loops, then looks for unsafe control actions, which the handbook defines as control actions that "in a particular context and worst-case environment, will lead to a hazard." Each controller acts on a process model, the term the handbook uses for its internal beliefs about what it controls. Feedback may update that model in part, and a process model inconsistent with reality can lead to unsafe control actions. It can start in early concept analysis, where it helps identify safety requirements and constraints. A 2022 scoping review finds the acronym in a 2005 paper by Leveson and Nicolas Dulac, there derived from "STamP Analysis"; the handbook she wrote with John Thomas came in 2018.
- **[F:cast]** CAST (Causal Analysis based on System Theory) (extended; derived from stamp): Accident analysis built on STAMP. Her 2019 handbook says it "does not attempt to assign blame" and looks for why the structures in place to prevent the events "were not successful", and its recommendations focus on strengthening those control structures. Google SRE's account of adopting STAMP names it, with STPA, among the methods its teams use, and Google SRE's STAMP page lists a talk on a CAST analysis of a Google Maps incident.
- **[F:stpa-sec]** STPA-Sec (co-authored, with nancy-leveson; derived from stpa): William Young and Leveson's extension of STPA "to include security analysis" (Communications of the ACM, 2014). It reframes security as a general loss-prevention problem, regardless of the actor's intent, and holds that focusing on adversaries "too early in the process" limits a security assessment's strategic value. The paper says it is "only now being applied to cybersecurity problems, but is showing promise in these case studies."
- **[F:safety-iii]** Safety-III (extended): Her 2020 label for a systems approach to safety, set against Erik Hollnagel's Safety-II. By her own account the approach "is not new", and Safety-III "incorporates and extends" the System Safety practice she dates to at least World War II.
- **[L:1]** Hochstein doubts the whole control model can be drawn for a system in constant flux like his (structural): STPA needs a control structure someone can draw and hazards a design can control. Lorin Hochstein, writing from software operations, doubts that anyone can sketch the whole structure of a system "in constant flux" like his, or control every hazard where he works, while endorsing the systems view and expecting a partial analysis to be useful (his card below). He also calls it "too simplistic" that STAMP models people the same way as every other controller. Every case this page names from her work comes from a designed system, among them TCAS II, the Milstar satellite lost on a Titan IV launch, and Therac-25. A team running production software should read a finished control structure as a picture of one moment, and a short list of unsafe control actions as a bound on the analysis.
- **[L:2]** Google's account of STPA is a self-report with no measured comparison (local): Google SRE's account, by Tim Falzone and Ben Treynor Sloss, is a practitioner report in its authors' own words. It reports what STPA found and what it cost, and it reports no other method run on the same systems to compare against.
- **[L:3]** STPA-Sec puts the attacker late, and its authors call it early work (structural): William Young and Leveson frame security as loss prevention whatever the actor's intent, and argue that attention to adversaries "too early in the process" limits the assessment. In 2014 they wrote that it was "only now being applied to cybersecurity problems, but is showing promise in these case studies." On this site's reading, an adaptive attacker aims at the control structure and at the controller's model of the process, so the deferred step carries more weight in security than in safety.
- **[L:4]** By her own account, Safety-III is not a new approach (local): Her 2020 paper says the approach "is not new" and that Safety-III "incorporates and extends" System Safety practice. Hollnagel's unpublished reply (his card below) answers the paper. Read Safety-III as her case about what safety engineering has done at least since World War II, and look to STAMP, STPA and CAST for the method.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** The question STAMP asks of a loss survives the limits above. By her 2004 account most software-related accidents had been system accidents, which "stem from the operation of the software, not from its lack of operation".

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:jens-rasmussen]** antecedent, primary → Jens Rasmussen (has a page): The 2004 paper calls Rasmussen and Svedung's 2000 framework the one "upon which STAMP is built", and says their model "contains only one control structure and the focus is on operations and not development". STAMP carries constraints into development and design as well.
- **[N:jens-rasmussen:thesis]** Jens Rasmussen's thesis: Rasmussen read the court reports from Bhopal, Flixborough, Zeebrügge and Chernobyl as showing that those accidents "have not been caused by a coincidence of independent failures and human errors, but by a systematic migration of organisational behaviour toward accident under the influence of pressure toward cost-effectiveness". Task analysis built on action sequences and occasional errors should therefore give way, he argued, to a model of "work system constraints, boundaries of acceptable performance, and subjective criteria guiding adaptation to change", with risk management treated as a control problem across the disciplines that shape the work. Improvement is aimed at the boundary: identify it, make it visible to the people who work near it, and give them the chance to learn to cope with it, on his view that "operation close to known boundaries may be safer than requiring excessive margins". His 1990 paper on causality gives the investigative side: what an analysis accepts as a cause depends on stop rules set by its aim, and errors as "a separate class of behaviour" grow harder to pick out in modern work.
- **[E:out:erik-hollnagel]** critiques, primary → Erik Hollnagel (has a page): "The Folly of Safety-III" (2025), an unpublished manuscript under his byline whose file metadata names David Slater, answers her 2020 paper by name. It says that of three writers on Safety-III "only Leveson (2020) attempted to 'define' Safety-III", and reads the goal she sets for it as "essentially my definition of Safety-I".
- **[N:erik-hollnagel:thesis]** Erik Hollnagel's thesis: Hollnagel argues that safety has traditionally been defined by what goes wrong, and that this leaves the object of study too small. Failures are rare, and the same adjustments that let work succeed under changing conditions are what occasionally let it fail. So the useful question for safety management is how things usually go right, and an investigation explains the rare failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism. People cannot be as efficient as possible and as thorough as possible at once, the trade between the two is normal, and the shortcuts that make work efficient are the same ones that sometimes let a failure through.
- **[E:out:lorin-hochstein]** critiques, secondary → Lorin Hochstein: "Thoughts on STAMP" (2020), from a software-operations seat, likes "taking a systems view" and the "sharp distinction between reliability and safety". It objects that STAMP models humans like any other controller, that no group of engineers knows enough to sketch the whole control model of his own system, which is "in constant flux", and that not every hazard can be controlled "in the context that I work in."
- **[E:out:ben-treynor-sloss]** extends, secondary → Ben Treynor Sloss: Second author, after Tim Falzone, of an account of Google SRE adopting STAMP, which describes STPA for hazard analysis and CAST for post-incident investigation. - **[E:out:james-reason]** parallel, secondary → James Reason (has a page): The STPA Handbook offers STAMP as the alternative to the chain-of-failure-events model, "dominos or Swiss cheese slices, all of which are essentially equivalent". The passage does not name Reason.
- **[N:james-reason:thesis]** James Reason's thesis: On Reason's account, accidents in complex systems come from unsafe acts at the front line triggering latent conditions that earlier decisions by designers and managers put in place (an observation he credited to Barry Turner and Charles Perrow), and defences fail when their weaknesses coincide. On that account, error management that looks only at the person who erred misses where the conditions came from, and in his 1997 words "identifying and eliminating latent conditions proactively still offer the best routes to improving system fitness." The argument extends to culture. A safe culture, in his 1998 argument, is an informed one, which depends on people reporting, which rests on a just culture in which the line between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour is clearly drawn; he rejected the blanket "no blame" alternative as neither feasible nor desirable.
- **[E:out:david-woods]** parallel, secondary → David Woods (has a page): One of her two co-editors, with Erik Hollnagel, on *Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts* (2006), the anthology documenting the 2004 workshop that Hollnagel dates the field to.
- **[N:david-woods:thesis]** David Woods's thesis: Complex systems are not stable artifacts; they are continuously adapting joint cognitive systems made up of humans, organizations, and machines. Their survival depends not on being robust to known threats (that property is locally useful but globally fragile), but on having reserves of adaptive capacity that humans supply in real time when surprises arrive. Every optimization push consumes that capacity; performance-pressured systems operate closer to their limits and absorb less surprise. Graceful extensibility (the ability to stretch performance under previously-unimagined conditions) is what you actually want, and it is exactly the property that efficiency-focused management most reliably destroys.
- **[E:out:inge-svedung]** antecedent, secondary → Inge Svedung: Co-author with Rasmussen of the 2000 risk-management framework that her 2004 paper says STAMP is built on.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:jens-rasmussen]** Rasmussen gives one account of how work moves before an accident and another of how the analysis stops after one. Workers follow an effort gradient, management supplies a cost gradient, and together they very likely produce a systematic migration toward the boundary of acceptable performance. Under that pressure defences in depth are likely to degenerate over time, and in his account the accidents he studied were caused by the migration, with a coincidence of independent failures and human errors ruled out. He aims improvement at the boundary and away from errors: make it visible to the people who work near it, and control performance where work meets it. The analysis afterwards typically ends by stop rules that are rarely stated: the causal trail runs out, a familiar abnormal event explains enough, or a cure is available. Which rule ends it depends on whether the analysis means to explain the events, to allocate responsibility and blame or to improve the system.
- **[R:erik-hollnagel]** Hollnagel moves the object of safety management from the rare accident to the ordinary working day. Safety-I counts adverse outcomes and aims to minimise them, which confines its evidence to rare events. Safety-II asks how work usually succeeds under varying conditions, and explains the occasional failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism: nobody can be as efficient and as thorough as possible at once, and trading one for the other is normal work. He catalogues the shortcuts that trade produces, among them "Looks fine" and "Has been checked by someone else". In his account, those shortcuts are how the work gets done and also what occasionally lets a failure through. The contrast is disputed at its root: Leveson says the Safety-I he describes bears little resemblance to safety engineering as practised, and Cooper groups Safety-II with other approaches into a New View whose propositions he calls untested.
- **[R:james-reason]** Reason separates active failures, the unsafe acts at a system's front line, from latent conditions, the "resident pathogens" left in place by earlier decisions. His co-authored history calls the observation behind the pair "nothing new", crediting Barry Turner and Charles Perrow. He and John Wreathall drew the account as five planes, which later became slices of Swiss cheese, a label someone else supplied; an accident needs their holes to align. In 1997 latent failures became latent conditions, because "a condition is not a cause, but it is necessary for a causal factor to have an impact." He argued that an organisation learns of its hazards through its people's reports, and that reporting rests on a just culture that separates acceptable from unacceptable behaviour. He questioned the model's use, writing in 1997 that the search for distant contributions may have gone too far and asking in a 2004 talk whether it was past its sell-by date.
- **[R:david-woods]** Woods describes complex systems as joint cognitive systems, in which people and automation do the thinking together and failures live in the joint between them. Such systems survive surprises through adaptive capacity: reserves that people and organizational arrangements supply, and that stay invisible until they are needed. The Law of Stretched Systems explains why those reserves shrink: slack looks like inefficiency, so every system gets pushed toward the edge of its envelope, where small disturbances produce large failures. Graceful extensibility, the ability to stretch under conditions nobody designed for, is the property resilience engineering aims at. The original papers are dense, which is why carriers such as Allspaw brought the vocabulary into software.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Leveson asks what a system failed to enforce, and sets STAMP against what the STPA Handbook calls "chain-of-failure-events" models. In STAMP, which she says is built on Rasmussen and Svedung's framework, the basic unit is a constraint, and an accident comes from inadequate control or enforcement of safety-related constraints, even when every component worked as specified. By her 2004 account most software-related accidents had been of this kind. In her view, safety belongs to the interacting system, and reliability to one component judged against its specification. STPA can apply the model from early concept work: draw the control structure, then search for control actions that are unsafe in a particular context. CAST applies it after a loss, asking why the preventive structures did not work, without trying to assign blame. William Young and Leveson extend STPA to security, treating a loss alike whatever the actor intended and deferring the adversary on purpose.

> Access provisioning is a controller in STPA's terms, and each grant is one of its control actions. Granting access can turn unsafe when the controller's process model of the requester drifts from the requester's actual standing, so that model, and the feedback that sets it, belongs in the spec beside the action. Her Mars Polar Lander example, a system accident in her terms, has that shape: the software took sensor noise for a landing. A nightly employment feed or an approver since reassigned leaves every component within specification while the controller grants what it should refuse, and an attacker who can delay or forge the feedback chooses when the gap opens. The audit record needs the constraint, the feedback that said it held, and who could write that feedback. Hochstein doubts anyone can sketch the whole control structure in a context like his, so the record covers only the provisioning paths someone drew.
