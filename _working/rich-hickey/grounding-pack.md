## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Reliability depends on being able to understand a system, and people can only reason about a few things at a time, so the property that matters most is whether the parts of a system are intertwined. Simplicity in that sense is objective and chosen; ease is relative to the person and says nothing about how tangled the result is. Incidental complexity enters when teams pick familiar constructs (mutable objects, in-place updates, breaking version changes) that braid together things the problem kept separate. The remedy is to build from values, plain data and composable pieces, and to treat tests and type systems as safety nets that cannot make a tangled design simple. (The premise that simplicity is a prerequisite for reliability is Dijkstra's, quoted at the start of "Simple Made Easy.")
- **[T:working-line]** Complecting things is the source of complexity. ("Simple Made Easy," Strange Loop, 2011.)
- **[F:simple-versus-easy]** Simple versus easy (original, with edsger-dijkstra): Simple means one fold or braid, with no interleaving, and can be checked in the artifact itself. Easy means near at hand, familiar to the person using it, and is relative. Treating the two words as synonyms is how teams choose complexity without noticing. Builds on Dijkstra's "simplicity is prerequisite for reliability," which the talk opens by quoting.
- **[F:complect]** Complect (original): An archaic English word for braiding or interleaving, revived for software: to complect is to entwine concerns that could stay independent, which is where complexity comes from. Its opposite is compose, to place together. Overlaps the older ideas of coupling and connascence.
- **[F:incidental-complexity]** Incidental complexity (extended, with fred-brooks, ben-moseley-peter-marks; derived from essential-vs-accidental-complexity): Complexity that was no part of what the user asked for and arrived with a chosen tool or construct. Hickey's name for what Brooks called accidental complexity, and what Moseley and Marks's "Out of the Tar Pit" traced mainly to mutable state; his gloss is that "incidental is Latin for 'your fault'."
- **[F:append-dont-overwrite]** Place-oriented programming (PLOP) (extended, with john-backus): Any design in which new information replaces old information in place. A habit born of early machines with little memory, kept long after the limits disappeared, at the cost of losing facts. The name is Hickey's; the critique of fetch-and-store programming goes back at least to Backus's 1977 Turing lecture and the functional programming tradition.
- **[F:accretion-versus-breakage]** Accretion versus breakage (extended, with martin-fowler): A published interface can grow by providing more (accretion) or requiring less (relaxation). Removing anything is breakage. When a change must break, give it a new name instead of a new version number. Related to Postel's robustness principle and Fowler's Tolerant Reader, and argued against Semantic Versioning's habit of breaking under the same name.
- **[F:guardrail-programming]** Guardrail programming (original): Relying on tests, refactoring tools and type checkers to make change safe, like driving by bouncing off guardrails. Those tools are safety nets; none of them makes a tangled program simple, so the ability to reason about the program still has to come from the design.
- **[F:value-identity-state-time]** Value, identity, state and time (extended, with alfred-north-whitehead): A value is an immutable magnitude; an identity is an entity associated with a series of values over time; state is the value of an identity at a moment. Separating them replaces mutable objects. Hickey credits Alfred North Whitehead's process philosophy as the inspiration.
- **[F:situated-programs]** Situated programs (extended, with meir-lehman): Programs that run continuously for years, deal mostly with information, remember what they did, and face real-world irregularity. The kind of system Hickey built for two decades, and the scope within which his arguments about data and types are made. The name is Hickey's. The distinction is older: Meir Lehman's E-type programs (defined in 1974) are software embedded in a real-world activity that must keep changing with it, set against programs written to an exact specification. The talk does not cite Lehman, and the record does not show whether Hickey knew his work.
- **[F:hammock-driven-development]** Hammock-driven development (extended, with george-polya): Load a problem into the waking mind with study and writing, then step away and let the background mind work on it. A programmer's name for the incubation stage of problem solving; the talk credits Polya's *How to Solve It* for treating problem solving as a practiced skill.
- **[F:persistent-data-structures]** Persistent data structures as the default (extended, with driscoll-sarnak-sleator-tarjan, phil-bagwell, chris-okasaki): Immutable collections that share structure so that making a changed copy is cheap, used as the language's everyday data. Driscoll, Sarnak, Sleator and Tarjan (1989) gave generic techniques for making linked structures persistent. Hickey's history says the in-place updating their fat node method needs to reach its advertised bounds made it impractical for multithreaded use. Clojure's versions build on Phil Bagwell's hash array mapped tries after Okasaki's structures proved too slow for Hickey's needs.
- **[L:1]** The case against static types is contested (structural): "Effective Programs" and "Maybe Not" argue that type systems do little for the information systems Hickey builds. Jonathan Lange's published response to "Maybe Not" disputes the talk's account of sum types, argues that Wadler's "Theorems for Free!" shows how much a type signature communicates, and says the talk ignores the type theory literature. Hickey scopes his claims to situated programs, and the scope matters: the argument does not settle the question for other kinds of software.
- **[L:2]** The evidence is one practitioner's experience (structural): The arguments rest on Hickey's own systems and his reading of what went wrong in them. The talks offer definitions, examples and a table of simpler replacements for complex constructs, but no measurement. The toolkit table itself arrives without argument in the talk; he says he did not label the columns good and bad and leaves the reader to.
- **[L:3]** Simple is objective, and the cost of getting there is relative (local): The talk defines simplicity as a property of the artifact and ease as a property of the person, then tells teams to choose simple. For a team whose members find the simpler constructs unfamiliar, the relative cost is real and falls on people the definition sets aside. The distinction explains the trade-off; it does not price it.
- **[L:4]** A singular vision has a governance cost (local): Hickey's history of Clojure says he prioritized "maintaining my vision for Clojure" over collaborative development, "for good and bad." His 2018 essay "Open Source is Not About You," which calls open source "a licensing and delivery mechanism, period," drew wide public criticism. The coherence of the language and the friction with contributors come from the same choice.
- **[L:5]** Permanent history meets retention limits (local): Accumulating facts instead of overwriting them means old records never age out on their own. Security and access records usually survive erasure requests, since data protection law such as GDPR exempts processing needed for legal obligations and legal claims, and treats network security as a legitimate interest. They still fall under storage limitation, which allows identifiable data to be kept only as long as its purpose requires. Datomic itself ships excision, a way to permanently remove facts for privacy or past a retention period, and keeps a record of each removal.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** The vocabulary survives its limits because the core observation holds in any codebase: things braided together have to be understood together, and familiarity hides the braid. The typing argument can stay contested and the evidence can stay anecdotal while "is this complected?" remains a sharper review question than "is this clean?" Accretion versus breakage holds up for a similar reason. Anyone who has upgraded a dependency across a major version has paid for breakage that arrived under a name they already trusted.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:fred-brooks]** antecedent, primary → Fred Brooks (has a page): Incidental complexity is Hickey's name for the accidental complexity Brooks separated from essential complexity in "No Silver Bullet," with the blame placed squarely on the choices a team makes.
- **[N:fred-brooks:thesis]** Fred Brooks's thesis: Software is fundamentally a communication problem dressed up as an engineering problem. The cost of building large systems is mostly the cost of coordinating the people building them; that cost grows superlinearly with team size and is bounded below by communication topology, not by individual programmer productivity. Therefore: adding people to a late project makes it later (Brooks's Law). Therefore: conceptual integrity is the single most important property of a system and must be defended by keeping the design in a small number of heads. Therefore: tools and methodologies that target *accidental* complexity (syntax, memory, type errors) can yield big productivity gains; tools and methodologies that promise to reduce *essential* complexity (the problem domain itself) are silver bullets and they do not exist.
- **[E:out:martin-fowler]** parallel, primary → Martin Fowler (has a page): Fowler treats tests as what makes refactoring safe; Hickey calls reliance on tests guardrail programming. On change, Fowler's Tolerant Reader (2011) is the consumer-side counterpart to Hickey's accretion-only interfaces.
- **[N:martin-fowler:thesis]** Martin Fowler's thesis: Software is a perishable medium for shared understanding. It rots when nobody is tending it, and the cost of letting it rot is paid not in some distant future but in next week's velocity. The way to keep software cheap to change is to evolve practice in public: name the moves, share the vocabulary, run the build-test-deploy loop tight, place the boundaries where change wants to flow, model the domain in the language the team already uses with the customer, and write the practice down as you discover it. Quality IS speed. Naming the implicit is how the argument becomes negotiable. The catalog, the bliki entry, the pattern, the named refactoring, the smell: these are the cartographer's instruments.
- **[E:out:ben-moseley-peter-marks]** antecedent, secondary → Ben Moseley and Peter Marks: "Out of the Tar Pit" (2006) traced most accidental complexity to mutable state and proposed functional and relational remedies. Hickey's history of Clojure describes Datomic as the final piece needed to get out of that tar pit.
- **[E:out:alfred-north-whitehead]** antecedent, secondary → Alfred North Whitehead: The process philosopher whose account of time as a succession of events Hickey credits as the inspiration for separating value, identity and state.
- **[E:out:jonathan-lange]** critiques, secondary → Jonathan Lange: A direct 2018 response to "Maybe Not," arguing that the talk is partly wrong about sum types, understates what type signatures communicate, and ignores the type theory literature.
- **[E:out:john-ousterhout]** parallel, secondary → John Ousterhout: *A Philosophy of Software Design* (2018) also treats complexity as the central problem of software design, with a different vocabulary built around deep modules and strategic programming.
- **[E:out:simon-willison]** parallel, primary → Simon Willison (has a page): Guardrail programming carried into security. Willison rules out defenses built from a second model because a black box cannot be shown to hold against an attacker who keeps retrying, which leaves the property to be designed in rather than caught on the way out. He does not cite Hickey, so the pairing is the site's reading of two arguments with one shape.
- **[N:simon-willison:thesis]** Simon Willison's thesis: Prompt injection, in Willison's definition, is what happens when an application concatenates a developer's trusted instruction with untrusted text and the model acts on the combination. He treats that as the reason the defenses built for older injection attacks have no equivalent here: SQL injection has a known mitigation that is guaranteed to work, in escaping and quoting user-provided strings, and no such function exists for natural language. From there he draws a rule about what a fix may be made of. A hole patched with another model cannot be verified, because the model is a black box and an adversary keeps trying until something works, so the direction he trusts is architectural, keeping untrusted content away from anything that holds privilege. His claim about the state of the art is narrower than the rule, and he states it plainly: two and a half years into the problem he had seen alarmingly little progress toward a robust solution, and nobody yet knows how to prevent it 100 percent reliably.
- **[E:out:kent-beck]** parallel, primary → Kent Beck (has a page): Beck reaches this page's conclusion from money rather than from meaning. Where Hickey argues that complecting independent concerns is what makes a program hard to reason about, Beck writes that the cost of software is approximately the cost of changing it, and puts coupling in the place where that cost accumulates. Neither man cites the other, and the two arguments arrive at the same advice about where design effort goes.
- **[N:kent-beck:thesis]** Kent Beck's thesis: Software costs what changing it costs. Beck states that as Constantine's Equivalence, cost(software) ~= cost(change), and credits the coupling and cohesion work in *Structured Design* for the underlying result. Coupling is then the thing to watch, because it is what makes one change drag others behind it, and design is whatever brings it down. The work happens in tidyings, small changes to structure that leave behavior alone. The two kinds of change are asymmetrical. A structure change can be undone, while a behavior change that has reached customers cannot, which is the reason his practices favor small steps with a test behind each one. Whether to do the structural work first is an investment question rather than a matter of virtue, and his own answer runs both ways: discounted cash flow argues for earning sooner and spending later, options argue for spending now, and the decision is the inequality between them.

## Shared concepts (frames other pages also hold)

- **[S:append-dont-overwrite:martin-fowler]** Martin Fowler also holds Event sourcing: Record every change to application state as an event, and rebuild the state at any time by replaying the log. Fowler wrote the pattern up in draft in 2005 and returned to it in 2017, comparing the event log to a git history and to the accounting transactions behind a balance. Greg Young popularized it alongside CQRS from 2007, and neither claims the underlying idea as new.
- **[C:append-dont-overwrite]** Concept page Append, Don't Overwrite: Record each change as a new entry and derive the current state from the accumulated history, instead of replacing old values in place. The present becomes a view over the record, and the record is what the system keeps.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:fred-brooks]** For Brooks, software is a communication problem before it is an engineering problem, and the cost of coordinating the people building a system grows faster than the team. Brooks's Law follows directly: adding people to a late project makes it later. No Silver Bullet sharpens the claim by separating essential complexity, which lives in the problem, from accidental complexity, which the tools add. Tooling can remove accidental complexity, sometimes dramatically, while no single development shrinks the essential part by an order of magnitude. The prediction dates from 1986 and still stands, which is why the vocabulary carries weight in any honest conversation about engineering.
- **[R:martin-fowler]** In Fowler's account, software is a medium for shared understanding, and it decays whenever nobody tends it. The cost of that decay arrives quickly, in next week's velocity, and his design stamina hypothesis makes a parallel claim that internal quality pays back within weeks. His method is to name the moves (refactoring, code smell, strangler fig, bounded context), attribute each to its origin, and revise them in public on the bliki. Naming turns tacit design choices into distinctions a team can argue about. The method has limits of its own: his 2014 microservices article undersold the cost of distribution, and later follow-ups had to correct it.
- **[R:simon-willison]** A single channel carries the developer's trusted instruction and untrusted text, and the model acts on the combination; that join is the problem Willison works. He proposed the name in September 2022, in a post documenting Riley Goodside's demonstration, four months after @himbodhisattva used the phrase and Preamble disclosed it as command injection. Five days later he ruled out defenses built from another model, which cannot be shown to hold against an attacker who retries. His answers are structural: a privileged model holding the tools, kept apart from a quarantined model that reads untrusted content, with ordinary software between them. The lethal trifecta names a configuration: three capabilities each ordinary alone and dangerous together. He polices the term afterwards, ruling that without a concatenation of trusted and untrusted strings it is not prompt injection. Most of the blog is links and quotes carrying other people's work; the authored spine is the twenty-three-post series behind these frames.
- **[R:kent-beck]** Beck's subject is the price of the next change, which Constantine's Equivalence states as cost(software) ~= cost(change). Coupling is what makes that price high, so design is whatever brings it down. The work happens in tidyings, small changes to structure that leave behavior alone. The two kinds are asymmetrical: a helper extracted can be inlined again, and a behavior change that reached customers cannot. Small steps with a test behind each one follow from that asymmetry, and he calls the test-first technique a rediscovery whose limits he states himself. His answer to whether to tidy first is an inequality rather than a rule, and it sometimes comes out no. Extreme Programming is the method he wrote up in 1999, out of the way of working a payroll team adopted in 1997. His 3X phases add the axis the others lack, since a practice that fits a product being explored is waste once it is being extracted.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> "Simple Made Easy" pulls apart two words the industry treats as one: simple means nothing is interleaved, a property of the artifact, and easy means near at hand, a property of the person. Hickey locates complexity in complecting, the braiding together of concerns that could stay independent. A familiar tool can bring complexity the problem never needed, which he calls incidental complexity, his name for what Brooks called accidental. Tests and type checkers catch mistakes after the fact, and in his account they are safety nets that cannot untangle a design. The later talks carry the same concern into data and change. Overwriting a record in place discards the fact it replaced, and removing anything from a published interface breaks whoever depends on it. He scopes these arguments to long-running information systems, and his case against static types remains contested.

> An audit log that overwrites its own entries fails at its one job. Applied past the log, the same test reaches the systems the log describes. Where access grants, configuration and policy are stored as current values only, the record of who could do what last month is gone when an investigation needs it. Keeping those as accumulated facts would put part of that history in the data model itself, and the history stays honest only if the credentials that write a grant cannot also edit the record of grants. The costs are real: teams used to overwriting records pay the relative cost the talk sets aside, and history that never ages out still needs a retention period. Even a complete fact store proves only that a change happened, so a malicious grant still waits on someone reviewing it to be noticed.
