## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** On Cook's account (a synthesis of the program he shared with Woods, which borrows Reason's language of latent failures), failure in a complex system belongs to the system. Such a system is heavily defended and always runs with flaws present, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together, and the people working in it create its safety continuously. An account that names one root cause, or one person's error, is then a social judgment made with the outcome known, and it does not explain how the failure worked. The explaining is done by the second story, the one about the systemic vulnerabilities the people involved were working inside (the pair he named with Woods and Miller in 1998). His later work takes up efficiency gains that couple distant parts of a system, and the line of representation he presented with Woods, across which operators see the system only through what represents it.
- **[T:working-line]** Complex systems run as broken systems. They keep working because they hold so many redundancies and because people make them work, flaws and all. (Paraphrased from proposition 5 of How Complex Systems Fail, 1998.)
- **[F:how-complex-systems-fail]** How Complex Systems Fail (eighteen propositions) (original, with david-woods; derived from new-view-of-human-error): Eighteen numbered propositions, first dated 1998, on why complex systems are hazardous, how they fail and how failure gets attributed afterwards. The text is Cook's alone and condenses a research program: six of the seven works on its reading list are co-written with David Woods, and it uses James Reason's vocabulary where it speaks of latent failures and the sharp end. Proposition 7 holds that post-accident attribution to a root cause is fundamentally wrong.
- **[F:first-story-second-story]** First story / second story (co-authored, with david-woods, charlotte-miller): The first story of an accident is one of "human error"; the second story is one of systemic vulnerabilities. From a December 1997 National Patient Safety Foundation workshop, written up in 1998 by Cook, David Woods and Charlotte Miller, who organized the workshop and captured its results.
- **[F:above-the-line-below-the-line]** Above the line / below the line (co-authored, with richard-cook): The software and hardware of a running service sit below a line of representation and cannot be seen or controlled directly; every interaction that crosses the line goes through a representation, and the operators keep building and refreshing their models of what lies below. Introduced in the 2017 STELLA report, prepared under David Woods's supervision. Cook presented it with Woods later that year and wrote its standalone statement in ACM Queue in 2020.
- **[F:going-solid]** Going solid (co-authored, with jens-rasmussen): Efficiency gains strip the buffers that keep the parts of a hospital loosely coupled, until distant units become tightly coupled; the paper calls that condition going solid. Written with Jens Rasmussen in 2005 and built on Rasmussen's dynamic model of risk. The phrase comes from nuclear plant operations, where a boiler goes solid when it fills completely with liquid.
- **[F:sharp-end-blunt-end]** Sharp end / blunt end (extended, with david-woods): Practitioners at the sharp end work directly with the hazardous process; people at the blunt end shape the resources and constraints they work under. The terms are James Reason's, and Cook and Woods credit them to him in their 1994 chapter "Operating at the Sharp End", which applies them to medicine.
- **[F:distancing-through-differencing]** Distancing through differencing (co-authored, with david-woods): After an accident elsewhere, people focus on the differences between that site and their own, and "see no lessons for their own operation and practices or only narrow well bounded responses." Named in a 2006 chapter with David Woods.
- **[L:1]** The propositions point to almost no evidence outside one research program (structural): The text opens on its first proposition with no credited premise, and its reading list is the program's own work. Apart from one footnote citing anthropological and sociological work on how cause is socially constructed, the evidence the propositions point to sits in that one research program. A reader citing a proposition is citing a condensed finding of that program.
- **[L:2]** Proposition 7 was written about accidents, and an attacker is a chosen cause (structural): This limit is the site's reading. Cook grounds root-cause attribution in "the social, cultural need to blame specific, localized forces or events." In a security incident an adversary is a localized force with intent who selected the weak point, and naming that adversary is an operational need. Security programs also trace defects to a shared class, such as a missing control, in order to remove the class. The proposition argues against blaming a single cause for an accident, and it does not say how to treat an agent who went looking for the flaws the system was already running with. Naming the attacker can end a review the way a first story does, and the proposition leaves open whether the flaws that attacker used still get a second story.
- **[L:3]** Going solid was built on hospital cases (local): The 2005 paper with Rasmussen builds its model on hospital cases, which Fred Hebert's reading notes describe as hospital and intensive care examples. Carrying it to software, where on the site's reading a shared service or a consolidated pipeline couples teams that used to be buffered from each other, is an inference a reader makes and the paper does not test.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** An incident review can put the propositions to itself as questions. Were several flaws involved, or one? Was the system already running degraded before the day it failed? Is the cause on the report there because it explains the failure, or because someone needed a place to put the blame? A review that asks those questions has used Cook whether it cites him or not.

- **[N:david-woods:thesis]** David Woods's thesis: Complex systems are not stable artifacts; they are continuously adapting joint cognitive systems made up of humans, organizations, and machines. Their survival depends not on being robust to known threats (that property is locally useful but globally fragile), but on having reserves of adaptive capacity that humans supply in real time when surprises arrive. Every optimization push consumes that capacity; performance-pressured systems operate closer to their limits and absorb less surprise. Graceful extensibility (the ability to stretch performance under previously-unimagined conditions) is what you actually want, and it is exactly the property that efficiency-focused management most reliably destroys.
- **[E:out:john-allspaw]** extends, primary → John Allspaw (has a page): Carried the propositions into web operations: a 2009 post on the paper, then a reprint in the 2010 operations book he edited with Jesse Robbins. He presented the line of representation to practitioners in 2017, and a 2019 post of his says his colleagues outlined the frame in the STELLA report. He co-founded Adaptive Capacity Labs with Cook, with Woods.
- **[N:john-allspaw:thesis]** John Allspaw's thesis: An incident is not a defect to be eliminated; it is a sample drawn from your system's actual operating envelope, telling you things about how the work is done that no design document, runbook, or simulation can show you. The way to honor that signal is to set up the conditions where engineers will tell you honestly what happened, what they thought was happening, what they almost did, and what they would do differently. That requires removing blame as a default, recognizing the gap between *work-as-imagined* and *work-as-done*, and treating the human side of the system as the primary source of expertise about how the system actually behaves.
- **[E:out:erik-hollnagel]** extends, secondary → Erik Hollnagel (has a page): Names A Tale of Two Stories (1998), by Cook, Woods and Miller, as one of the two best-known precedents for contrasting two views of safety, the device behind his Safety-I and Safety-II.
- **[N:erik-hollnagel:thesis]** Erik Hollnagel's thesis: Hollnagel argues that safety has traditionally been defined by what goes wrong, and that this leaves the object of study too small. Failures are rare, and the same adjustments that let work succeed under changing conditions are what occasionally let it fail. So the useful question for safety management is how things usually go right, and an investigation explains the rare failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism. People cannot be as efficient as possible and as thorough as possible at once, the trade between the two is normal, and the shortcuts that make work efficient are the same ones that sometimes let a failure through.
- **[E:out:sidney-dekker]** parallel, secondary → Sidney Dekker (has a page): Co-author with Cook, Woods, Johannesen and Sarter of the second edition of Behind Human Error (2010).
- **[N:sidney-dekker:thesis]** Sidney Dekker's thesis: Treat human error as a *consequence*, not a cause. The investigator's job is not to determine what someone failed to do, but to reconstruct how the situation made sense to them at the time, because if it made sense, it will make sense again, and the next operator in that position will make the same choice. *Just culture* names the institutional posture that makes this kind of investigation possible. *Drift into failure* names the slow-motion process by which good organizations end up in bad places without anyone making a single obviously wrong decision. *Safety differently* names the prescription: invest in human adaptive capacity, not procedural compliance.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:david-woods]** Woods describes complex systems as joint cognitive systems, in which people and automation do the thinking together and failures live in the joint between them. Such systems survive surprises through adaptive capacity: reserves that people and organizational arrangements supply, and that stay invisible until they are needed. The Law of Stretched Systems explains why those reserves shrink: slack looks like inefficiency, so every system gets pushed toward the edge of its envelope, where small disturbances produce large failures. Graceful extensibility, the ability to stretch under conditions nobody designed for, is the property resilience engineering aims at. The original papers are dense, which is why carriers such as Allspaw brought the vocabulary into software.
- **[R:john-allspaw]** Allspaw treats an incident as a sample of how a system really operates, carrying information no design document or runbook can supply. Getting that information requires conditions where engineers can say honestly what they saw and decided without career risk, which is the purpose of the blameless postmortem. The analysis then looks above the line, at the mental models and coordination that operate the system, as well as below it at code and configuration. Dark debt names what that analysis keeps finding: failure modes that live in the interactions between components and cannot be found by inspecting any one of them. The frame fails when blameless gets read as consequence-free, which keeps the label and drops the learning discipline.
- **[R:erik-hollnagel]** Hollnagel moves the object of safety management from the rare accident to the ordinary working day. Safety-I counts adverse outcomes and aims to minimise them, which confines its evidence to rare events. Safety-II asks how work usually succeeds under varying conditions, and explains the occasional failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism: nobody can be as efficient and as thorough as possible at once, and trading one for the other is normal work. He catalogues the shortcuts that trade produces, among them "Looks fine" and "Has been checked by someone else". In his account, those shortcuts are how the work gets done and also what occasionally lets a failure through. The contrast is disputed at its root: Leveson says the Safety-I he describes bears little resemblance to safety engineering as practised, and Cooper groups Safety-II with other approaches into a New View whose propositions he calls untested.
- **[R:sidney-dekker]** Dekker's starting point is that human error is a consequence of system conditions, so the investigator's job is to reconstruct how an action made sense to the person at the time. If it made sense once, it will make sense to the next person in the same position. Just culture draws the accountability line: people answer for willful violations, while honest mistakes and system-induced failures become material for learning. Drift into failure explains how organizations reach unsafe states through many small, reasonable choices toward efficiency, none of them obviously wrong. The frame works best when used surgically, because invoking drift for every incident can close off the question of which decisions could have gone differently.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> The eighteen propositions of How Complex Systems Fail run from the nature of hazard, through how blame gets assigned, to safety as something people create. A complex system is heavily defended and always carries flaws, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together. It follows, for Cook, that naming a single root cause after an accident answers a social need to blame and leaves the mechanism of the failure unexplained. The 1998 report he wrote with Woods and Miller names the alternative: the first story is one of human error, the second one of the systemic vulnerabilities behind it. Going solid, written with Rasmussen in 2005, describes efficiency gains stripping the buffers between distant parts of a hospital until they are tightly coupled. Cook's 2020 statement of the line of representation, a frame introduced in the 2017 STELLA report, makes the argument in software's own terms, where operators reach the system only through representations.

> Nobody running a system sees it directly, a condition that predates generated code. In Cook's 2020 account, operators hold a model built through representations and kept current by continual refreshing. On that reading, the security question this frame adds for generated code is how stale the organization's model of its estate has become, which reviewing each change does not measure. An attacker acting below the line aims to change the system without changing anything its operators see, and refreshing prompted by those representations may never pick that change up. The practical move is to budget model refresh as work of its own, drawn from a representation the attacker does not control, such as an out-of-band inventory checked against what operators believe is running. The frame itself sets the ceiling, because the refresh is still a representation: its worth is whether it could show a change the watched ones hide, and a record that reviews were held cannot.
