## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Anderson's argument is that security failures are distributed by incentive more than by technical difficulty, and that the field keeps looking in the wrong place because nothing tells it where the losses are. The 1993 work set the shape. Aviation institutionalised learning from accidents and cryptography did not, and the absence of that feedback let a false threat model stand for years while the real frauds ran through implementation and management. Once the losses are visible, the pattern in them is economic. Where the party who can protect a system is not the party who suffers when it fails, failure should be expected, and better cryptography does not change that arithmetic. The remedy he reached for sits mostly outside the security team. The 2001 paper borrows liability dumping, network externalities, moral hazard and the tragedy of the commons from microeconomics, because the interesting variable is who bears the cost. His work for the British Medical Association made a matching argument about aggregation: the likelihood a resource is abused depends on its value and on the number of people who can reach it, and putting personal information into one large database raises both at once. And where no venue existed for the questions that followed, he convened one.
- **[T:working-line]** In general, where the party who is in a position to protect a system is not the party who would suffer the results of security failure, then problems may be expected. ("Why Information Security is Hard: An Economic Perspective," ACSAC, December 2001.)
- **[F:threat-model-was-wrong]** The threat model was wrong (original): A threat model can be wrong as a matter of fact, and the way to find out is to collect how the losses actually happened. Anderson did that for cash machine fraud and reported that most of it came from implementation error and management failure, with designers "focussed on what could possibly go wrong, rather than on what was likely to". The phrase is the 1993 paper's own section heading.
- **[F:security-economics]** Security economics (extended, with hal-varian): Reading security failure through microeconomics: network externalities, asymmetric information, moral hazard, adverse selection, liability dumping and the tragedy of the commons, all borrowed from the economics literature, none of it coined here. The founding conversation was with Hal Varian, who suggested that the liability problem in online payments was classic moral hazard; the 2001 paper credits him two pages in, and the two convened the first Workshop on the Economics of Information Security at Berkeley in June 2002. The Cambridge department calls Anderson one of the founders of the field.
- **[F:misaligned-incentives]** Misaligned incentives (co-authored, with tyler-moore): Systems are "particularly prone to failure when the person guarding them is not the person who suffers when they fail". The phrase is a section heading in Anderson and Tyler Moore's 2006 Science paper, which is where it entered security vocabulary; the 2001 paper says "perverse incentives". The term itself is older economics vocabulary, and Cormac Herley credits economics for the term while crediting Anderson for carrying the frame into security.
- **[F:trojan-source]** Trojan Source (co-authored, with ross-anderson): Source code that renders one way to a human reviewer and compiles another, using Unicode bidirectional control characters and homoglyphs. Nicholas Boucher, Anderson's doctoral student, is first author and Anderson is second. The paper demonstrates working examples in twelve languages and records two CVE identifiers, CVE-2021-42574 and CVE-2021-42694. Rust fixed it in 1.56.1, a -Wbidi-chars warning was merged for GCC 12, and LLVM merged similar patches.
- **[F:model-collapse]** Model collapse (co-authored, with zakhar-shumaylov, yiren-zhao, nicolas-papernot, ross-anderson, yarin-gal): What happens to a generative model trained on data produced by earlier generative models. In the paper's own abstract, "indiscriminate use of model-generated content in training causes irreversible defects in the resulting models, in which tails of the original content distribution disappear". Ilia Shumailov is first author of six, with Zakhar Shumaylov, Yiren Zhao, Nicolas Papernot, Ross Anderson and Yarin Gal; Anderson is fifth. Nature, 2024, after an earlier preprint of the same work titled "The Curse of Recursion".
- **[F:security-engineering-as-a-discipline]** Security engineering as a discipline (popularized): Building dependable systems treated as an engineering discipline with its own body of knowledge, a textbook and named chairs. The phrase predates Anderson and no coiner is established here. What the record supports is the building: the BCS gave him the Lovelace Medal in 2015 "in recognition of his many contributions to building security engineering into a discipline", Computer Weekly repeats the framing independently, Cambridge and Edinburgh both named chairs for it, and his biographer calls it "the thread that linked his diverse contributions".
- **[F:andersons-rule]** Anderson's Rule (community): The claim that a database cannot hold scale, functionality and security at the same time. The formulation belongs to Henry Porter, in a Guardian column of 10 August 2009, and neither the named rule nor the phrase "scale, functionality" appears anywhere in the third edition of Security Engineering; Wikipedia's standalone article on the rule cites only that column. What Anderson wrote is the mechanism without the name, in section 10.4: "The likelihood that a resource will be abused depends on its value and on the number of people with access to it. Aggregating personal information into large databases increases both these risk factors at the same time."
- **[L:1]** The prescription is liability law, and no security leader can enact it (structural): The 2001 paper reaches for liability dumping, moral hazard and the tragedy of the commons, and what follows from that diagnosis is regulation: put the cost on the party in a position to act. A security executive holds no such instrument. The version available inside a company, moving the cost of an incident onto the team that shipped the change, needs an executive who can change the scorecard, and it is a different and much smaller lever than the one the paper argues for. Read the economics as a diagnosis of why a programme keeps failing, and expect the remedy to sit above the security organisation.
- **[L:2]** The incentive argument comes from retail banking and centralised state records (structural): The settings behind the argument are cash machines and points of sale at Barclays, a branch network across 23 countries at Standard Chartered, and a government plan to centralise medical records. In those settings aggregation is a pathology: value and access count rise together and the system gets worse. A 2026 reader works on platforms where aggregation is the product, and where the party bearing the loss is often a customer who never chose the architecture. The mechanism still applies. Those settings do not transfer without first asking which party actually pays.
- **[L:3]** Six of the seven frames here belong substantially to someone else (local): Security economics has Hal Varian in the founding conversation and borrows its vocabulary from economics. "Misaligned incentives" is a section heading in a paper with Tyler Moore. Model collapse is first-authored by Ilia Shumailov with Anderson fifth of six. Trojan Source is first-authored by his own doctoral student. Anderson's Rule was formulated by a journalist and appears nowhere in his book. What the record shows him doing best, on his biographer's account, is convening: he helped establish several new research areas and catalysed the academic communities around them. A citation that credits him alone for any of this describes a coiner where the evidence shows a convener and co-author.
- **[L:4]** The one clean original does not travel as vocabulary (local): "Why the threat model was wrong" is the 1993 paper's own section heading and the only frame here that is Anderson's alone. It is a sentence, and the paper carries 144 citations against 956 and 920 for the two economics papers. Nothing in the record shows practitioners using the phrase detached from the paper. Treat it as an argument to apply, and expect no room to recognise the phrase.
- **[L:5]** The advocacy ran ahead of the specification at least once (local): David Safford of IBM Research answered Anderson's TCPA FAQ in October 2002 under a section headed "Specific Technical Comments: Ross Anderson's TCPA FAQ", whose subheadings read "Incorrectly lumps together TCPA, Palladium, and DRM:", "Is full of technical errors:" and "Presents speculation as fact:". Safford states in the paper that the views are his own and not necessarily IBM's, and his broader three-part charge is levelled at three papers together, so only those subheadings are aimed at Anderson. The campaigning was part of the work and it used a register the research papers did not, which is worth knowing before citing the policy writing at the evidentiary standard of the papers.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** None of that touches the method under the 1993 paper, and a single company can run a narrow version of it: count the losses that already happened, then hold the threat model up against the count. Most security programmes already hold the first half in an incident log nobody reads that way. What they cannot hold is the loop the paper argues for, which is public and crosses companies. The economics gives the second half its shape, because the pattern in real losses tends to be about who pays more than about what is hard, and that question can be asked inside one company without waiting for a regulator to act. The convening is the part a reader can copy most directly, and the research communities outlived him. A workshop meeting for a twenty-fifth time two years after his death makes the case better than any citation count.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:david-woods]** parallel, primary → David Woods (has a page): The 1993 paper opens by contrasting cryptosystem design with aviation, where accidents are investigated and the findings feed back into practice, and concludes that the missing loop is what let a false threat model stand. - **[N:david-woods:thesis]** David Woods's thesis: Complex systems are not stable artifacts; they are continuously adapting joint cognitive systems made up of humans, organizations, and machines. Their survival depends not on being robust to known threats (that property is locally useful but globally fragile), but on having reserves of adaptive capacity that humans supply in real time when surprises arrive. Every optimization push consumes that capacity; performance-pressured systems operate closer to their limits and absorb less surprise. Graceful extensibility (the ability to stretch performance under previously-unimagined conditions) is what you actually want, and it is exactly the property that efficiency-focused management most reliably destroys.
- **[E:out:simon-willison]** parallel, primary → Simon Willison (has a page): Two accounts of a channel that carries more authority than its reader notices. Willison works the join where a trusted instruction and untrusted text reach a model through one input. Trojan Source, first authored by Nicholas Boucher with Anderson second, works the join where source text renders one way to a reviewer and compiles another, and the fixes landed in Rust, GCC and LLVM, leaving review practice untouched.
- **[N:simon-willison:thesis]** Simon Willison's thesis: Prompt injection, in Willison's definition, is what happens when an application concatenates a developer's trusted instruction with untrusted text and the model acts on the combination. He treats that as the reason the defenses built for older injection attacks have no equivalent here: SQL injection has a known mitigation that is guaranteed to work, in escaping and quoting user-provided strings, and no such function exists for natural language. From there he draws a rule about what a fix may be made of. A hole patched with another model cannot be verified, because the model is a black box and an adversary keeps trying until something works, so the direction he trusts is architectural, keeping untrusted content away from anything that holds privilege. His claim about the state of the art is narrower than the rule, and he states it plainly: two and a half years into the problem he had seen alarmingly little progress toward a robust solution, and nobody yet knows how to prevent it 100 percent reliably.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:david-woods]** Woods describes complex systems as joint cognitive systems, in which people and automation do the thinking together and failures live in the joint between them. Such systems survive surprises through adaptive capacity: reserves that people and organizational arrangements supply, and that stay invisible until they are needed. The Law of Stretched Systems explains why those reserves shrink: slack looks like inefficiency, so every system gets pushed toward the edge of its envelope, where small disturbances produce large failures. Graceful extensibility, the ability to stretch under conditions nobody designed for, is the property resilience engineering aims at. The original papers are dense, which is why carriers such as Allspaw brought the vocabulary into software.
- **[R:simon-willison]** A single channel carries the developer's trusted instruction and untrusted text, and the model acts on the combination; that join is the problem Willison works. He proposed the name in September 2022, in a post documenting Riley Goodside's demonstration, four months after @himbodhisattva used the phrase and Preamble disclosed it as command injection. Five days later he ruled out defenses built from another model, which cannot be shown to hold against an attacker who retries. His answers are structural: a privileged model holding the tools, kept apart from a quarantined model that reads untrusted content, with ordinary software between them. The lethal trifecta names a configuration: three capabilities each ordinary alone and dangerous together. He polices the term afterwards, ruling that without a concatenation of trusted and untrusted strings it is not prompt injection. Most of the blog is links and quotes carrying other people's work; the authored spine is the twenty-three-post series behind these frames.

> Anderson's 1993 finding came from collecting how cash machine fraud happened: most of it ran through implementation error and management failure while the field designed against cryptanalysis. The paper blames a missing feedback loop: aviation investigates its accidents and cryptography had no equivalent. Once the losses are visible the pattern is economic, and his rule is that problems may be expected wherever the party able to protect a system is not the one who suffers. That reading began in a conversation with the economist Hal Varian, borrowing moral hazard and network externalities from economics. The phrasing that travelled is "misaligned incentives", a section heading in the 2006 paper with Tyler Moore. The late papers carry his name inside other people's work: second to his student Nicholas Boucher on Trojan Source, fifth of six behind Ilia Shumailov on model collapse. He wrote a standard textbook, and his biographer credits him with catalysing the communities that outlived him.

> Trojan Source, first-authored by Anderson's doctoral student Nicholas Boucher, is the sharpest case on this site for enforcing boundaries in infrastructure rather than trusting code review: the human reads one program and the compiler builds another. The fixes bear that out, and they differ in strength: Rust rejects those characters by default, while the GCC warning still ends at a human. The move worth copying is to push the check into the toolchain wherever the rendered and executed artifact can differ. Its precondition is ownership of the build, so it reaches only the compiler versions, linters and package resolution an organisation pins, and no further into a vendor's. It also buys false confidence, because a build rule covers the encodings somebody already named. Anderson's correction is the cheaper companion: collect how the losses happened, then check the threat model against them. The next rule comes from asking which of those losses a build rule could have refused.
