## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Treat human error as a *consequence*, not a cause. The investigator's job is not to determine what someone failed to do, but to reconstruct how the situation made sense to them at the time, because if it made sense, it will make sense again, and the next operator in that position will make the same choice. *Just culture* names the institutional posture that makes this kind of investigation possible. *Drift into failure* names the slow-motion process by which good organizations end up in bad places without anyone making a single obviously wrong decision. *Safety differently* names the prescription: invest in human adaptive capacity, not procedural compliance.
- **[T:working-line]** Underneath every simple, obvious story about an "error," there is a deeper, second story: the one where you actually have to do the thinking. (Paraphrased from *The Field Guide to Understanding Human Error*.)
- **[F:new-view-of-human-error]** The new view of human error (extended, with david-woods, jens-rasmussen): Human error is a symptom of deeper system conditions, not a cause of failure. The diagnosis stops where most accident reports start.
- **[F:just-culture]** Just culture (extended, with james-reason, david-marx): An institutional posture in which the line between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour is clearly drawn, in Reason's framing. Dekker's extension asks who gets to draw that line, arguing that culpability does not inhere in the act itself. The cultural precondition for blameless investigation.
- **[F:drift-into-failure]** Drift into failure (extended, with david-woods, jens-rasmussen; derived from migration-toward-the-boundary): The slow-motion process by which organizations end up in unsafe states without any single obviously-wrong decision. Many small reasonable choices in the direction of efficiency, until the envelope is breached.
- **[F:safety-differently]** Safety differently (original): An alternative to compliance-centric safety: invest in human adaptive capacity, build the conditions where workers can do their jobs well, and treat the front line as the source of safety rather than the source of risk.
- **[F:work-as-imagined-vs-work-as-done]** Work-as-imagined vs. work-as-done (extended, with erik-hollnagel, sidney-dekker): The gap between how procedures, designs, and management imagine the work happens (imagined) and how it actually happens under real constraints (done). Most "human error" lives in this gap.
- **[F:adaptive-capacity]** Adaptive capacity (extended, with david-woods, sidney-dekker): The reserve human and organizational capacity that absorbs surprises beyond the designed-for envelope. Dekker's safety- differently prescription is mostly about investing in this.
- **[L:1]** The frame's silence on adversaries shows under security pressure (structural): Same gap as Woods's. Dekker writes about complex systems where the disturbances come from nature, accident, or organizational drift. Security work involves disturbances that are *optimized against the system* by humans who are also reading the organization's safety culture and using its blind spots. Dekker's frame still applies; the security overlay is not built in.
- **[L:2]** *Drift into failure* can become a totalizing explanation (local): Applied loosely, "drift into failure" can be invoked to explain every incident as the inevitable product of organizational pressure, which then forecloses the question of what specific decisions could have been made differently. The frame is best when used surgically, not as a default story.
- **[L:3]** Safety-differently's "trust the front line" assumes alignment (local): Dekker's prescription works when front-line workers and the organization share goals. In organizations with severe goal misalignment (bonus structures rewarding incident suppression, ops teams under headcount cuts, security orgs measured on counter-productive metrics), "investing in adaptive capacity" gets reinterpreted into something it isn't. The framework has no story for getting management to want what the framework wants.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** *The Field Guide to Understanding Human Error* is a working manual for investigators. Its 2014 third edition reads as a checklist for an incident review, chapter by chapter: the Bad Apple Theory, the hindsight bias, putting data in context, cause as something you construct. The argument holds up under the limits above.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:david-woods]** parallel, primary → David Woods (has a page): Co-builder of the new view of human error. Woods is the cognitive-systems-engineering side; Dekker is the safety-culture side. Read them together.
- **[N:david-woods:thesis]** David Woods's thesis: Complex systems are not stable artifacts; they are continuously adapting joint cognitive systems made up of humans, organizations, and machines. Their survival depends not on being robust to known threats (that property is locally useful but globally fragile), but on having reserves of adaptive capacity that humans supply in real time when surprises arrive. Every optimization push consumes that capacity; performance-pressured systems operate closer to their limits and absorb less surprise. Graceful extensibility (the ability to stretch performance under previously-unimagined conditions) is what you actually want, and it is exactly the property that efficiency-focused management most reliably destroys.
- **[E:out:john-allspaw]** extends, primary → John Allspaw (has a page): Allspaw carried the new view into software operations. His 2012 Etsy post "Blameless PostMortems and a Just Culture" sets aside what "Sidney Dekker calls the 'Bad Apple Theory'", and draws as well on Erik Hollnagel, and on *Behind Human Error* for its first and second stories. Allspaw's 2015 Lund master's thesis in Human Factors and System Safety, supervised by Anthony Smoker and Johan Bergström, thanks Dekker for "his encouragement, patience, and 'thrown in the deep end' approach".
- **[N:john-allspaw:thesis]** John Allspaw's thesis: An incident is not a defect to be eliminated; it is a sample drawn from your system's actual operating envelope, telling you things about how the work is done that no design document, runbook, or simulation can show you. The way to honor that signal is to set up the conditions where engineers will tell you honestly what happened, what they thought was happening, what they almost did, and what they would do differently. That requires removing blame as a default, recognizing the gap between *work-as-imagined* and *work-as-done*, and treating the human side of the system as the primary source of expertise about how the system actually behaves.
- **[E:out:james-reason]** antecedent, primary → James Reason (has a page): Reason's 1990 *Human Error* and 1997 *Managing the Risks of Organizational Accidents* are the academic predecessors Dekker built from. Swiss-cheese model, just culture (originally Reason's framing) all run through here.
- **[N:james-reason:thesis]** James Reason's thesis: On Reason's account, accidents in complex systems come from unsafe acts at the front line triggering latent conditions that earlier decisions by designers and managers put in place (an observation he credited to Barry Turner and Charles Perrow), and defences fail when their weaknesses coincide. On that account, error management that looks only at the person who erred misses where the conditions came from, and in his 1997 words "identifying and eliminating latent conditions proactively still offer the best routes to improving system fitness." The argument extends to culture. A safe culture, in his 1998 argument, is an informed one, which depends on people reporting, which rests on a just culture in which the line between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour is clearly drawn; he rejected the blanket "no blame" alternative as neither feasible nor desirable.
- **[E:out:will-larson]** parallel, primary → Will Larson (has a page): Drift into failure with none of the drift hidden. Every deviation Larson counts as exception debt was requested, argued and granted on the record, and the written policy still loses contact with the work it governs. His remedy is procedural: batch a month of exception requests and rewrite the policy around what was granted anyway.
- **[N:will-larson:thesis]** Will Larson's thesis: Larson's subject is the written machinery of an engineering organization, and the recurring move across the corpus is to name a way one of those pieces decays and then prescribe the repair. A policy that spends significant time granting exceptions has exception debt, and working the exceptions one at a time is what sustains it; the repair is to batch a month of requests and merge the escalations into the next revision. An engineering strategy usually already exists and has simply never been written down, so writing it down solves half the problem (the structure he writes into is Richard Rumelt's diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action, which he credits by name). A team sits in one of four states and the state decides what it needs, in what he calls a loose framework. The staff engineer archetypes came the other way around, out of interviews with people already in the roles rather than from a mechanism. Holding all of it up is his account of authority: the power that comes with a title is loaned by a sponsor, can be retracted, and lasts only while its holder stays aligned with whoever bestowed it (the conferral-and-revocation structure is French and Raven's legitimate power, 1959; the loan framing and the maintenance condition it places on the holder are his).
- **[E:out:erik-hollnagel]** parallel, primary → Erik Hollnagel (has a page): Co-editor of the 2006 resilience-engineering anthology, which carried a chapter by Dekker. Hollnagel's Safety-II runs parallel to Dekker's *Safety Differently*: one studies how everyday work goes right, the other treats the people doing it as a source of insight.
- **[N:erik-hollnagel:thesis]** Erik Hollnagel's thesis: Hollnagel argues that safety has traditionally been defined by what goes wrong, and that this leaves the object of study too small. Failures are rare, and the same adjustments that let work succeed under changing conditions are what occasionally let it fail. So the useful question for safety management is how things usually go right, and an investigation explains the rare failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism. People cannot be as efficient as possible and as thorough as possible at once, the trade between the two is normal, and the shortcuts that make work efficient are the same ones that sometimes let a failure through.
- **[E:out:jens-rasmussen]** antecedent, secondary → Jens Rasmussen (has a page): A 2013 paper by Dekker and Shawn Pruchnicki cites Rasmussen 1997 for the drift account at its centre. Rasmussen's own word for the movement is migration, under an effort gradient and a cost gradient.
- **[N:jens-rasmussen:thesis]** Jens Rasmussen's thesis: Rasmussen read the court reports from Bhopal, Flixborough, Zeebrügge and Chernobyl as showing that those accidents "have not been caused by a coincidence of independent failures and human errors, but by a systematic migration of organisational behaviour toward accident under the influence of pressure toward cost-effectiveness". Task analysis built on action sequences and occasional errors should therefore give way, he argued, to a model of "work system constraints, boundaries of acceptable performance, and subjective criteria guiding adaptation to change", with risk management treated as a control problem across the disciplines that shape the work. Improvement is aimed at the boundary: identify it, make it visible to the people who work near it, and give them the chance to learn to cope with it, on his view that "operation close to known boundaries may be safer than requiring excessive margins". His 1990 paper on causality gives the investigative side: what an analysis accepts as a cause depends on stop rules set by its aim, and errors as "a separate class of behaviour" grow harder to pick out in modern work.
- **[E:out:richard-cook]** parallel, secondary → Richard Cook (has a page): Cook argued the same ground from the operating room. His 1998 report with David Woods and Charlotte Miller set a first story of human error against a second story of systemic vulnerabilities, and he shares a byline with Dekker on the 2010 second edition of Behind Human Error.
- **[N:richard-cook:thesis]** Richard Cook's thesis: On Cook's account (a synthesis of the program he shared with Woods, which borrows Reason's language of latent failures), failure in a complex system belongs to the system. Such a system is heavily defended and always runs with flaws present, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together, and the people working in it create its safety continuously. An account that names one root cause, or one person's error, is then a social judgment made with the outcome known, and it does not explain how the failure worked. The explaining is done by the second story, the one about the systemic vulnerabilities the people involved were working inside (the pair set out in his 1998 report with Woods and Miller). His later work takes up efficiency gains that couple distant parts of a system, and the line of representation he presented with Woods, across which operators see the system only through what represents it.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:david-woods]** Woods describes complex systems as joint cognitive systems, in which people and automation do the thinking together and failures live in the joint between them. Such systems survive surprises through adaptive capacity: reserves that people and organizational arrangements supply, and that stay invisible until they are needed. The Law of Stretched Systems explains why those reserves shrink: slack looks like inefficiency, so every system gets pushed toward the edge of its envelope, where small disturbances produce large failures. Graceful extensibility, the ability to stretch under conditions nobody designed for, is the property resilience engineering aims at. The original papers are dense, which is why carriers such as Allspaw brought the vocabulary into software.
- **[R:john-allspaw]** Allspaw treats an incident as a sample of how a system really operates, carrying information no design document or runbook can supply. Getting that information requires conditions where engineers can say honestly what they saw and decided without career risk, which is the purpose of the blameless postmortem. The analysis then looks above the line, at the mental models and coordination that operate the system, as well as below it at code and configuration. Dark debt names what that analysis keeps finding: failure modes that live in the interactions between components and cannot be found by inspecting any one of them. The frame fails when blameless gets read as consequence-free, which keeps the label and drops the learning discipline.
- **[R:james-reason]** Reason separates active failures, the unsafe acts at a system's front line, from latent conditions, the "resident pathogens" left in place by earlier decisions. His co-authored history calls the observation behind the pair "nothing new", crediting Barry Turner and Charles Perrow. He and John Wreathall drew the account as five planes, which later became slices of Swiss cheese, a label someone else supplied; an accident needs their holes to align. In 1997 latent failures became latent conditions, because "a condition is not a cause, but it is necessary for a causal factor to have an impact." He argued that an organisation learns of its hazards through its people's reports, and that reporting rests on a just culture that separates acceptable from unacceptable behaviour. He questioned the model's use, writing in 1997 that the search for distant contributions may have gone too far and asking in a 2004 talk whether it was past its sell-by date.
- **[R:will-larson]** The organization Larson writes about is the one on paper: the ladder, the policy, the strategy. The essays work one decay at a time: name how a written artifact fails, then prescribe the repair. A policy that spends its time granting exceptions carries exception debt, and the repair is to batch a month of requests and rewrite the policy around what the escalations show. Most companies already have an engineering strategy and never wrote it down, so by his account the writing solves half the problem, inside Rumelt's kernel of diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action. The staff engineer archetypes came the other way, out of interviews with people who already held the title, which is what the taxonomy describes and what bounds it. Underneath sits his account of authority: the power a title carries is bestowed by a sponsor, can be retracted, and holds only while its holder stays aligned with whoever bestowed it.
- **[R:erik-hollnagel]** Hollnagel moves the object of safety management from the rare accident to the ordinary working day. Safety-I counts adverse outcomes and aims to minimise them, which confines its evidence to rare events. Safety-II asks how work usually succeeds under varying conditions, and explains the occasional failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism: nobody can be as efficient and as thorough as possible at once, and trading one for the other is normal work. He catalogues the shortcuts that trade produces, among them "Looks fine" and "Has been checked by someone else". In his account, those shortcuts are how the work gets done and also what occasionally lets a failure through. The contrast is disputed at its root: Leveson says the Safety-I he describes bears little resemblance to safety engineering as practised, and Cooper groups Safety-II with other approaches into a New View whose propositions he calls untested.
- **[R:jens-rasmussen]** Rasmussen gives one account of how work moves before an accident and another of how the analysis stops after one. Workers follow an effort gradient, management supplies a cost gradient, and together they very likely produce a systematic migration toward the boundary of acceptable performance. Under that pressure defences in depth are likely to degenerate over time, and in his account the accidents he studied were caused by the migration, with a coincidence of independent failures and human errors ruled out. He aims improvement at the boundary and away from errors: make it visible to the people who work near it, and control performance where work meets it. The analysis afterwards typically ends by stop rules that are rarely stated: the causal trail runs out, a familiar abnormal event explains enough, or a cure is available. Which rule ends it depends on whether the analysis means to explain the events, to allocate responsibility and blame or to improve the system.
- **[R:richard-cook]** The eighteen propositions of How Complex Systems Fail run from the nature of hazard, through how blame gets assigned, to safety as something people create. In Cook's account a complex system is heavily defended and always carries flaws, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together. It follows that naming a single root cause after an accident answers a social need to blame and leaves the mechanism of the failure unexplained. The 1998 report he wrote with Woods and Miller gives the alternative: the first story is one of human error, the second one of the systemic vulnerabilities behind it. Going solid, written with Rasmussen in 2005, describes efficiency gains stripping the buffers between distant parts of a hospital until they are tightly coupled. Cook's 2020 statement of the line of representation, a frame set out in the 2017 STELLA report, is written for software operators, who reach the system only through representations.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Dekker's starting point is that human error is a consequence of system conditions, so the investigator's job is to reconstruct how an action made sense to the person at the time. If it made sense once, it will make sense to the next person in the same position. Just culture draws a line between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour, and Dekker's version asks who gets to draw it, since culpability does not inhere in the act itself. Drift into failure explains how organizations reach unsafe states through many small, reasonable choices toward efficiency, none of them obviously wrong. The frame works best when used surgically, because invoking drift for every incident can close off the question of which decisions could have gone differently.

> Security programs drift the same way, one reasonable exception at a time. Each risk acceptance makes sense on the day it is signed, and the unsafe state appears only in the accumulation. Recording every exception as a structured, auditable object with an owner and an expiry makes that accumulation visible before an incident does. An expiry helps only if renewal demands a fresh justification, since routine renewal turns the record into a paper trail of the drift. Just culture then shapes the review, which asks how each acceptance made sense at the time. The register cannot stop drift by itself, and it does stop the organization from being the last to notice what has accumulated.
