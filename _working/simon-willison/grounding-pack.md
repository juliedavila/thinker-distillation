## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Prompt injection, in Willison's definition, is what happens when an application concatenates a developer's trusted instruction with untrusted text and the model acts on the combination. He treats that as the reason the defenses built for older injection attacks have no equivalent here: SQL injection has a known mitigation that is guaranteed to work, in escaping and quoting user-provided strings, and no such function exists for natural language. From there he draws a rule about what a fix may be made of. A hole patched with another model cannot be verified, because the model is a black box and an adversary keeps trying until something works, so the direction he trusts is architectural, keeping untrusted content away from anything that holds privilege. His claim about the state of the art is narrower than the rule, and he states it plainly: two and a half years into the problem he had seen alarmingly little progress toward a robust solution, and nobody yet knows how to prevent it 100 percent reliably.
- **[T:working-line]** If you patch a hole with even more AI, you have no way of knowing if your solution is 100% reliable. ("You can't solve AI security problems with more AI," 2022.)
- **[F:prompt-injection]** Prompt injection (popularized, with riley-goodside, himbodhisattva, jonathan-cefalu): An attack that works by concatenating a trusted developer instruction with untrusted text, so the model acts on the attacker's words as if they were the developer's. Willison proposed the name on 2022-09-12 in a post documenting Riley Goodside's demonstration. The phrase was already in use: @himbodhisattva wrote "prompt-injection attack" on 2022-05-13, four months earlier, a priority Willison surfaced and conceded himself in 2025, and Jonathan Cefalu of Preamble had disclosed the vulnerability to OpenAI in May 2022 under the name command injection.
- **[F:lethal-trifecta]** The lethal trifecta (original): Three capabilities that are each ordinary alone and dangerous in combination: access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and the ability to communicate externally. An agent holding all three can be steered into stealing what it can reach. Meta's Agents Rule of Two credits the frame by name, alongside Chromium's separately credited Rule of Two.
- **[F:dual-llm-pattern]** The Dual LLM pattern (original): A privileged model that reads only trusted input and holds the tools, a quarantined model that reads untrusted content and holds nothing, and ordinary software as the controller between them. The rule that makes it work is that unfiltered output from the quarantined model never reaches the privileged one. A fourteen-author paper from Google, Microsoft, IBM, ETH Zurich and EPFL carries it as one of six named patterns. The post credits no one, and an antecedent search found no published source placing the split in the older privilege-separation lineage of systems security.
- **[F:no-ai-fix-for-ai-security]** You can't solve AI security problems with more AI (original): A defense made of another model cannot be verified, because the model is a black box and an attacker who keeps retrying needs only one attempt to land. He sets this against SQL injection, which has a known mitigation that is guaranteed to work.
- **[F:prompt-injection-is-not-jailbreaking]** Prompt injection and jailbreaking are not the same thing (original): Jailbreaking subverts a model's own safety training. Prompt injection subverts an application built on top of a model, and his test is exact: "Crucially: if there's no concatenation of trusted and untrusted strings, it's not prompt injection." The post exists because the term he proposed had been taken up for both, so it is the coiner policing a name he had lost control of. Jailbreaking is community vocabulary he did not name.
- **[F:vibe-coding]** Vibe coding (extended, with andrej-karpathy): Andrej Karpathy coined the term on 2025-02-06. Willison's contribution is a narrowing: "Building software with an LLM without reviewing the code it writes." The review step is what the definition turns on.
- **[F:slop]** Slop (popularized, with deepfates): Unwanted AI-generated content published without review, as spam is the term for unwanted email. The coinage is @deepfates's and the analogy to spam is @deepfates's sentence; Willison's post quotes the tweet in its first line and endorses it. His part is circulation.
- **[L:1]** Most of the output is the link blog (structural): In 2025 he published 1,044 items. Of those, 119 were entries of his own and 99 were short notes; the remaining 826 are links and quotes carrying work that is not his. Much of the research a reader associates with him belongs to other people. Markdown-image exfiltration against LLM applications is Johann Rehberger's, published in May 2023, and Rehberger himself credits Roman Samoilenko's disclosure at the end of March 2023 and calls his own finding independent. Willison's 2025 post "The Summer of Johann" is about Rehberger's output. Read the volume as curation and the prompt-injection series as the authored work.
- **[L:2]** The trifecta is a diagnostic and covers exfiltration only (structural): He concedes the limit himself. The lethal trifecta "only covers the risk of data exfiltration: there are plenty of other, even nastier risks," and Meta's Agents Rule of Two "neatly solves this, through the addition of 'changing state' as a property to consider." A security team that adopts the three ingredients as a threat model inherits that blindness, because an agent that destroys or corrupts what it can reach needs no way to communicate outward, so the third ingredient is absent and the frame does not fire. Meta's rule also scopes its test to what an agent can do "within a session," a boundary the trifecta post does not carry. The post also says plainly that no defense is known to hold every time, so the three ingredients can gate a capability grant without standing as evidence that the grant is safe.
- **[L:3]** Containment means the model that decides never reads the content (structural): The Dual LLM pattern works because unfiltered output from the quarantined model never reaches the privileged one. What the privileged model gets instead are variable names standing in for whatever the quarantined model read, so every workflow has to be expressible as operations on opaque references. Whether any production system runs the pattern is not documented either way in the record behind this page, so its standing rests on the security literature that names it and says nothing about operation at scale.
- **[L:4]** The analogy that made the name travel is the thing defenders unlearn (structural): Prompt injection was named by analogy with SQL injection, and the analogy implies a fix by escaping that does not exist. Dave Chismon of the UK NCSC makes the case that "under the hood of an LLM, there's no distinction made between 'data' or 'instructions'; there is only ever 'next token'," so classical confused deputy problems can be mitigated while LLMs stay "inherently confusable." His named target is security professionals with web application experience who assume the two attacks are alike. Willison set that contrast up himself in 2022 and reached the same conclusion, so the limit falls on the name while the substance holds.
- **[L:5]** Nothing published argues against him (local): Two searches for a durable critique of the frames on this page returned adoption: institutional uptake, vendor explainers, defensive research he covered approvingly. The NCSC post credits him and reaches his own 2022 conclusion. The CaMeL and design-patterns papers propose defenses he endorsed. Meta's rule extends the trifecta with his blessing. The page records the absence rather than promoting a friendly post into a hostile one, and a reader should treat the absence as untested rather than as agreement, given how recent the frames are.
- **[L:6]** The corpus is written from outside a security function (local): The career runs through Django, the Guardian, Lanyrd, Eventbrite and Datasette, with no security role in it. What he brings is a web developer's reading of a new attack class, published daily and corrected in public. What he does not bring is the experience of defending a running estate under a budget, which is where the gap between a good frame and an operable control usually shows up.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** The frames name combinations rather than techniques, which is why they survive model releases that obsolete specific attacks. The lethal trifecta describes a configuration, the Dual LLM pattern describes a topology, and the rule about defenses made of more AI describes what evidence a security control has to be able to produce. All three keep working when the underlying models change. The naming discipline is load-bearing in its own right for anyone who has to buy or govern agent systems, because the vocabulary is now arriving from OWASP, from vendors, and from research notes that no longer cite where it came from.

- **[N:charity-majors:thesis]** Charity Majors's thesis: Complex systems fail in ways their builders did not predict, so the useful question about telemetry is whether it can answer a question formed after the failure began, whatever it collects along the way. Pre-aggregated metrics cannot, because the decision about what to count was made before the question existed. Her remedy is to record one wide, structured event per unit of work, keep its high-cardinality fields intact, and derive dashboards, traces and alerts from that single record at read time. The same instinct drives her other positions: staging environments never match production, so the useful testing happens in production behind guardrails, and management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering, so good engineers should be able to move between the two.
- **[E:out:rich-hickey]** parallel, primary → Rich Hickey (has a page): The same argument in different material. Hickey's guardrail programming says tests and type checkers are safety nets, and the ability to reason about a program still has to come from the design. Willison says a filter made of another model is a safety net, and the security property still has to come from the architecture.
- **[N:rich-hickey:thesis]** Rich Hickey's thesis: Reliability depends on being able to understand a system, and people can only reason about a few things at a time, so the property that matters most is whether the parts of a system are intertwined. Simplicity in that sense is objective and chosen; ease is relative to the person and says nothing about how tangled the result is. Incidental complexity enters when teams pick familiar constructs (mutable objects, in-place updates, breaking version changes) that braid together things the problem kept separate. The remedy is to build from values, plain data and composable pieces, and to treat tests and type systems as safety nets that cannot make a tangled design simple. (The premise that simplicity is a prerequisite for reliability is Dijkstra's, quoted at the start of "Simple Made Easy.")
- **[E:out:martin-fowler]** parallel, primary → Martin Fowler (has a page): Fowler's bounded context draws a line where the language changes, which is also where authority usually changes. Willison's frames describe what goes wrong when no such line exists: a model that reads instructions and outside text through one channel has no boundary to place authority against.
- **[N:martin-fowler:thesis]** Martin Fowler's thesis: Software is a perishable medium for shared understanding. It rots when nobody is tending it, and the cost of letting it rot is paid not in some distant future but in next week's velocity. The way to keep software cheap to change is to evolve practice in public: name the moves, share the vocabulary, run the build-test-deploy loop tight, place the boundaries where change wants to flow, model the domain in the language the team already uses with the customer, and write the practice down as you discover it. Quality IS speed. Naming the implicit is how the argument becomes negotiable. The catalog, the bliki entry, the pattern, the named refactoring, the smell: these are the cartographer's instruments.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:charity-majors]** Majors judges telemetry by one test: can an engineer ask a question of a live system that nobody thought to ask while designing it. Pre-aggregated metrics fail that test, because the choice of what to count was made before the question existed. Her answer is one wide structured record per unit of work, with its high-cardinality fields kept whole, so views can be derived from it at read time instead of being fixed at write time. The fields she insists on keeping are the ones that identify a single request: user id, build id, feature flag. The pattern came from Scuba at Facebook, and the company she co-founded sells it. The same instinct runs through her other positions, that staging cannot reproduce production and that management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering.
- **[R:rich-hickey]** "Simple Made Easy" pulls apart two words the industry treats as one: simple means nothing is interleaved, a property of the artifact, and easy means near at hand, a property of the person. Hickey locates complexity in complecting, the braiding together of concerns that could stay independent. A familiar tool can bring complexity the problem never needed, which he calls incidental complexity, his name for what Brooks called accidental. Tests and type checkers catch mistakes after the fact, and in his account they are safety nets that cannot untangle a design. The later talks carry the same concern into data and change. Overwriting a record in place discards the fact it replaced, and removing anything from a published interface breaks whoever depends on it. He scopes these arguments to long-running information systems, and his case against static types remains contested.
- **[R:martin-fowler]** In Fowler's account, software is a medium for shared understanding, and it decays whenever nobody tends it. The cost of that decay arrives quickly, in next week's velocity, and his design stamina hypothesis makes a parallel claim that internal quality pays back within weeks. His method is to name the moves (refactoring, code smell, strangler fig, bounded context), attribute each to its origin, and revise them in public on the bliki. Naming turns tacit design choices into distinctions a team can argue about. The method has limits of its own: his 2014 microservices article undersold the cost of distribution, and later follow-ups had to correct it.
