## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Cunningham's subject is the distance between a program and what the people who own it currently understand about the problem. Code written to reflect a partial understanding is legitimate and useful in his account, so long as the understanding is allowed to sharpen and the code is rewritten promptly to match. What costs money is the gap left open. Every minute spent working against not-quite-right code is interest on that debt, and an entire engineering organization can be brought to a standstill under the load. The wiki is the same premise applied to prose. A group working out a shared understanding needs a medium cheap enough to revise that people who would not normally author will edit it, which is why he made the editing surface as thin as he could and delegated control to the people using it. Both positions rest on a repair step that somebody has to actually take, and on a community that can be trusted to take it.
- **[T:working-line]** I'm never in favor of writing code poorly, but I am in favor of writing code to reflect your current understanding of a problem even if that understanding is partial. ("Debt Metaphor," 2009.)
- **[F:technical-debt]** Technical debt (original): The cost of code that no longer matches what its team understands about the problem. Interest is paid in the friction of working against the mismatch, and the debt is cleared by rewriting the code to fit the current understanding. Cunningham credits the method behind the frame, choosing a metaphor deliberately because metaphors govern how a team reasons, to George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By*.
- **[F:wikiwikiweb]** The wiki (original): A site that anyone reading it can edit, with the editing surface made thin enough that people who would not normally author will. He built the first one as an automated supplement to the Portland Pattern Repository and installed it in March 1995, choosing wiki-wiki as an alliterative substitute for quick. The antecedent is his own HyperCard stack from the late 1980s, which was single user.
- **[F:simplest-thing-that-could-possibly-work]** The simplest thing that could possibly work (original): A question to ask "if you're not sure what to do yet," put to Kent Beck across a Smalltalk session whenever the pair had been stuck for more than a minute. The aim is to get something on the screen and then look at it. That qualifier travels with the frame or the frame misstates him, and his own limit is that the question reached the field as a command.
- **[F:pattern-languages-for-programs]** Pattern languages for programs (extended, with kent-beck, christopher-alexander): The proposal, written with Kent Beck in a September 1987 technical report, that the design of programs could be set down as a pattern language in the sense Christopher Alexander gave the term for buildings. The paper credits the borrowing inside its own proposal sentence. Beck is first byline on that report, as he is on the CRC paper. CRC cards sit under Beck's name here because his page carries that frame, and this one stays with Cunningham. His stated purpose for the first wiki was to link people's experience together and discover the pattern language of programming.
- **[F:crc-cards]** CRC cards (co-authored, with ward-cunningham): Index cards carrying a class, its responsibilities and its collaborators, proposed with Kent Beck at OOPSLA in October 1989 as a teaching tool and picked up afterwards by expert designers. Beck holds this frame on his own page and the credit there is complete, so it sits in the deep tier here. The same late-1980s HyperCard stack that led to the wiki produced it.
- **[F:federated-wiki]** Federated wiki (original): A wiki built around federated sharing, drag refactoring and data visualization, in the repository's own description. He started it in 2011 and still works on it. The flagship repository has been archived since 2016 and no use by other practitioners was found, so it sits here as live practice rather than as vocabulary anyone else carries.
- **[F:fit-framework]** Fit (original): A tool, in the project's own words, for enhancing collaboration in software development. Cunningham created it and James Shore coordinates the project, which makes Shore a coordinator rather than a co-creator. No year appears beside it because the only date the project site carries is a copyright notice on site content.
- **[L:1]** The wiki works only where control can be delegated (structural): He states the precondition himself, in 2003: "wikis work best in environments where you're comfortable delegating control to the users of the system." Two sentences later he gives the condition behind the condition, that this works where you have "a trusting community that isn't looking for the computer to enforce some behavior." Then he draws the boundary: "Corporations that wouldn't trust their own employees to be able to maintain a web site need something other than wiki." That is a designer naming the assumption his design cannot survive without, and it excludes most settings a security or compliance function operates in. Whether the largest descendants of the wiki added page protection and dispute resolution as open editing scaled is the empirical test of the claim, and no source was checked for it here.
- **[L:2]** The corrections never travelled with the words (structural): Twice a phrase of his reached practitioners with the condition that made it true stripped off, and the second of the two drew a corrective from the community on his own wiki. Bloggers explained the debt metaphor and, in his words, "confused it, I think, with the idea that you could write code poorly with the intention of doing a good job later." The question about the simplest thing that could possibly work arrived as a command, which he says makes it complicated, because a team then has to prove a thing is simplest. The community on his own wiki had to post a warning above the phrase saying its history and therefore its meaning are lost on many people who wield it. Cunningham's Law is a different case: Steven McGeady named it after him, after advice he gave at Tektronix, so the phrase travelling under his name was never a coinage of his at all. Each time his answer was to go back on the record, and each time the record reached fewer people than the phrase.
- **[L:3]** The metaphor is worked on one side only (local): Both primary statements of technical debt develop the interest: minutes spent against not-quite-right code, an organization brought to a standstill under the load. Neither says what repayment costs, how a team should price a rewrite against the work it displaces, or how to tell a debt worth carrying from one worth clearing now. Later writers supplied that, and Fowler's quadrant is one such attempt. A team reaching for the metaphor to justify a remediation budget is using an analogy that argues the cost of waiting and says little about the cost of paying, beyond the assurance that objects make the transaction tolerable.
- **[L:4]** Federated wiki has no uptake outside his own practice (local): He has worked on federated wiki since 2011 and keynoted on it in 2024, so it is live rather than abandoned. The flagship repository has been archived since a last push in May 2016, and no use by practitioners other than him was found. It sits in the deep tier and in the Live Edge here for that reason. A reader looking for the next thing with the reach of the wiki should not assume this is it.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** The vocabulary is still the best available, which is the point rather than an accident. Technical debt is the phrase the industry uses for the thing, thirty-four years after it reached print, with tooling built to compute a figure from it and a working practitioner borrowing its shape to name a second debt. The wiki's design assumption, that the cheapest possible editing surface plus a community you can trust beats a permission model, is the design question a system that opens its pages to its readers still has to answer. And the pattern of a word outrunning its condition is itself the transferable finding, a fact about how vocabulary moves through an industry.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:kent-beck]** parallel, primary → Kent Beck (has a page): Beck stands on the other side of three of the artifacts here. He is first byline on the CRC cards paper and on the pattern-languages report, and he is the person the simplest-thing question was put to, across a Smalltalk session, whenever the pair had been stuck for more than a minute. Beck's page carries CRC cards as the shared frame and credits Cunningham in full, so the traffic runs both ways.
- **[N:kent-beck:thesis]** Kent Beck's thesis: Software costs what changing it costs. Beck states that as Constantine's Equivalence, cost(software) ~= cost(change), and credits the coupling and cohesion work in *Structured Design* for the underlying result. Coupling is then the thing to watch, because it is what makes one change drag others behind it, and design is whatever brings it down. The work happens in tidyings, small changes to structure that leave behavior alone. The two kinds of change are asymmetrical. A structure change can be undone, while a behavior change that has reached customers cannot, which is the reason his practices favor small steps with a test behind each one. Whether to do the structural work first is an investment question rather than a matter of virtue, and his own answer runs both ways: discounted cash flow argues for earning sooner and spending later, options argue for spending now, and the decision is the inequality between them.
- **[E:out:martin-fowler]** extends, primary → Martin Fowler (has a page): Fowler carried both of Cunningham's forms forward. His Technical Debt Quadrant (2009) partitions the metaphor into deliberate against inadvertent and prudent against reckless, and puts Cunningham's own meaning approvingly in one cell as the kind of debt that Ward talked about. The bliki is the wiki form crossed with a blog, and Fowler's own post defining the word says Cunningham is the one who named it.
- **[N:martin-fowler:thesis]** Martin Fowler's thesis: Software is a perishable medium for shared understanding. It rots when nobody is tending it, and the cost of letting it rot is paid not in some distant future but in next week's velocity. The way to keep software cheap to change is to evolve practice in public: name the moves, share the vocabulary, run the build-test-deploy loop tight, place the boundaries where change wants to flow, model the domain in the language the team already uses with the customer, and write the practice down as you discover it. Quality IS speed. Naming the implicit is how the argument becomes negotiable. The catalog, the bliki entry, the pattern, the named refactoring, the smell: these are the cartographer's instruments.
- **[E:out:will-larson]** extends, primary → Will Larson (has a page): Larson borrowed the shape of the debt metaphor to coin a second one. Exception debt is what an organization accumulates when it spends real time granting exceptions to its own policies, and his repair is to batch the escalations and rewrite the policy rather than work them one at a time. A working practitioner reaching for this analogy to name something new is direct evidence of how far it travelled.
- **[N:will-larson:thesis]** Will Larson's thesis: Larson's subject is the written machinery of an engineering organization, and the recurring move across the corpus is to name a way one of those pieces decays and then prescribe the repair. A policy that spends significant time granting exceptions has exception debt, and working the exceptions one at a time is what sustains it; the repair is to batch a month of requests and merge the escalations into the next revision. An engineering strategy usually already exists and has simply never been written down, so writing it down solves half the problem (the structure he writes into is Richard Rumelt's diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action, which he credits by name). A team sits in one of four states and the state decides what it needs, in what he calls a loose framework. The staff engineer archetypes came the other way around, out of interviews with people already in the roles rather than from a mechanism. Holding all of it up is his account of authority: the power that comes with a title is loaned by a sponsor, can be retracted, and lasts only while its holder stays aligned with whoever bestowed it (the conferral-and-revocation structure is French and Raven's legitimate power, 1959; the loan framing and the maintenance condition it places on the holder are his).
- **[E:out:christopher-alexander]** antecedent, secondary → Christopher Alexander: A Pattern Language (1977) gave the word pattern its architectural sense, and the 1987 report with Beck credits him inside its own proposal sentence, adapting concepts from his work. The software pattern movement, the Portland Pattern Repository and the wiki built to hold its literature all descend from that borrowing.
- **[E:out:george-lakoff]** antecedent, secondary → George Lakoff: Metaphors We Live By, written with Mark Johnson, is the credited premise under technical debt. Cunningham opens his own account of the metaphor by saying he became interested in how metaphors influence thinking after reading it, and that we reason by analogy with the metaphors that have entered our language. The debt analogy is his; the conviction that the choice of analogy changes how a team reasons is theirs.

## Current reading (input only when redrafting, not a source)

> Cunningham puts a price on the gap between a program and what its owning team now understands about the problem. Writing code to a partial understanding is legitimate in his account, and the interest is the friction of working against the gap until it is rewritten. The wiki applies the same premise to prose, with an editing surface thin enough that people who would not normally author will revise what is there. What the site takes is the pattern across the vocabulary. Two phrases he coined reached practitioners with the condition stripped: debt as permission to write bad code, the simplest thing as a command. Cunningham's Law carries his name and was never his. He answered the first two on the record, and his own wiki's community posted a warning above the phrase for later arrivals. The correction reached fewer people than the phrase each time. Vocabulary that needs a warning label has stopped belonging to its author.

> Technical debt is the phrase engineering leadership reaches for when it budgets remediation, which makes the condition attached to it a spending question. His repayment model assumes a team whose understanding of the problem outran the code, because a rewrite needs something to be rewritten toward. Generated code strains that assumption, since it arrives faster than anyone's understanding of it. Where that gap opens, a plan funded as paying down AI debt is priced against an alignment nobody ever held. What the work actually buys is comprehension the estate is missing, a different job from the rewrite his metaphor describes, and one that runs alongside the boundaries constraining code nobody has read. The precondition is an owner for the problem model the estate should match, and generated volume supplies none. The risk is that AI debt becomes the next phrase to arrive without its condition and fund whatever was already planned.
