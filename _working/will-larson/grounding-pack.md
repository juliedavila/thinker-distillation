## The thinker (spine, handles, limits)

- **[T:thesis]** Larson's subject is the written machinery of an engineering organization, and the recurring move across the corpus is to name a way one of those pieces decays and then prescribe the repair. A policy that spends significant time granting exceptions has exception debt, and working the exceptions one at a time is what sustains it; the repair is to batch a month of requests and merge the escalations into the next revision. An engineering strategy usually already exists and has simply never been written down, so writing it down solves half the problem (the structure he writes into is Richard Rumelt's diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action, which he credits by name). A team sits in one of four states and the state decides what it needs, in what he calls a loose framework. The staff engineer archetypes came the other way around, out of interviews with people already in the roles rather than from a mechanism. Holding all of it up is his account of authority: the power that comes with a title is loaned by a sponsor, can be retracted, and lasts only while its holder stays aligned with whoever bestowed it (the conferral-and-revocation structure is French and Raven's legitimate power, 1959; the loan framing and the maintenance condition it places on the holder are his).
- **[T:working-line]** Titles come with the sort of power called organizational authority, and that variety of authority is loaned to you by a greater organizational authority. ("Staying aligned with authority," staffeng.com.)
- **[F:staff-engineer-archetypes]** The staff engineer archetypes (original): Four shapes the staff engineer job takes: Tech Lead, Architect, Solver and Right Hand. Larson built the set from interviews and describes the clusters as emerging from those conversations rather than from a theory. The titles themselves are much older than the taxonomy, and Google was filling staff engineer roles a decade before StaffEng existed, so what is his is the taxonomy and the literature around it. His 2020 post called the first archetype Team Lead; the guide and the book settled on Tech Lead, which is the form that travels.
- **[F:staff-plus]** Staff-plus (popularized): One label over the three titles that sit above senior engineer: Staff, Principal and Distinguished. StaffEng collects career stories from all three under the single term. It now circulates without him: Gergely Orosz defined Staff+ for his readers in 2023 as established industry vocabulary and did not mention Larson.
- **[F:exception-debt]** Exception debt (original): What an organization accumulates when it spends significant time granting exceptions to its own policies. The prescription is to stop working the exceptions one at a time: document the problem, batch the requests for a month, then merge the escalations into a new revision of the policy. The name borrows the debt metaphor Ward Cunningham introduced for technical debt in 1992; the diagnosis is Larson's and credits no prior source.
- **[F:loaned-organizational-authority]** Organizational authority is loaned by a sponsor (extended, with john-r-p-french, bertram-raven): The power that comes with a title is bestowed by a greater authority, can be retracted, and holds only while its holder stays deeply aligned with the sponsor who bestowed it, usually a direct manager. The underlying idea is older than the essay: French and Raven's legitimate power (1959) already located authority in designation by a legitimizing agent and treated that legitimacy as revocable by whoever granted it. Larson's own contribution is the loan framing and the maintenance condition it places on the holder.
- **[F:engineering-strategy-as-written-practice]** Writing the engineering strategy down (extended, with richard-rumelt): Most companies already have an engineering strategy and have never written it down, so writing it down solves half the problem. The structure Larson writes into is Richard Rumelt's kernel of diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action, and he credits Rumelt for it in terms. What he adds is the engineering split into three artifacts: a vision of how the technology and the organization should work in two or three years, context-specific strategies, and technical specifications.
- **[F:four-team-states]** The four team states (original): A team is falling behind, treading water, repaying debt, or innovating, and the state decides what it needs. Falling behind means the backlog is longer every week; innovating means technical debt is sustainably low, morale is high, and most of the work serves new user needs. He presents it as a loose framework of his own for reasoning about what a given team needs, and pairs each state with its own system fix: hiring, process, time, or slack.
- **[F:team-sizing-heuristics]** Team sizing heuristics (distributed): Numbers for organization design: a manager supports six to eight engineers, a manager of managers supports four to six managers, an oncall rotation wants eight engineers, and a group under four is not a team. Span-of-control ratios are much older than the post, running back to Graicunas in 1933 and Urwick after him, and the recommended number varies by author. Larson presents his as an iterated framework of his own and credits no prior source.
- **[F:systems-models-for-engineering-decisions]** Systems models for engineering decisions (extended, with donella-meadows): Modelling part of an engineering organization as stocks and flows before deciding anything about it. Larson sends readers to Donella Meadows for the fundamentals and names *Thinking in Systems* as the place to learn them; what he supplies is worked engineering cases, including four systems models among the chapters of the strategy book.
- **[F:strategy-testing]** Strategy testing (original): His named method for adoption work: identify a few goals, pick an initial approach, then iterate rapidly in the details until the approach genuinely works. Defined first-person in December 2025 and too recent to show whether anyone outside his own writing has picked it up.
- **[L:1]** The archetypes are an artifact of who was interviewed (structural): Larson built the taxonomy by talking to engineers who already held the title, and he describes the four clusters as emerging from those conversations. Sean Goedecke's 2026 argument reaches the same warning by a different route: the archetypes do not work as goals, because "You don't become a technical leader by trying really hard to be a technical leader," and "Important projects are usually assigned top-down." He grants that Larson wrote description and that readers turned it into prescription. A reader using the four names to plan a promotion is using them for something the sampling method cannot support.
- **[L:2]** The escape from falling behind is hiring, and not every function has one (structural): The four team states and the sizing heuristics were both published in 2018, out of Uber and Stripe, high-growth and well funded companies. A team that is falling behind escapes by adding people, and the sizing ratios describe an organization that can staff to them. In a function with flat headcount, a contractor-heavy roster, or a regulated coverage requirement, that escape is missing and the ratios become a description of somebody else's company. No published critic makes this point; the site makes it in its own voice.
- **[L:3]** Most of the frames have no recorded life outside his writing (structural): The archetypes travel, and Staff-plus travels further. Exception debt, the loan account of authority, the four team states, the sizing heuristics and strategy testing do not, as far as a search can show. Both "exception debt" and the loan account turn up in book summaries, reading notes and quote collections, which is circulation rather than practitioners reaching for them. That does not make the mechanisms wrong, and it does mean a reader adopting them is running an experiment rather than joining a practice.
- **[L:4]** The strategy material is Rumelt applied (local): Diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action is Richard Rumelt's kernel, and Larson says so himself. The systems modelling points readers at Donella Meadows, and the Wardley map chapters credit Simon Wardley and David Anderson. The engineering examples are real work and the underlying theory is borrowed, so a reader who wants the theory should go to the originals and treat the books as the application layer.
- **[T:still-load-bearing]** Exception debt survives every limit above, because it is checkable from records an organization already keeps. Count the exceptions granted against a policy over a quarter, and the count answers whether the policy still describes how the work is done, without an audit or an interview. The loan account of authority holds for a similar reason: it predicts something, which is that a mandate thins when its sponsor's attention moves, and anyone who has watched a reorganization can test that against what they saw. The archetypes hold as description, which is what he said they were.

## Outbound edges (this page points at)

- **[E:out:camille-fournier]** parallel, primary → Camille Fournier (has a page): The two tracks above senior engineer, described by the two people who documented them. Fournier maps the management sequence one role at a time; Larson catalogues the roles on the individual-contributor side and asks where their authority comes from, since none of it arrives with a reporting line. Exception debt and loaned authority are the parts of his corpus her books do not reach.
- **[N:camille-fournier:thesis]** Camille Fournier's thesis: Engineering leadership is mostly the work of managing complexity that code alone does not absorb. On the people side, each step of a management career changes the work itself, from building to coaching to designing organizations, and people fail when they keep doing the job they were promoted out of. On the systems side, every team making its own technology choices produces integration code that no one owns, and that code slows the whole organization over time. The remedy in both cases is deliberate design of the role, or of the platform, around the people who have to live with it (her platform book names this "the accidental complexity of human coordination," in Brooks's vocabulary).
- **[E:out:charity-majors]** parallel, primary → Charity Majors (has a page): Two answers to the same question about what happens after senior engineer. Majors argues the strongest technical leaders swing between management and hands-on work; Larson describes a track that stays technical and treats influence without command as the problem to be solved.
- **[N:charity-majors:thesis]** Charity Majors's thesis: Complex systems fail in ways their builders did not predict, so the useful question about telemetry is whether it can answer a question formed after the failure began, whatever it collects along the way. Pre-aggregated metrics cannot, because the decision about what to count was made before the question existed. Her remedy is to record one wide, structured event per unit of work, keep its high-cardinality fields intact, and derive dashboards, traces and alerts from that single record at read time. The same instinct drives her other positions: staging environments never match production, so the useful testing happens in production behind guardrails, and management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering, so good engineers should be able to move between the two.
- **[E:out:sidney-dekker]** parallel, primary → Sidney Dekker (has a page): Both watch a written rule lose contact with the work it governs, by different mechanisms. Dekker's drift into failure explains why nobody notices the deviation accumulating; Larson's exception debt starts after everyone has noticed, because each exception was requested, argued and granted on the record, and it prescribes a cadence for rewriting the rule around what was granted anyway.
- **[N:sidney-dekker:thesis]** Sidney Dekker's thesis: Treat human error as a *consequence*, not a cause. The investigator's job is not to determine what someone failed to do, but to reconstruct how the situation made sense to them at the time, because if it made sense, it will make sense again, and the next operator in that position will make the same choice. *Just culture* names the institutional posture that makes this kind of investigation possible. *Drift into failure* names the slow-motion process by which good organizations end up in bad places without anyone making a single obviously wrong decision. *Safety differently* names the prescription: invest in human adaptive capacity, not procedural compliance.
- **[E:out:nicole-forsgren]** extends, primary → Nicole Forsgren (has a page): He endorsed Accelerate's definition of developer productivity when it arrived, calling it one of the best he had encountered, and later named DORA, SPACE and DX Core 4 together as great frameworks. His argument sits on top of them rather than against them: output metrics are effective at showing whether something improved and poor at directing attention toward improving it, so a measurement program needs a theory of improvement beside its numbers.
- **[N:nicole-forsgren:thesis]** Nicole Forsgren's thesis: Engineering performance is empirically tractable, and the instruments to prove it are real. The intuition that good software organizations differ structurally from bad ones, in kind rather than merely in degree, turns out to be testable, and the testing keeps confirming the claim. Throughput and stability do not trade off in well-run systems; they correlate. Elite performers cluster; the gap between elite and low is not normally distributed. The measurement substrate is operational: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore service. The decomposition is multi-dimensional: satisfaction, performance, activity, communication, efficiency become the SPACE; feedback loops, cognitive load, flow state become DevEx. Each layer is a sharper instrument for the same underlying argument: engineering work is a system, the system has lawful properties, the properties can be measured, and the measurement changes how the work gets organized. The cost of skipping the empirical work is that every measurement argument stays a measurement *argument* instead of a measurement program.
- **[E:out:richard-rumelt]** antecedent, secondary → Richard Rumelt: *Good Strategy, Bad Strategy* supplies the kernel the engineering strategy work is built on: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action. Larson calls it the most approachable book on strategy he has read and says Rumelt's views shaped how he thinks about engineering strategy.
- **[E:out:sean-goedecke]** critiques, secondary → Sean Goedecke: "Why I don't like the 'staff engineer archetypes'" (2026) argues the archetypes fail as goals, because important projects are assigned top-down and nobody becomes a technical leader by trying to be one. He is fair about where the fault lies, granting that Larson wrote anthropological description and readers turned it into a how-to, and he opens by calling the essay the most influential writing about staff engineers in a decade.
- **[E:out:tanya-reilly]** parallel, secondary → Tanya Reilly: Wrote the foreword to *Staff Engineer*, which she states in her own voice, and then wrote the other in-depth book on the same track, *The Staff Engineer's Path* (O'Reilly, 2022). The two books cover one career from different distances.

## Neighbor readings (for the novelty check only; do not echo)

- **[R:camille-fournier]** Fournier treats engineering leadership as a series of distinct jobs, and *The Manager's Path* maps them one role at a time, from mentoring to running an organization. Its central advice is that each step changes the work itself, so people struggle when they keep doing the job they were promoted out of. *Platform Engineering*, written with Ian Nowland, turns the same attention to internal systems. Its core diagnosis is the over-general swamp: every team picks its own building blocks and writes its own glue, so applications get easier to build and harder to maintain. The book's answer is a platform curated like a product that teams choose to use, because standards imposed by authority do not hold.
- **[R:charity-majors]** Majors judges telemetry by one test: can an engineer ask a question of a live system that nobody thought to ask while designing it. Pre-aggregated metrics fail that test, because the choice of what to count was made before the question existed. Her answer is one wide structured record per unit of work, with its high-cardinality fields kept whole, so views can be derived from it at read time instead of being fixed at write time. The fields she insists on keeping are the ones that identify a single request: user id, build id, feature flag. The pattern came from Scuba at Facebook, and the company she co-founded sells it. The same instinct runs through her other positions, that staging cannot reproduce production and that management is a different job rather than a rung above engineering.
- **[R:sidney-dekker]** Dekker's starting point is that human error is a consequence of system conditions, so the investigator's job is to reconstruct how an action made sense to the person at the time. If it made sense once, it will make sense to the next person in the same position. Just culture draws the accountability line: people answer for willful violations, while honest mistakes and system-induced failures become material for learning. Drift into failure explains how organizations reach unsafe states through many small, reasonable choices toward efficiency, none of them obviously wrong. The frame works best when used surgically, because invoking drift for every incident can close off the question of which decisions could have gone differently.
- **[R:nicole-forsgren]** The part of Forsgren that travels furthest is a finding: in well-run delivery systems, throughput and stability rise together. That finding removes the most common argument for slowing change down, the belief that each additional deploy spends safety. DORA's stability measures, change failure rate and time to restore, make the safety question observable: how often a change degrades service, and how fast the team recovers when one does. Because DORA is itself the doctrine here, the four keys belong to the delivery system they were built to measure. Turning them into targets for teams invites the misuse the frame's own limits describe, where people manage to the number until it stops describing the system.
