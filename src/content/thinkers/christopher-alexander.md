tagline: The architect who wrote, with co-authors, the pattern vocabulary software borrowed in 1987, and who stood in front of a room of programmers in 1996 to say what a pattern language was supposed to do besides.
thirty_second_take: >
  Alexander designed buildings and towns by writing patterns: short statements
  of a problem and a current best guess at what will resolve it, gathered into
  what he and his co-authors called a pattern language. The 1977 book says all
  253 patterns together form a language, and both his books say a language is
  worked through in sequence, so that the patterns compose into something
  coherent. His name for the instructions that do that is generative schemes,
  which he traces to traditional building cultures rather than to any theory of
  his own. Software borrowed the vocabulary in 1987, and the catalogue that
  descends from that borrowing sold more than half a million copies in English
  and thirteen other languages. Standing in front of the OOPSLA audience in
  1996, Alexander named three things beyond the format that "the pattern
  language that we began creating in the 1970s" was built to do: carry
  a moral component, aim at coherence, and generate. Whether software had taken those he would not say,
  telling the room he was ignorant of their field and did not know whether the
  features had yet been translated into it. He said separately, in the same
  talk, that the instruction to work through a language in order had dropped
  out of sight and was not emphasized in use.

background: >
  Born in Vienna in 1936 and educated in England, at Trinity College,
  Cambridge, where he took a bachelor's in architecture and a master's in
  mathematics. He took a doctorate at Harvard, described as the first PhD in
  Architecture awarded there, on a dissertation completed in 1962, and worked
  at MIT on transportation theory and computer science. In 1963 he moved to
  Berkeley to accept an appointment as Professor of Architecture and held that
  appointment for decades. He founded the Center
  for Environmental Structure, which the 1987 software report calls him the
  founder of and his vitae gives him as president of from 1967, and the book
  series the pattern work appears in carries its name. He
  also built, which matters to how the writing reads: the Eishin Campus near
  Tokyo, the West Dean Visitors Centre in Sussex, the Julian Street Inn in San
  Jose, houses in Albany and Martinez in California, and low-cost housing in
  Mexicali, Mexico. He died in Sussex in 2022. The mathematics and the
  construction sit on either side of the work, which is why the patterns arrive
  as hypotheses about what will work for the people who end up living inside
  them.
why_they_matter_as_a_thinker: >
  The vocabulary outran the field it was written for. Kent Beck and Ward
  Cunningham adapted his concepts for programming in a technical report dated
  17 September 1987, crediting the borrowing inside its own proposal sentence,
  and citing one work of his in the bibliography as "Christopher Alexander et.
  al., A Pattern Language." From there it went to the Gang of Four's catalogue
  in 1994, the first Pattern Languages of Programming conference the same year,
  and the Portland Pattern Repository in 1995, then out again to pedagogical
  patterns, systems architecture, design science, community organising,
  permaculture and the wiki, which a 2013 paper co-authored by Cunningham
  describes as built to share patterns. What makes the ideas portable is the
  form: a pattern names a problem and a resolution compactly enough that
  someone can carry it into a field Alexander never worked in. That portability
  is also what he came to OOPSLA to qualify.

thesis: >
  A pattern language was built to generate, and the mechanism is order. Each
  pattern states a problem and what Alexander calls a current best guess at
  what will resolve it, and the 253 in the 1977 book are meant to be worked
  through in sequence so that what comes out is coherent. His name for the
  instructions that do this is generative schemes, sets of steps that let a
  person or a group make a coherent artifact simply. The claim
  underneath is that coherence cannot be assembled out of parts chosen one at
  a time, however good each part is alone.

  Software took the pattern; whether it took the sequence he declined to rule
  on. At OOPSLA in 1996 he named three things a pattern language is for besides
  its format: a moral component, the aim
  of creating coherence, and generativity. He would not say they were missing
  here, only that he did not know whether they had been translated into the
  field. On the mechanism he was exact, quoting his own two books to say that
  the language is used sequentially and that in practice the instruction
  dropped out of sight and was not emphasized in use. That last sentence
  describes the fate of his own languages. It lays no charge against
  programmers. The charge is this page's inference from the two.
working_line: >
  In fact, both A Pattern Language and The Timeless Way Of Building say that the pattern
  language is to be used sequentially. In practice, however, this feature
  dropped out of sight, and was not emphasized in use. (OOPSLA keynote, San
  Jose, October 1996.)

reading: >
  Sequence is the working part of a pattern language. Each of the 253 patterns
  in the 1977 book states a problem and a current best guess at its resolution,
  and in order they are meant to compose into a whole. He calls the instructions
  that do that generative schemes. Of his own languages he said at OOPSLA in
  1996 that the sequential instruction was not emphasized in use. That gap is
  between a language and a catalogue: the same entries, without the mechanism.
  The catalogue is what got copied into practice, from the 1987 report
  through Design Patterns into fields with no
  buildings. On whether his condition carries across he stopped short, saying he
  does not know whether the moral component exists in computer science. That
  leaves the order claim, which any library of patterns can be asked about.
extension: >
  A software factory ontology puts security constraints inside the spec, which
  makes the security content an enumeration of objects. Alexander's claim is
  what an enumeration cannot answer, that a coherent whole comes from applying
  parts in an order rather than from each part being right alone. Read that way,
  the missing field on a control object is its dependence: what must hold before
  it applies, and what it opens next. Auditability then has an order to judge
  against, since a dated record of what happened asserts no required sequence.
  Someone has to own the sequence, since one nobody may change is a waterfall
  and one nobody enforces is a comment. The risk runs two ways: the order
  hardens into another gate, and a control whose precondition goes unmet never
  fires at all. Outside his own books the sequential reading survives as an
  argument about what makes a language a language, and not as a practice anyone
  was found running.

limits:
  - headline: He did not claim his own transfer condition held
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      The first of the three things he names is a moral component, and in the
      same passage he says of it: "I do not know whether that sort of moral
      component exists in computer science." He says the same of all three at
      once, that he does not know whether they "have yet been translated" into
      the discipline, and opens the whole evaluation by calling himself
      ignorant of the field. That is his own uncertainty, on the record. So on that count the
      1996 verdict may be a statement about the limits of his own transfer
      rather than a diagnosis of anyone's inattention. A
      reader who turns it into an indictment of engineers has gone past what he
      was willing to say, and a leader who reads it as an ethics mandate has
      gone further still.
  - headline: The part that travelled is the format
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      That software kept the format and dropped the sequence is this page's
      reading. He granted the format and then declined to say what had crossed,
      so the verdict is not his. Against that reading, here is where the reach
      actually is.
      The format has been in use by other fields since 1987, across
      programming, teaching, systems architecture, design science, community
      organising and permaculture. The sequential reading has thinner going. It
      survives in the field's own argument about whether a body of patterns is
      a language or "merely a catalog of patterns," which Fowler describes in
      2006 as a matter of one pattern leading to the next, but no practice was
      found that works a language through in order the way the books ask. Read
      one way that confirms him. Read another way it raises the question his
      talk does not answer, which is whether a pattern has to keep the sequence
      to be worth carrying.
  - headline: Most of the later vocabulary has no reach to point at
    severity: local
    elaboration: >
      *The Timeless Way of Building* introduces the quality without a name, and
      the four volumes of *The Nature of Order* set out centers,
      structure-preserving transformations and living structure. Only the first
      was found in use outside architecture, in Richard Gabriel's 1996 essays
      on software. No use of the other three outside it was found in this
      round's search, and the fullest available account of those volumes
      carries an active neutrality dispute, which bounds what the search could
      establish about them.
      Anyone who meets Alexander through the software
      borrowing is meeting a narrow slice of him, and the rest has not been
      tested the way the patterns have.
  - headline: The one critic on record is arguing about harmony
    severity: local
    elaboration: >
      Peter Eisenman debated him at Harvard's Graduate School of Design in
      November 1982, published as "Contrasting Concepts of Harmony in
      Architecture." Eisenman calls the relationship adversarial and asks
      why Alexander needs harmony and comfort where he does not. The
      disagreement is real and it is internal to architecture. Nobody was found
      who takes on his account of what software
      did with his work, which leaves this page's central argument without a
      named opponent.

still_load_bearing_despite: >
  The order claim comes through intact, because whoever holds the list can test
  it. Take a library of patterns, controls or templates and ask whether anything
  in it says in what order to apply them, and whether following that order
  produces something coherent or only something complete. Alexander's answer for
  buildings was that the sequence did the generating. That question reaches a
  control library, a threat-model template set or a collection of golden paths
  without anyone having to accept a word about buildings, and it is cheaper to
  ask before the list grows than after.
