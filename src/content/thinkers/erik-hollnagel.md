tagline: The safety scientist who argued that safety should be studied in the ordinary work that usually succeeds, and who gave practitioners a name for the trade between being quick and being careful.
thirty_second_take: >
  Hollnagel argues that safety management studies too little. Defining
  safety by how few adverse outcomes there are is what he calls Safety-I,
  and the white paper he co-wrote calls its method "find and fix". Failures are rare, and the adjustments that let
  everyday work succeed are the same ones that occasionally let it fail, so
  he proposes Safety-II: study how work usually goes right, and explain the
  rare failure against that background. His efficiency-thoroughness
  trade-off, ETTO, names the bargain people strike all day between getting
  work done and checking it, and he treats that bargain as normal. He
  co-founded resilience engineering with David Woods, Nancy Leveson and
  others, wrote the joint cognitive systems work with Woods, and built FRAM,
  a method for tracing how the ordinary variability of everyday work can
  resonate into an outcome. Leveson has since argued that the Safety-I he
  describes bears little resemblance to safety engineering as it is
  practised, and he has answered her by name.

background: >
  Trained in psychology in Denmark, with a master's degree from the
  University of Copenhagen and a doctorate from Aarhus. He worked as a
  programmer in Copenhagen in the 1960s, and through the 1970s and 1980s held
  posts at Aarhus, at Risø National Laboratory and at the OECD Halden Reactor
  Project. Industry posts in
  Copenhagen and the UK followed before he became professor of human-machine
  interaction at Linköping in 1999. He held the industrial safety chair at
  MINES ParisTech from 2006 to 2011, then worked as chief consultant at the
  Centre for Quality in the Region of Southern Denmark, with a professorship
  at the University of Southern Denmark. His publisher lists nuclear power,
  aviation, air traffic management, software engineering and healthcare among
  the domains he has worked in. He is now Scientific Director at the Institute
  of Resilient Systems+ in Seoul and professor emeritus in Sweden, France and
  Denmark, with over 500 publications and 28 books to his name.
why_they_matter_as_a_thinker: >
  Two software practitioners carry his terms by name. Lorin
  Hochstein applies Safety-II to software availability, and John
  Allspaw's thesis on outage response reads the heuristics engineers use under
  pressure as ETTO at work. Each gives a practitioner a question to ask of an
  ordinary day as well as a bad one: what did success depend on, and which
  check was traded away to get the work done? Work-as-imagined versus
  work-as-done also reached English-language readers through resilience
  engineering, though Hollnagel traces the distinction himself to French
  ergonomics.

thesis: >
  Hollnagel argues that safety has traditionally been defined by what goes
  wrong, and that this leaves the object of study too small. Failures are rare,
  and the same adjustments that let work succeed under changing conditions
  are what occasionally let it fail. So the useful question for safety
  management is how things usually go right, and an investigation explains
  the rare failure against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism. People cannot be as efficient
  as possible and as thorough as possible at once, the trade between the two
  is normal, and the shortcuts that make work efficient are the same ones
  that sometimes let a failure through.
working_line: >
  Safety-II is the ability to succeed under varying conditions: look at what
  goes right, and use it to understand everyday performance. ("A bird's eye
  view of resilience engineering," lecture slides, 2012.)

reading: >
  Hollnagel moves the object of safety management from the rare accident to the
  ordinary working day. Safety-I counts adverse outcomes and aims to minimise
  them, which confines its evidence to rare events. Safety-II asks how work
  usually succeeds under varying conditions, and explains the occasional failure
  against that background. ETTO supplies the mechanism: nobody can be as
  efficient and as thorough as possible at once, and trading one for the other
  is normal work. He catalogues the shortcuts that trade produces, among them
  "Looks fine" and "Has been checked by someone else". In his account, those
  shortcuts are how the work gets done and also what occasionally lets a failure
  through. The contrast is disputed at its root: Leveson says the Safety-I he
  describes bears little resemblance to safety engineering as practised, and
  Cooper groups Safety-II with other approaches into a New View whose
  propositions he calls untested.
extension: >
  Read together, his heuristics "Will be checked by someone else" and "Has been
  checked by someone else" describe a chain in which each stage relies on a
  check it assumes another made. Agent pipelines build the same chain: a tool
  call runs because an upstream step was assumed to validate the request, and
  that step passed it on because a later one was assumed to check. The result is
  a confused deputy built from two efficient shortcuts, exercising authority on
  a judgement no stage made. Safety-II's attention to work that went right says
  where to look: trace which stage checked what on requests that went through
  cleanly, and find the paths where both heuristics meet, which look like
  success until someone aims at them. Any added check would face the same trade,
  so the repair belongs in the structure: the tool call carries the originating
  requester's authority and nothing more, which requires every stage to pass the
  requester's identity along.

limits:
  - headline: A peer-reviewed critique finds no empirical base for Safety-II
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      M. D. Cooper's peer-reviewed critique finds the tenets "a collection of
      untested propositions" and says "no published, peer-reviewed empirical
      evidence" shows whether any of them hold. His target is wider than
      Hollnagel: he folds Safety-II, Safety Differently, resilience
      engineering and related approaches into one "New View". Cooper himself
      records that Hollnagel does not count Safety-II as part of the New View.
      The charge is still aimed squarely at the frame this page leads with,
      and a reader adopting Safety-II is adopting a proposal whose tenets,
      on Cooper's reading, have not been tested.
  - headline: A co-founder says his account of Safety-I misdescribes safety engineering
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      Nancy Leveson, who co-edited the field's founding anthology with him,
      argues that the Safety-I he describes "has very little or no
      resemblance to what is done today or to what has been done in safety
      engineering for at least 70 years", and proposes a systems approach she
      calls Safety-III. His reply quotes the goal she sets for Safety-III, to
      "eliminate, mitigate, or control hazards", and calls it "essentially my
      definition of Safety-I". The EUROCONTROL white paper he co-wrote had
      already proposed "moving toward Safety-II while combining the two ways
      of thinking", with most existing methods kept in use.
  - headline: ETTO may be a new name for satisficing
    severity: local
    elaboration: >
      A 2013 blog review asks whether ETTO offers new insight or "merely updated
      terminology from earlier work (most notably, Herbert Simon's
      'satisficing')". The same reviewer grants that a name for a practice
      "we've all seen and used" is "of some value in itself". Read ETTO as a
      working vocabulary whose distance from satisficing is still an open
      question.
  - headline: The variability Safety-II studies has no hostile intent behind it
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      This limit is the site's reading. Most
      of the domains his publisher lists, such as nuclear power, aviation and
      healthcare, face hazards that do not study the people managing them.
      Safety-II treats the variability of everyday work as the source of both
      success and failure. In security an attacker studies that variability
      on purpose, and a shortcut such as "has been checked by someone else"
      becomes a route to aim at. The transfer holds for learning how work
      succeeds, and it stops at the assumption that nobody hostile is steering the
      variability.

still_load_bearing_despite: >
  Whatever the verdict on Safety-II as a programme for safety management,
  the question at its center costs little to ask of any team:
  how does this work usually succeed, and what does that success depend on?
  ETTO keeps
  its value for a similar reason. An incident review needs a word for the
  shortcut that worked a hundred times before the time it failed, and his
  word lets the review describe the trade without treating the person who
  made it as the cause.
