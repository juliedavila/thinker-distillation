tagline: The author who numbered the DevOps doctrine and then built what carried it, from a novel with two co-authors to a summit he has run since 2014.
thirty_second_take: >
  Kim's doctrine arrives in numbered sets. The Three Ways, published in August
  2012 under his name alone, put flow, feedback and a learning culture in that
  order. The Five Ideals, worked out in *The Unicorn Project* and numbered in a
  2020 post, run from locality and simplicity to customer focus, with
  psychological safety fourth.
  The 2023 book with Steven Spear
  names three mechanisms, slowification, simplification and amplification, which
  it says move problem solving out of high-risk danger zones into low-risk
  winning zones. Each set hands its lineage to someone he names in his own text:
  Deming inside the First Way, Goldratt in the novel, Jon Smart and Geoffrey
  Moore inside the Five Ideals. The numbering is his, and so is the distribution: a
  novel with two co-authors that dramatizes the first set, a summit he has organized since 2014,
  a podcast, and a handbook assembled out of case studies from that summit.

background: >
  He has been studying high-performing technology organizations since 1999, by
  his own account in two separate author bios and again in the preface to the
  2023 book. The security work came first. He wrote Tripwire with Eugene
  Spafford at Purdue, published as Purdue University technical report 93-071 in
  November 1993 and at the ACM Conference on Computer and Communications
  Security the following year. Tripwire, Inc. followed in 1997, and he spent
  thirteen years there as CTO, leaving in mid-2010. The management books
  begin in 2004 with *The Visible Ops Handbook*, where Kevin Behr has the first
  byline, and run through *The Phoenix Project* (2013, with Behr and George
  Spafford), *The DevOps Handbook* (2016, with Jez Humble, Patrick Debois and
  John Willis), *Accelerate* (2018, with Nicole Forsgren and Humble), *Wiring
  the Winning Organization* (2023, with Steven Spear) and *Vibe Coding* (2025,
  with Steve Yegge). Almost all of it is co-authored, with one solo byline in
  *The Unicorn Project* (2019). With Nicole Forsgren and Jez Humble he was one
  of the founders of DORA, which Google acquired in 2018. Since 2014 he has organized the DevOps
  Enterprise Summit, now the Enterprise Technology Leadership Summit and the
  Enterprise AI Summit, and he hosts *The Idealcast*.
why_they_matter_as_a_thinker: >
  A set of principles with an order in it is portable in a way an argument is not,
  and the strongest evidence of authorship here is that his own co-authors cite him for
  it in print. Figure 1.4 in the first chapter of *The DevOps
  Handbook, Second Edition* carries the source line "Gene Kim, 'The Three Ways:
  The Principles Underpinning DevOps,' ITRevolution.com (blog), August 22,
  2012," on a book with four other names on the cover. The second thing that
  makes the work portable is the delivery. The Three Ways reached practitioners
  inside a novel, where a character states all three in sequence and credits
  Goldratt for the Theory of Constraints along the way; the handbook that
  followed draws on close to a hundred case studies presented at a summit he
  convenes; and a one-line Wikipedia gloss calls that novel a fundamental book
  that introduced the concept of DevOps. How much of that
  circulation is independent of him is the question the limits below take up.

thesis: >
  One argument runs under all of it. Technology work is a system, and its
  performance is set by how work moves through it, how quickly the news of a
  problem comes back, and whether the people inside it are free to experiment.
  The Three Ways state that as a sequence. The First Way is put as the
  performance of the entire system rather than the performance of a specific
  silo, and Deming is credited inside it for always seeking to achieve
  profound understanding of the system. The Second Way is the right-to-left
  feedback loop, shortened and amplified so corrections can be made
  continually. The Third Way is a culture that fosters experimentation and
  learning from failure while treating repetition and practice as the
  prerequisite to mastery. The later sets come at the same claim from other
  sides, and both are lists of names before they are arguments. The Five Ideals
  read as the conditions the work is meant to meet, in an order that starts at
  locality and simplicity. The three mechanisms of the 2023 book answer a
  question about where problem solving should happen, and the book's answer is
  that an organization wins by moving it out of the zones where it is
  dangerous. The lean lineage is named by both authors in their own
  prefaces (Steven Spear and H. Kent Bowen's *Harvard Business Review* article
  decoding the Toyota Production System), and Goldratt's theory of constraints
  is credited inside the novel's own text.
working_line: >
  The Third Way is about creating a culture that fosters two things: continual
  experimentation, taking risks and learning from failure; and understanding
  that repetition and practice is the prerequisite to mastery. ("The Three
  Ways: The Principles Underpinning DevOps," itrevolution.com, 2012.)

reading: >
  The Three Ways were the first of Kim's numbered sets, posted in August 2012.
  The later sets arrive as named lists, the Five Ideals from the solo book and the post naming them, and three
  mechanisms with Steven Spear the book says move problem solving out of
  high-risk zones. Each of the sets comes at one claim, that technology work is
  a system whose performance is set by flow, feedback and experiment. The
  distribution is the second contribution: a novel, a summit he has run since
  2014, and a handbook crediting the 2012 post for the frame.
extension: >
  The Third Way asks for a culture where taking risks and learning from failure
  are ordinary, and where repetition and practice are the prerequisite to
  mastery. Held against a security organization, that is a claim about which
  difficulty in the job is the right one, because mastery by repetition requires
  that the repeated work be the work itself. Where the repeated work is the
  approval and the sign-off, the mastery that accrues is in coordination. The
  First Way points at the test rather than the design: judge the whole system's
  performance, and a control that moves work between departments has to earn its
  place there. The Second Way asks for feedback shortened and amplified, and the
  loop that matters here runs from a control failing to anyone hearing of it,
  which no approval shortens. And the transfer stops at the
  blast radius, since failure can be made ordinary only where it is recoverable.

  - headline: Every numbered set hands its lineage to someone he names
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      The Three Ways credit Deming inside the First Way. The novel credits
      Goldratt for the Theory of Constraints in a character's own words. The
      Five Ideals credit Jon Smart for one phrase and Geoffrey Moore for
      another, and the fourth of the five is called Psychological Safety, an

      the lineage to Spear and Bowen's Toyota article in their own prefaces. The

      names an antecedent in his own text. What the record supports is that he
      numbered these sets, named them and carried them.
  - headline: The post that introduces the Three Ways names a collaborator who reached no byline
    severity: local
    elaboration: >
      The 2012 post is bylined to Kim alone and argues in the plural, and it
      contains this sentence: "It has been especially fun working on this with
      fellow co-author Mike Orzen, the most recent winner of the cherished
      Shingo Prize for his contribution to operational excellence for his book
      'Lean IT.'" Orzen is an author of neither book the post says the Three
      Ways are being used in. The likeliest reading is a collaboration on work
      in progress, and the post does not say that, so this page does not say it
      either. The credit stays with him because the post carries his name
      alone, and because the second edition of *The DevOps Handbook*, which he
      wrote with four co-authors, cites the post as his.
  - headline: The research half of the corpus has a published objection
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      Keunwoo Lee's 2022 review of *Accelerate* argues that the book's
      scientific claim does not hold, and declines to grant its survey
      constructs on the authors' standing: "to accept the validity of survey
      constructs based on the qualifications of the authors would be an argument
      from authority." Kim is named there as one of three co-authors rather than
      as the argument's subject, so the objection is scoped to the measurement
      work, which this page hands to Forsgren. No
      published objection to the Three Ways, the Five Ideals or the three
      mechanisms was found for this page, which is a thin result rather than
      evidence that none exists.

still_load_bearing_despite: >
  Strip all of that away and one dependency is still there: a team that cannot
  watch work move has nothing to shorten a feedback loop around, and a team
  with no feedback has nothing to learn an experiment from. That dependency
  does not rest on who numbered the Ways or who printed them. The First Way
  stands on its own terms too. Judging the performance of an entire system
  rather than the performance of one department is a demand about where to
  look, and it outlasts the set that carries it. What this page cannot settle is
  how much of the reach was his own doing. Building the channel an idea travels
  through is work either way, and it is the work a reader is least likely to
  see, because afterwards the numbered set looks like the whole of it.
