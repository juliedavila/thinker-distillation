tagline: >
  The psychologist who set out two terms, active failures for the unsafe
  acts of people at the front line and latent conditions for the flaws that
  earlier decisions leave in a system, and who drew, with John Wreathall,
  the layered-defences picture now called the Swiss cheese model.
thirty_second_take: >
  After early work on motion sickness, Reason studied human error, first as
  an experimental psychologist working on slips and lapses, then as the author of an
  organisational account of accidents. Its core is a distinction between
  active failures, the unsafe acts of people in direct contact with a system,
  and latent conditions, the "resident pathogens" that earlier decisions by
  designers and managers leave in place. Defences in that account are slices
  with holes, and an accident needs the holes in many layers to line up. The
  picture, drawn with the engineer John Wreathall and labelled "Swiss cheese"
  by someone else, was adopted by ICAO's human factors group in the early
  1990s, by the account of a report he co-wrote, and spread into patient
  safety. In 1998 he argued that an organisation learns about its
  hazards only if people report them, and that reporting rests on a just
  culture.

background: >
  Born James Tootle in 1938, he took the surname of the maternal grandfather
  who raised him. He read psychology at the University of Manchester,
  graduating in 1962, and took a PhD on motion sickness at the University of
  Leicester in 1967, working with the RAF Institute of Aviation Medicine and
  spending time at the US Naval Aerospace Medical Institute in Pensacola. In
  *Motion Sickness* (1975) he and John Brand suggested that the condition comes
  from a conflict between the senses and stored patterns of motion. He began lecturing at Leicester, and in
  1977 returned to Manchester as Professor and Head of Psychology, where he
  stayed until he retired in 2001. His later research turned to everyday
  errors, to driver behaviour (he devised most of the items of the Driver
  Behaviour Questionnaire) and to organisational accidents in aviation,
  nuclear power and healthcare. He sat on the committee behind *An
  Organisation with a Memory* (2000), the Chief Medical Officer's report for
  the Department of Health. He was elected a Fellow of the
  British Academy in 1999 and appointed CBE in the 2003 New Year Honours for
  services to reducing the risk in healthcare. He died in February 2025.

thesis: >
  On Reason's account, accidents in complex systems come from unsafe acts at
  the front line triggering latent conditions that earlier decisions by
  designers and managers put in place (an observation he credited to Barry
  Turner and Charles Perrow), and defences fail when their weaknesses
  coincide. On that account, error management that looks only at the person who
  erred misses where the conditions came from, and in his 1997 words
  "identifying and eliminating latent conditions proactively still offer the
  best routes to improving system fitness." The argument extends to culture.
  A safe culture, in his 1998 argument, is an informed one, which depends on
  people reporting, which rests on a just culture in which the line between
  acceptable and unacceptable behaviour is clearly drawn; he rejected the blanket "no
  blame" alternative as neither feasible nor desirable.

reading: >
  Reason separates active failures, the unsafe acts at a system's front line,
  from latent conditions, the "resident pathogens" left in place by earlier
  decisions. His co-authored history calls the observation behind the pair
  "nothing new", crediting Barry Turner and Charles Perrow. He and John
  Wreathall drew the account as five planes, which later became slices of Swiss
  cheese, a label someone else supplied; an accident needs their holes to align.
  In 1997 latent failures became latent conditions, because "a condition is not
  a cause, but it is necessary for a causal factor to have an impact." He argued
  that an organisation learns of its hazards through its people's reports, and
  that reporting rests on a just culture that separates acceptable from
  unacceptable behaviour. He questioned the model's use, writing in 1997 that
  the search for distant contributions may have gone too far and asking in a
  2004 talk whether it was past its sell-by date.
extension: >
  Defence in depth around an AI agent is a stack of partial controls, the
  arrangement Reason drew as slices. His model was built to explain accidents,
  which nobody intends. An injected instruction is written to find the
  alignment, inside the text every filtering layer reads. So those layers can
  fail together, and counting them overstates the protection. The steadier lever
  is on the latent side: what the agent could reach before any text arrived. A
  standing permission, the authority a confused deputy needs, works like his
  resident pathogen, and the attacker supplies the trigger his account leaves to
  front-line acts. Removing it narrows what any injection can do, whichever
  layer it slipped past. That fix carries a hazard close to the one his critics
  named: once a review finds the permission, it has a latent condition to blame,
  and the crafted text can drop out of the account.

limits:
  - headline: Reason raised the model's limits himself
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      In the 1997 book he wrote that "the pendulum may have swung too far" in
      tracking down contributions "widely separated in both time and place
      from the events themselves," and at a 2004 workshop at Brétigny he
      titled a talk "Üeberlingen: Is Swiss cheese past its sell-by date?" The
      2006 report he co-wrote gives his definition of a root cause as "the
      contributing factor that you are working on when the money or the time
      runs out."
  - headline: Used as a procedure, its critics argue, it moves blame upstream
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      The cost falls on any team that adopts the model as an investigation
      procedure. A review required to end at a latent condition can reach one
      in hindsight even where an active error carried the event, and that
      error then drops out of the account. The 2006 report Reason co-wrote
      lists their point as a shift "from a 'blame the pilot' to 'blame the
      management' culture."
  - headline: Two of its adopters say the holes were never defined
    severity: local
    elaboration: >
      Shappell and Wiegmann, who built the HFACS classification on the model,
      wrote that "the theory never defines what the 'holes in the cheese'
      really are," as the 2006 report quotes them. A team using the picture
      still has to decide for itself what counts as a hole.
  - headline: The model was built for accidents; on this site's reading, an attacker lines the holes up on purpose
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      The BMJ paper has accidents happen when the holes in many layers
      "momentarily line up to permit a trajectory of accident opportunity."
      On the site's reading, a
      defence-in-depth argument borrowed from it needs adjusting for an adversary, who searches for the
      alignment and can work on several layers at once.

still_load_bearing_despite: >
  None of the objections reaches the split itself. An unsafe act by
  the person at the front line and a condition someone built into the system
  earlier are different kinds of thing, they call for different fixes, and
  an investigation that names only the first has stopped early. His critics
  on this page keep the model. Young, Shorrock and their co-authors dispute
  how far upstream to take it, a worry he had voiced in 1997, and find his
  own case studies open to hindsight bias; their own summary aims both points at where and how the
  model is applied. Just culture holds for a related
  reason: the line between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour is a
  question every reporting system has to answer, and he gave it a form that
  can be argued about.
