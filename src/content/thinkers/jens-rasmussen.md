tagline: >
  The Danish engineer who modelled work migrating, under pressure for less
  effort and lower cost, toward the boundary of acceptable performance, and
  who listed the usually unstated rules that typically end an accident investigation's search
  for causes.
thirty_second_take: >
  Rasmussen worked at Risø, the Danish atomic energy research establishment,
  from 1956, on the safety of hazardous process plants. By his own account
  the research moved from control and safety systems to operator error, then
  to the people who set operators' working conditions, and on to management
  and law. His 1997 paper gathers that path into one model. Work moves inside
  a space bounded by economic failure, unacceptable workload and functionally
  acceptable performance, and an "effort gradient" felt by the people doing
  it, together with a "cost gradient" supplied by management, will "very
  likely" produce "a systematic migration toward the boundary" of acceptable
  performance. Defences in depth "are likely to degenerate systematically
  through time" under the same pressure. He aimed improvement at the boundary: make it visible, and work
  on "the control of performance in interaction with the boundary, not on
  control of errors." In 1990 he described the pragmatic stop rules that
  typically end an investigation's search for causes, which change with what the
  investigation is for. His skill, rule and knowledge levels of behaviour
  (1983) are the base that Reason's GEMS extends, by a secondary account.

background: >
  Born in Ribe, Denmark, on 11 May 1926, he took an engineering master's
  degree with honours at the Technical University of Denmark in 1950 and
  joined the Atomic Energy Commission's Research Establishment Risø in 1956,
  where he headed the Electronics Department from 1962 to 1987. In a
  historian's account, the early work was on reactor instrumentation and its
  reliability, using verbal protocol studies and accident reports. He was elected to the Danish Academy of
  Technical Sciences in 1962, received the Human Factors Society's
  Distinguished Foreign Colleague Award in 1987, and was elected to the US
  National Academy of Engineering in 2013 for "contributions to the science
  and engineering of human error and reliability". He was married to Annelise Mark Pejtersen,
  a research professor emeritus at Risø and his co-author on *Cognitive Systems
  Engineering* (1994). He died in Copenhagen on 5 February 2018.
why_they_matter_as_a_thinker: >
  The migration model travels in software and safety writing, and some of
  the writers who carry it name it in their own words. Lorin Hochstein uses
  it in three posts, calls it the "dynamic safety model", and says it lacks
  an evocative name; Cook and Rasmussen built going solid on it in 2005; Dekker and Pruchnicki cite the 1997
  paper for their account of drifting into failure; and Kelly Shortridge
  cites it in a footnote to her essay on security obstructionism. Harwood
  and Sanderson wrote in 1986 that the skill, rule and knowledge
  classification "has had a widespread impact". Nancy Leveson's STAMP and
  her specification work extend him by name.

thesis: >
  Rasmussen read the court reports from Bhopal, Flixborough, Zeebrügge and
  Chernobyl as showing that those accidents "have not been caused by a
  coincidence of independent failures and human errors, but by a systematic
  migration of organisational behaviour toward accident under the influence
  of pressure toward cost-effectiveness". Task analysis built on action
  sequences and occasional errors should therefore give way, he argued, to a
  model of "work system constraints, boundaries of acceptable performance,
  and subjective criteria guiding adaptation to change", with risk
  management treated as a control problem across the disciplines that shape
  the work. Improvement is aimed at the boundary: identify it, make it
  visible to the people who work near it, and give them the chance to learn
  to cope with it, on his view that "operation close to known boundaries may
  be safer than requiring excessive margins". His 1990 paper on causality
  gives the investigative side: what an analysis accepts as a cause depends
  on stop rules set by its aim, and errors as "a separate class of
  behaviour" grow harder to pick out in modern work.
working_line: >
  Effort to improve safety should go into how performance is controlled where
  it meets the boundary, and the control of errors is the wrong target.
  (Paraphrased from "Risk management in a dynamic society", 1997.)

named_frames:
  - concept_slug: migration-toward-the-boundary
    display_name: Migration toward the boundary of acceptable performance
    one_line_gloss: >
      Work varies locally like the "Brownian movements" of gas molecules,
      inside a space bounded by economic failure, unacceptable workload and
      functionally acceptable performance. The people doing it find "an
      effort gradient", management supplies "a cost gradient", and "the result
      will very likely be a systematic migration toward the boundary of
      functionally acceptable performance", where, if the crossing is irreversible, an
      error or an accident may occur. The 1997 paper is the canonical statement and
      cites his own 1993 and 1994 work for the claim; Rankin and colleagues
      date the idea to his 1986 book. Hochstein calls it the dynamic safety
      model, and later writers describe it as drift; the paper's word is
      migration.
    attribution_kind: original
    primary_author: jens-rasmussen
    source_link: https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/158016663/SAFESCI.PDF

  - concept_slug: stop-rules
    display_name: Stop rules in accident analysis
    one_line_gloss: >
      The search for an accident's causes typically ends by rules that "are not usually
      formulated explicitly": when the causal path can no longer be followed
      for missing information, when "a familiar, abnormal event" offers a
      reasonable explanation, or when "a cure is available." The rules
      "depend on the aim of the analysis", whether that is to explain the
      events, "to allocate responsibility and blame, or to identify possible
      system improvements." He illustrates the point with Kuhn's account of
      Dalton's atomic theory.

reading: >
  Rasmussen gives one account of how work moves before an accident and another
  of how the analysis stops after one. Workers follow an effort gradient,
  management supplies a cost gradient, and together they very likely produce a
  systematic migration toward the boundary of acceptable performance. Under that
  pressure defences in depth are likely to degenerate over time, and in his
  account the accidents he studied were caused by the migration, with a
  coincidence of independent failures and human errors ruled out. He aims
  improvement at the boundary and away from errors: make it visible to the
  people who work near it, and control performance where work meets it. The
  analysis afterwards typically ends by stop rules that are rarely stated: the
  causal trail runs out, a familiar abnormal event explains enough, or a cure is
  available. Which rule ends it depends on whether the analysis means to explain
  the events, to allocate responsibility and blame or to improve the system.
extension: >
  A security review stops by the same rarely stated rules, and his third is
  the risky one: once a cure such as an unapplied patch appears, the review can
  end, whether or not the patch closed the only way in. An
  attacker can trip the second rule on purpose by planting a familiar
  explanation, such as a known exploit. Writing down, before the review starts,
  its aim, its stop rule and the evidence that would rule out a familiar
  explanation makes the rules checkable. After a breach, advice on preserving
  privilege can put the forensic work under counsel, aimed at "litigation
  preparation" and kept "need-to-know", so a review meant to improve the system
  can lose the analyses it needs and stop at the first rule. Writing the rules
  down costs time his cost gradient will press the team to give back.

limits:
  - headline: On this site's reading, the model has no adversary in it
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      The cases behind the 1997 paper are Bhopal, Flixborough, Zeebrügge and
      Chernobyl, and its remedy rests on boundaries that can be identified
      and made "visible to the actors". On the site's reading, an attacker
      moves that boundary and hides where it is, and a security crossing can
      stay silent long after it happens.
  - headline: Dekker and Pruchnicki explain margin erosion by a mechanism he doubted
    severity: local
    elaboration: >
      Their 2013 paper calls widening the margin between normal operation and
      the safety boundary "another strategy proposed by Rasmussen", and finds
      it "only partially effective due to risk homeostasis", because "normal
      operations will likely follow" a boundary moved further away. The 1997
      paper lists that approach and already expects "the natural adaptation to
      the boundary" to "compensate", and its most promising approach "appears
      to be" identifying boundaries and making them visible. The two differ on
      the cause. He wrote that risk homeostasis "can be an artefact" of
      modelling behaviour from accident and error analysis, and held that
      performance stays near the boundary through perceived "dynamic control
      characteristics", where Dekker and Pruchnicki describe a system
      gravitating back "to a certain level of risk acceptance".
  - headline: Leveson finds his influence concentrated at the interface
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      In her account of his legacy, "his ideas have had the most impact on
      human factors and cognitive systems engineering and not on the
      engineering of the hardware and software beyond the interface design."
      The software uses named on this page are blog posts by Hochstein and a
      footnote in a Shortridge essay.
  - headline: Hochstein reads the model as having a single safety boundary
    severity: local
    elaboration: >
      In Rasmussen's model "there is only one boundary in the work space
      related to accidents: the safety boundary", while in Woods's account
      "the competence envelope is defined by multiple boundaries". Hochstein
      presents Woods as extending the model; a team using Rasmussen alone
      watches one accident boundary where its work may have several.

still_load_bearing_despite: >
  The mechanism does not depend on the points the limits raise. Whatever a
  team makes of known boundaries, and whether perceived control or accepted
  risk keeps performance near the boundary, the account of how work moves
  still describes something the team can check: local
  adaptation to an effort gradient and a cost gradient is likely to push
  performance one way, and layered defences tend to wear down under the same pressure. The stop
  rules hold for a similar reason. Every investigation stops somewhere, and
  asking whether one of his three rules ended it, and what the analysis was for,
  is a test a review team can run on its own report.
