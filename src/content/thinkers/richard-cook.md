tagline: The anesthesiologist and former software engineer who condensed a research program on accidents and safety into eighteen short propositions about how complex systems fail.
thirty_second_take: >
  Cook's short treatise How Complex Systems Fail (1998) sets out, in eighteen
  numbered propositions, what the research program he shared with David
  Woods had found. In his account complex systems are hazardous by nature,
  heavily defended and always carrying flaws, so a catastrophe needs several
  failures together.
  Proposition 7 draws the consequence: a root
  cause named after an accident reflects a social need to blame, and does not
  explain how the failure worked. A 1998 report he wrote with Woods and
  Charlotte Miller, after a National Patient Safety Foundation workshop, sets
  the first story of an accident against the second. Going solid, written with Jens Rasmussen in 2005 and built on
  Rasmussen's dynamic model of risk, describes how efficiency gains couple distant parts
  of a hospital. He gave the propositions as a keynote at O'Reilly's web
  operations conference in 2012, wrote a chapter on SRE cognitive work with
  John Allspaw in 2018, and in 2020 wrote a standalone statement of the line of
  representation that he had presented with Woods three years earlier.

background: >
  Studied physics and urban planning at Lawrence University, graduating in
  1975, and then worked as a software engineer at Control Data before medical
  school. He took his MD at the University of Cincinnati in 1986, started
  human-performance research in Ohio State's Department of Anesthesiology in
  1987, began working with David Woods's team at the university in 1988, and
  completed his anesthesiology residency in 1994. He was on the University of Chicago
  faculty in anesthesia and critical care from 1994 to 2012, as assistant
  professor until 2002, and founded the Cognitive Technologies Laboratory
  there in 1996, the year he helped start the National Patient Safety
  Foundation as a founding board member. In 2012 he became Professor of
  Healthcare Systems Safety at KTH in Stockholm, emeritus from 2015. In 2017
  he co-founded Adaptive Capacity Labs with John Allspaw and David Woods. He
  died on 31 August 2022.
why_they_matter_as_a_thinker: >
  His propositions have been quoted and reprinted in software operations
  word for word. A 2012 Microsoft post applied
  "complex systems run as broken systems" and the rejection of a single root
  cause to that year's AWS and Azure outages, and John Allspaw, who wrote in
  2009 that he could not overstate how right the paper was for web
  operations, reprinted it the following year in an operations book he
  edited with Jesse Robbins. The work also reaches software from its author's
  side: he wrote software at Control Data before medical school, and his 2020
  article addresses the people who run internet services in their own terms.

thesis: >
  On Cook's account (a synthesis of the program he shared with Woods, which
  borrows Reason's language of latent failures), failure in a complex system
  belongs to the system. Such a system is heavily defended and always runs
  with flaws present, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together, and the
  people working in it create its safety continuously. An
  account that names one root cause, or one person's error, is then a social
  judgment made with the outcome known, and it does not explain how the
  failure worked. The explaining is done by the second story, the one about
  the systemic vulnerabilities the people involved were working inside (the
  pair set out in his 1998 report with Woods and Miller). His later work takes up
  efficiency gains that couple distant parts of a system, and the line of
  representation he presented with Woods, across which operators see the
  system only through what represents it.
working_line: >
  Complex systems run as broken systems. They keep working because they hold
  so many redundancies and because people make them work, flaws and all.
  (Paraphrased from proposition 5 of How Complex Systems Fail, 1998.)

reading: >
  The eighteen propositions of How Complex Systems Fail run from the nature of
  hazard, through how blame gets assigned, to safety as something people create.
  In Cook's account a complex system is heavily defended and always carries
  flaws, so a catastrophe takes several flaws together. It follows that naming a
  single root cause after an accident answers a social need to blame and leaves
  the mechanism of the failure unexplained. The 1998 report he wrote with Woods
  and Miller gives the alternative: the first story is one of human error, the
  second one of the systemic vulnerabilities behind it. Going solid, written
  with Rasmussen in 2005, describes efficiency gains stripping the buffers
  between distant parts of a hospital until they are tightly coupled. Cook's
  2020 statement of the line of representation, a frame set out in the 2017
  STELLA report, is written for software operators, who reach the system only
  through representations.
extension: >
  Nobody running a system sees it directly, a condition that predates generated
  code. In Cook's 2020 account, operators hold a model built through
  representations and kept current by continual refreshing. On that reading, the
  security question this frame adds for generated code is how stale the
  organization's model of what it runs has become, which reviewing each change
  does not measure. An attacker acting below the line aims to change the system
  without changing anything its operators see, and refreshing prompted by those
  representations may never pick that change up. The practical move is to
  refresh the model on a schedule of its own, from a representation the attacker
  does not control, such as an out-of-band inventory checked against what
  operators believe is running. The frame sets the ceiling, because the refresh
  is itself a representation, worth something only if it could show a change the
  usual representations hide. A review log shows no such change.

limits:
  - headline: The propositions point to almost no evidence outside one research program
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      The text opens on its first proposition with no credited premise, and
      its reading list is the program's own work. Apart from one footnote
      citing anthropological and sociological work on how cause is socially
      constructed, the evidence the propositions point to sits in that one
      research program. A reader citing a proposition is citing a condensed
      finding of that program.
  - headline: Proposition 7 was written about accidents, and an attacker is a chosen cause
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      This limit is the site's reading. Cook grounds root-cause attribution in
      "the social, cultural need to blame specific, localized forces or
      events." In a security incident an adversary is a localized force with
      intent who selected the weak point, and naming that adversary is an
      operational need. Security programs also trace defects to a shared class,
      such as a missing control, in order to remove the class. The proposition
      argues against blaming a single cause for an accident, and it does not
      say how to treat an agent who went looking for the flaws the system was
      already running with. Naming the attacker can end a review the way a
      first story does, and the proposition leaves open whether the flaws that
      attacker used still get a second story.
  - headline: Going solid was built on hospital cases
    severity: local
    elaboration: >
      The 2005 paper with Rasmussen builds its model on hospital cases, which
      Fred Hebert's reading notes describe as hospital and intensive care
      examples. Carrying it to software, where on the site's reading a shared service or a consolidated
      pipeline couples teams that used to be buffered from each other, is an
      inference a reader makes and the paper does not test.

still_load_bearing_despite: >
  An incident review can put the propositions to itself as questions. Were several flaws involved, or one? Was the
  system already running degraded before the day it failed? Is the cause on
  the report there because it explains the failure, or because someone needed
  a place to put the blame? A review that asks those questions has used Cook
  whether it cites him or not.
