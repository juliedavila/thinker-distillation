tagline: Catalogued the shapes the staff engineer job takes, and wrote an unsentimental account of where their authority comes from and why written policy erodes.
thirty_second_take: >
  Larson writes about the parts of an engineering organization that are held
  together by documents: career ladders, policies and strategies. His
  best-known work is a taxonomy of the staff engineer role. After interviewing
  engineers who already held the title, he sorted the job into four shapes,
  Tech Lead, Architect, Solver and Right Hand. His site StaffEng gathers Staff,
  Principal and Distinguished under one label, Staff-plus. The rest of the
  corpus is about how written machinery
  decays and what maintains it. A policy that spends its time granting
  exceptions carries exception debt, and the repair is to batch a month of
  requests and rewrite the policy around them. A strategy most companies
  already have and never wrote down is, on his account, half solved once
  somebody writes it down. And the authority to do any of this is bestowed by a sponsor and
  can be taken back, which is the part of his writing that generalizes furthest
  past engineering.

background: >
  Grew up in North Carolina, studied computer science at Centre College in
  Kentucky, spent a year in Japan on the JET Program, and co-founded a since
  defunct iOS gaming startup. His own career account runs Yahoo, then Digg as an
  engineer through to director, then SocialCode after the Digg acquihire, where
  he rose to VP of platform technology. He left in 2014 for Uber, hired into a
  five-person infrastructure operations team, where the SRE function was built
  under him; his post about it is titled "Founding Uber SRE" and records hiring
  Uber's first SRE and its first SRE manager. He spent about four years at
  Stripe supporting infrastructure engineering, joining a roughly thirty-person
  group called Sys that took the Foundation name a year later in a reorganization
  and reached about 250 people by the time he left. He was CTO at Calm, then at
  Carta, and has been CTO at Imprint since 2025. He has written at lethain.com
  under the blog name *Irrational Exuberance* throughout, and also publishes
  staffeng.com and craftingengstrategy.com. Month-level dates are not available
  from any first-party source, so this page keeps to years.
why_they_matter_as_a_thinker: >
  The archetypes gave a career track its working vocabulary. LeadDev built a
  career-development piece around the four of them in 2024 and credited him in
  the opening sentence, and a 2026 argument against them began by conceding
  the essay's standing in the field. The label over the titles circulates
  without his name:
  Gergely Orosz defined Staff+ for his readers in 2023 as ordinary industry
  vocabulary, with no mention of Larson at all. The rest of the corpus is
  portable for a different reason. Exception debt, the four team states and the
  loan account of authority are each a mechanism a reader can check against
  their own organization in an afternoon, which is rarer in leadership writing
  than the volume of leadership writing suggests.

thesis: >
  Larson's subject is the written machinery of an engineering organization, and
  the recurring move across the corpus is to name a way one of those pieces
  decays and then prescribe the repair. A policy that spends significant time granting exceptions has
  exception debt, and working the exceptions one at a time is what sustains it;
  the repair is to batch a month of requests and merge the escalations into the
  next revision. An engineering strategy usually already exists and has simply
  never been written down, so writing it down solves half the problem (the structure he
  writes into is Richard Rumelt's diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent
  action, which he credits by name). A team sits in one of four states and the
  state decides what it needs, in what he calls a loose framework. The staff
  engineer archetypes came the other
  way around, out of interviews with people already in the roles rather than
  from a mechanism. Holding all of it up is his account of authority: the power
  that comes with a title is loaned by a sponsor, can be retracted, and lasts
  only while its holder stays aligned with whoever bestowed it (the
  conferral-and-revocation structure is French and Raven's legitimate power,
  1959; the loan framing and the maintenance condition it places on the holder
  are his).

reading: >
  The organization Larson writes about is the one on paper: the ladder, the
  policy, the strategy. The essays work one decay at a time: name
  how a written artifact fails, then prescribe the repair. A policy that spends
  its time granting exceptions carries exception debt, and the repair is to
  batch a month of requests and rewrite the policy around what the escalations
  show. Most companies already have an engineering strategy and never wrote it
  down, so by his account the writing solves half the problem, inside
  Rumelt's kernel of diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action. The staff
  engineer archetypes came the other way, out of interviews with people who
  already held the title, which is what the taxonomy describes and what bounds
  it. Underneath sits his account of authority: the power a title carries is
  bestowed by a sponsor, can be retracted, and holds only while its holder stays
  aligned with whoever bestowed it.
extension: >
  Larson's account covers staff engineers and their managers, and no security
  function appears in the record behind this page. A security mandate arrives
  partly the same way. Where New York's Part 500 applies, the rule requires a
  designated CISO and an annual signature, duties no sponsor conferred and none
  can waive, with no discretion attached. Everything above
  that floor is on loan, and that margin is the autonomy an executive weighs
  before taking a job. The exception queue is where the loan gets spent, since
  each granted exception suspends a control over somebody else's work. His
  repair does not transfer whole: a policy rewritten around the exceptions it
  granted ratifies whatever pressure produced them, and his setting holds no
  adversary who benefits from the rewrite. The diagnostic half carries over:
  read the queue per control and per sponsor. Read as a score it teaches people
  to stop filing, and it never sees the control nobody bothers to except
  against.

limits:
  - headline: The archetypes are an artifact of who was interviewed
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      Larson built the taxonomy by talking to engineers who already held the
      title, and he describes the four clusters as emerging from those
      conversations. Sean Goedecke's 2026
      argument reaches the same warning by a different route: the archetypes do not work as goals,
      because "You don't become a technical leader by trying really hard to be a
      technical leader," and "Important projects are usually assigned top-down."
      He grants that Larson wrote description and that readers turned it into
      prescription. A reader using the four names to plan a promotion is using
      them for something the sampling method cannot support.
  - headline: The escape from falling behind is hiring, and not every function has one
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      The four team states and the sizing heuristics were both published in
      2018, out of Uber and Stripe, high-growth and well funded companies. A team that is
      falling behind escapes by adding people, and the sizing ratios describe an
      organization that can staff to them. In a function with flat headcount, a
      contractor-heavy roster, or a regulated coverage requirement, that escape
      is missing and the ratios become a description of somebody else's company.
      No published critic makes this point; the site makes it in its own voice.
  - headline: Most of the frames have no recorded life outside his writing
    severity: structural
    elaboration: >
      The archetypes travel, and Staff-plus travels further. Exception debt, the
      loan account of authority, the four team states, the sizing heuristics and
      strategy testing do not, as far as a search can show. Both "exception
      debt" and the loan account turn up in book summaries, reading notes and
      quote collections, which is circulation rather than
      practitioners reaching for them. That does not make the mechanisms wrong,
      and it does mean a reader adopting them is running an experiment rather
      than joining a practice.
  - headline: The strategy material is Rumelt applied
    severity: local
    elaboration: >
      Diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action is Richard Rumelt's kernel,
      and Larson says so himself. The systems modelling points readers at
      Donella Meadows, and the Wardley map chapters credit Simon Wardley and
      David Anderson. The engineering examples are real work and the underlying
      theory is borrowed, so a reader who wants the theory should go to the
      originals and treat the books as the application layer.

still_load_bearing_despite: >
  Exception debt survives every limit above, because it is checkable from
  records an organization already keeps. Count the exceptions granted against a
  policy over a quarter, and the count answers whether the policy still
  describes how the work is done, without an audit or an interview. The loan
  account of authority holds for a similar reason: it predicts something, which
  is that a mandate thins when its sponsor's attention moves, and anyone who has
  watched a reorganization can test that against what they saw. The archetypes
  hold as description, which is what he said they were.
